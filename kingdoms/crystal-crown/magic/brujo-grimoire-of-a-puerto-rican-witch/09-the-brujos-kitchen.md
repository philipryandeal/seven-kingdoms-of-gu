# The Brujo's Kitchen

A brujo who buys all his supplies pre-made from the botánica is a brujo who has handed his power to someone else's hands.

I am not talking about the candles — buy your candles, that is fine. I am not talking about the Florida water — Lanman and Kemp have been making Florida water since 1808 and they do it better than you will. I am talking about the preparations that carry your intention, your prayer, your hands, and your relationship with the plants into the working. Your spiritual baths. Your floor washes. Your oils. Your sachets. Your tonics. The things that touch your body and your altar and your home. Those should come from your kitchen, made by your hands, timed to the sky, and prayed over by your mouth.

There is a reason every curandero in the history chapter of this book made his own remedies. Don Pedrito Jaramillo did not order his prescriptions from a catalog. The h-men of Yucatán did not buy pre-packaged limpias. The Olosanyin does not outsource his herb gathering. The preparation *is* part of the working. The act of cutting, crushing, steeping, straining, and bottling is not the boring part before the magic starts. It *is* the magic. Your hands are on the plant. Your breath is in the room. Your prayer is in the water. By the time the preparation is finished, it is already charged — not because you waved a crystal over it, but because you made it with intention from the first moment to the last.

This chapter teaches you how to make every preparation a brujo needs. I am going to keep the instructions practical and clear because these are skills, not performances. You do not need a cauldron and a pointy hat. You need a kitchen, a stove, a few glass jars, and the willingness to learn. Everything in this chapter can be made with materials available at a grocery store, a health food store, or an online herb supplier. Nothing here is expensive. Nothing here is complicated. The only thing it requires is that you do it.

Let me walk you through the brujo's kitchen, preparation by preparation.

## Tinctures

A tincture is the workhorse of herbal magic. It is a concentrated liquid extract made by soaking herbs in alcohol for several weeks, pulling the active compounds out of the plant and into the liquid. The result is a potent, shelf-stable preparation that you can use by the dropperful in workings, baths, floor washes, candle dressings, and — when the herbs are safe for ingestion — taken internally for health.

I keep tinctures of my most-used herbs on hand at all times. A tincture of rue for protection. A tincture of basil for money work. A tincture of mugwort for dream work and psychic development. A tincture of lavender for calming and mental clarity. These are my staples. You will develop your own based on the workings you do most often.

**How to make a tincture:**

Choose your herb. Fresh or dried — both work. Fresh herbs should be chopped or bruised to increase surface area. Dried herbs can be lightly crushed.

Choose your solvent. Vodka at 80 proof (40% alcohol) is the standard and works well for most herbs. If you need a stronger extraction — for roots, barks, or resins — use a higher proof like Everclear or grain alcohol at 150–190 proof. For an alcohol-free option, use apple cider vinegar or vegetable glycerin, but know that these will not extract as broadly as alcohol and the result will be less potent.

Combine. Place the prepared herb in a clean glass jar — a Mason jar works perfectly. Pour the alcohol over the herb until the plant material is completely submerged with about an inch of liquid above it. For dried herbs, the standard ratio is one part herb by weight to five parts alcohol by volume. For fresh herbs, use one part herb to two parts alcohol because the fresh plant already contains water.

Steep. Seal the jar tightly. Store it in a cool, dark place — a kitchen cabinet is fine. Shake it once a day. The steeping period is four to six weeks. Do not rush this. The alcohol needs time to pull the full range of compounds from the plant.

Strain. After steeping, strain through cheesecloth or a fine mesh strainer into a clean glass bottle. Squeeze the cheesecloth to get every drop out. For an even cleaner result, strain a second time through a coffee filter.

Bottle and label. Transfer to dark glass dropper bottles. Label each one with the herb name, the solvent you used, the ratio, and the date. This matters. When you have six tinctures on a shelf and they all look like brown liquid, you will be grateful you labeled them.

Store properly. In a cool, dark place, an alcohol-based tincture will last for years. Vinegar and glycerin tinctures have a shorter shelf life — about one to two years.

**Magical use:** A few drops of the appropriate tincture can be added to candle dressings, bath water, floor washes, mojo bags, or petition papers. A tincture is concentrated enough that a small amount carries significant herbal power. When I dress a candle for protection, I anoint it with rue tincture and olive oil — the rue carries the Martial protective energy and the alcohol evaporates, leaving the herbal essence bonded to the wax.

## Infusions

An infusion is an herbal tea made with intention. That is not a cute reframing. That is what it is. When you steep chamomile in hot water and drink it to calm your nerves, you are making an infusion. When you steep chamomile in hot water, pray over it, and pour it into your spiritual bath, you are making a magical infusion. The technique is identical. The intention is what changes the category.

Infusions are best for the delicate parts of plants — leaves, flowers, and soft stems. These parts release their compounds readily in hot water without needing the prolonged extraction of a tincture.

**How to make an infusion:**

Select your herb. One to two teaspoons of dried herb per cup of water, or two to three tablespoons of fresh herb per cup. Adjust to taste and desired strength.

Heat your water. Bring water to just below a full boil. Water that is too hot can destroy delicate volatile compounds in some herbs. Water that is not hot enough will not extract fully.

Steep. Pour the hot water over the herbs in a teapot, French press, or Mason jar. Cover it — this is important. Covering the vessel traps the steam, which carries volatile compounds that would otherwise escape into the air. Those volatiles are part of the medicine and part of the magic.

Time. Fifteen to twenty minutes for leaves and flowers. Up to thirty minutes for a stronger therapeutic infusion. Some practitioners steep overnight for maximum potency — this is especially effective with nourishing herbs like nettle or oatstraw.

Strain and use. Strain out the plant material. The resulting liquid is your infusion. Use it immediately or refrigerate — infusions do not keep long. Use within twenty-four to forty-eight hours.

**Cold infusions:** Some herbs benefit from cold water steeping — particularly delicate flowers and herbs where you want to preserve heat-sensitive compounds. Place the herbs in cold water, cover, and refrigerate overnight. Strain in the morning.

**Magical use:** Infusions are the base for spiritual baths, floor washes, and room sprays. An infusion of hyssop for spiritual cleansing. An infusion of basil and cinnamon for money drawing. An infusion of rosemary and rue added to your mop water for protection on the floor and threshold of your home. Every floor wash you have ever heard of in the Hoodoo and rootwork traditions is, at its core, an herbal infusion applied to the physical space with intention.

## Decoctions

A decoction is what you make when the plant material is too tough for a simple infusion. Roots, barks, seeds, dried berries, woody stems — these do not surrender their compounds to hot water sitting still. You need to simmer them. The sustained heat breaks down the tough cell walls and pulls the medicine out.

Every curandero in the borderlands knows how to make a decoction. It is how you extract the power from cinnamon bark, ginger root, dandelion root, and the hard woody materials that carry some of the most potent herbal magic available. If the infusion is a conversation, the decoction is an interrogation — you are applying sustained pressure until the plant gives up what it is holding.

**How to make a decoction:**

Prepare the herb. Chop or crush roots and barks into small pieces. The more surface area you expose, the better the extraction.

Combine with water. Use about one tablespoon of dried herb or two tablespoons of fresh per cup of water. Place the herb and cold water together in a saucepan. Starting with cold water and bringing it up to temperature together extracts more efficiently than dropping herbs into already-boiling water.

Simmer. Bring to a boil, then reduce heat and simmer gently. Twenty minutes minimum. Up to an hour for very hard materials. Do not boil vigorously — a gentle simmer is what you want. You will lose some water to evaporation. That is fine. It concentrates the decoction.

Strain. Remove from heat, let cool slightly, and strain through cheesecloth or a fine mesh strainer. Press or squeeze the plant material to extract every drop.

**Combining with infusions:** If you want to work with both tough roots and delicate flowers in the same preparation, make the decoction first with the hard materials. When you take it off the heat, add the delicate herbs and let them steep in the hot decoction for ten to fifteen minutes. Then strain everything together. This gives you the full range of both without destroying the delicate compounds.

**Magical use:** Decoctions of cinnamon bark and ginger root make powerful bases for money-drawing baths. A decoction of valerian root can be added to workings for deep rest and ancestral contact during sleep. A decoction of Solomon's seal root — traditionally associated with wisdom and binding — adds Saturnian depth to workings that need staying power.

## Tonics

A tonic is a preparation you take regularly over time to build and maintain a specific capacity in your body and your spirit. Tonics are not emergency medicine. They are not for acute situations. A tonic is the long game — the daily or weekly practice of nourishing a specific system so that when the acute situation arrives, you are already strong enough to handle it.

For a brujo, tonics serve both physical and spiritual functions. A nervine tonic — chamomile, lemon balm, passionflower — taken daily over weeks will calm your nervous system *and* make your mediumship clearer, because a calm nervous system receives spiritual signals more cleanly than an agitated one. An adaptogenic tonic — ashwagandha, ginseng, rhodiola — taken over time will build your physical resilience *and* your capacity to hold heavy spiritual work without burning out.

## Apple Cider Vinegar Tonic — The Brujo's Daily Shot

This is my recommendation for a simple daily tonic that any practitioner can make and maintain. Apple cider vinegar is a solvent, a preservative, and a health tonic in its own right. When you infuse it with herbs chosen for your specific needs, you get a preparation that is easy to take, easy to make, and effective over time.

Choose raw, organic, unfiltered apple cider vinegar — the kind with "the mother," the cloudy strands of beneficial bacteria floating at the bottom.

Select herbs based on what you are building. For immune support: echinacea, elderberry, rosehip. For digestion and grounding: ginger, peppermint, fennel. For energy and endurance: ginseng, ashwagandha, rhodiola. For spiritual clarity and calm: lavender, chamomile, lemon balm.

Place one to two tablespoons of dried herbs (or two to four tablespoons of fresh) per cup of vinegar in a clean glass jar. Make sure the herbs are completely submerged.

Seal with a non-metallic lid — vinegar corrodes metal. If you only have a metal lid, put a layer of wax paper or plastic wrap between the lid and the jar.

Store in a cool dark place for three to four weeks. Shake every few days.

Strain, bottle, and label.

To use: one to two tablespoons diluted in a glass of water, taken in the morning before food. Start with a smaller dose and see how your body responds.

You can add honey to taste after straining. If you are adding sweetener, raw honey is best — it has its own medicinal and spiritual properties and it does not fight the vinegar the way refined sugar does.

## Essential Oils

I need to be direct with you about essential oils: you are not going to make them at home. The extraction process — steam distillation, cold pressing, solvent extraction — requires equipment that no home practitioner is going to invest in, and the yield is small enough that it takes enormous quantities of plant material to produce even a small bottle. A single pound of lavender essential oil requires roughly two hundred pounds of lavender flowers. You are not growing that in your garden.

What you need to know about essential oils is how to *buy* them well and how to *use* them in your practice.

**Buying:** Purchase from reputable suppliers. The essential oil market is flooded with adulterated products — oils cut with synthetic fragrance, diluted with cheap carrier oils, or mislabeled entirely. Look for oils that are labeled as 100% pure, that list the botanical name of the plant (not just "lavender" but *Lavandula angustifolia*), and that come in dark glass bottles. Good essential oils are not cheap. If a bottle of rose essential oil costs five dollars, it is not rose essential oil. Real rose otto costs hundreds of dollars per ounce because it takes thousands of pounds of rose petals to produce. The price tells you whether it is real.

**Using in magical practice:** Essential oils are concentrated. A few drops go a long way. Here is how I use them:

*Candle dressing:* A drop or two of essential oil mixed with a carrier oil (olive oil is traditional) rubbed onto a candle before burning. The oil carries the herbal energy into the flame.

*Spiritual baths:* Three to five drops of essential oil added to bath water. Mix the essential oil with a tablespoon of carrier oil or a handful of Epsom salt before adding to the bath — essential oils do not dissolve in water on their own and will float on the surface in concentrated droplets that can irritate skin.

*Diffusion:* A few drops in a diffuser to set the energetic tone of a room before a working. Frankincense before ceremony. Lavender before mediumship. Eucalyptus to clear a space after heavy spiritual work.

*Anointing:* Diluted in a carrier oil, essential oils can be used to anoint yourself, your tools, your doorways, or petition papers. Always dilute. Undiluted essential oils on skin can cause irritation or burns — especially hot oils like cinnamon, clove, and oregano.

**What to keep in stock:** Frankincense (ceremony, purification, solar work), lavender (calm, clarity, Mercury work), peppermint (clearing, communication, mental sharpness), eucalyptus (deep cleansing, respiratory support, clearing heavy energy), rosemary (protection, memory, Solar and Fire work), cinnamon (money, warmth, Solar and Venus work), tea tree (purification, antimicrobial, spiritual hygiene). Those seven will cover the vast majority of workings a brujo performs. Build from there as your practice tells you what you need.

## Hydrosols

A hydrosol — also called floral water — is the water left behind after steam distillation of essential oils. The essential oil floats to the top and gets collected. The water underneath has absorbed a gentle amount of the plant's aromatic and therapeutic compounds. It is essentially a very mild version of the essential oil — same plant, same properties, much gentler.

Why a brujo should care about hydrosols: they are safe to spray directly on skin, on fabrics, on altar cloths, and in rooms without dilution. They are gentle enough for children and for sensitive spiritual work where the full force of an essential oil is too much.

Rose hydrosol is my favorite. A light mist of rose water over the bóveda cloth, over your pillow before dream work, or over your face before sitting in mediumship — it softens the space and invites Venusian gentleness into whatever follows. Lavender hydrosol calms an agitated room. Orange blossom hydrosol (neroli water) brings sweetness and joy. Florida water — the classic brujo's tool — is a cousin of the hydrosol tradition, a cologne-weight aromatic water used for cleansing, blessing, and setting the spiritual tone of a space.

Buy hydrosols from the same reputable suppliers you buy essential oils from. Store them in the refrigerator — they are more perishable than essential oils and will last about twelve to eighteen months. If the smell changes or the liquid turns cloudy, it has spoiled. Replace it.

## Compresses and Poultices

A compress is a cloth soaked in an herbal infusion or decoction and applied to the body. A poultice is the plant material itself — crushed into a paste and applied directly to the skin or wrapped in thin cloth. Both deliver herbal medicine through the skin to a specific area.

For a brujo, these are healing tools. When I work on someone who has pain, inflammation, or a localized condition, a compress or poultice lets me put the herb exactly where it needs to go. A warm compress of chamomile and lavender on a tension headache. A poultice of comfrey on a sprain. A cool compress of peppermint on inflammation.

**Making a compress:** Prepare a strong infusion or decoction — double the herb concentration you would use for drinking. Soak a clean cloth in the warm liquid. Wring out the excess so it is damp but not dripping. Apply to the affected area. Cover with a towel to retain warmth and moisture. Reapply as it cools.

**Making a poultice:** Grind or crush fresh herbs into a paste. If using dried herbs, add enough hot water to rehydrate them into a thick, spreadable consistency. Apply the paste directly to clean skin, or spread it onto a piece of clean cloth and apply cloth-side down. Wrap with a bandage to hold it in place. Leave on for a few hours or as the situation requires.

**Practical note:** Always test a small area of skin first for allergic reaction. Keep materials clean to avoid introducing bacteria, especially on broken skin. Compresses and poultices are complementary to medical care, not replacements for it. If the condition is serious, see a doctor and bring the herbs with you.

## Infused Oils

An infused oil — sometimes called a macerated oil — is an herb steeped in a carrier oil over time, allowing the oil to absorb the plant's compounds. This is different from an essential oil. An essential oil is the pure volatile essence of the plant extracted through distillation. An infused oil is a carrier oil that has been *flavored* and *charged* with the plant's properties through long, slow contact.

Infused oils are one of the most versatile preparations in the brujo's kitchen. You use them to dress candles, anoint tools, anoint yourself, make salves, and create condition oils for specific workings. A money-drawing oil made by infusing olive oil with basil, cinnamon, and a bay leaf. A protection oil made by infusing olive oil with rue, rosemary, and garlic. A love-drawing oil made by infusing olive oil with rose petals, jasmine, and a vanilla bean. These are the oils that rootworkers and brujos have been making for centuries — and buying from the botánica for a markup you do not need to pay when you can make them yourself.

**How to make an infused oil:**

Choose your carrier oil. Olive oil is traditional in most Latin American and rootwork practices and has its own protective, nourishing spiritual properties. Coconut oil works well for hair and body applications. Almond oil is lighter and absorbs into skin more easily. Jojoba oil has an extremely long shelf life and resists going rancid.

Choose your herbs. Fresh herbs should be completely dry — any water in the jar will breed bacteria and ruin your oil. Dried herbs are safer for this reason. Chop or bruise them to release their compounds.

Fill a clean, dry glass jar about halfway with your herbs. Pour the carrier oil over them until the jar is nearly full and all plant material is completely submerged. Leave a little space at the top.

**Cold infusion method:** Place the sealed jar in a cool, dark place for four to six weeks. Shake gently every day or two. This is the traditional method and produces the best results. It requires patience. You are a brujo. You have patience.

**Warm infusion method:** If you need the oil sooner, place the sealed jar in a saucepan with a few inches of water. Heat on the lowest setting for two to four hours. Do not let the oil get hot — warm is what you want. You can also use a slow cooker on its lowest setting. This method produces a usable oil in a day but with less depth than the cold method.

Strain through cheesecloth into a clean, dry bottle. Squeeze every drop from the herbs. Label with the contents and date. Store in a cool, dark place. A well-made infused oil will last up to a year.

**Magical use:** Dress candles by rubbing the oil from base to tip (to draw something toward you) or tip to base (to push something away). Anoint your wrists, the back of your neck, or the soles of your feet before a working. Add a few drops to a spiritual bath. Anoint the four corners of a room for protection. Dab a drop on a petition paper before placing it under a candle. Every condition oil you have ever bought at a botánica — Follow Me Boy, Money Stay With Me, Run Devil Run, Van Van — is an infused oil with a clever name. Now you can make your own.

## Salves and Balms

A salve is an infused oil thickened with beeswax into a semi-solid consistency that you can apply to skin. That is it. Infused oil plus beeswax equals salve. The oil carries the herbal medicine. The beeswax gives it structure so it stays where you put it instead of running down your arm.

For a brujo, salves are useful for any working that involves sustained skin contact. A protection salve rubbed on the hands before doing heavy spiritual work. A healing salve applied to a client's temples, wrists, or the soles of their feet during a healing session. A dream salve applied to the forehead and wrists before sleep to enhance dream recall and lucidity.

**How to make a salve:**

Start with one cup of infused oil — already prepared using the method above, strained and ready.

Measure one quarter cup of beeswax pellets. This ratio gives a firm but spreadable consistency. More beeswax makes a harder salve. Less makes a softer one. Experiment until you find the consistency you prefer.

Melt together using a double boiler — a glass bowl set over a pot of simmering water. Stir occasionally until the beeswax is completely melted into the oil.

Remove from heat. This is when you add any essential oils — a few drops for scent and additional herbal power — and a few drops of vitamin E oil, which acts as a natural preservative and antioxidant. Stir well.

Pour immediately into small jars or tins. The mixture begins to set quickly at room temperature. Work fast.

Let cool completely — a few hours at room temperature. The liquid will solidify into a smooth, waxy balm.

Label and store. A well-made salve lasts up to a year in a cool, dark place.

**Magical use:** Apply to the body at the pulse points — wrists, temples, behind the ears, soles of the feet — before workings. These are the places where your blood runs close to the surface and the herbal medicine enters your system most efficiently, both physically and energetically. A protection salve of rue and rosemary before spiritual warfare. A love salve of rose and jasmine before a working to draw a partner. A psychic salve of mugwort and frankincense before divination or mediumship. Your hands are your primary tools as a brujo. Let the salve be on them.

## Herbal Bath Salts

Bath salts are not magic. Epsom salt is magnesium sulfate. Sea salt is sodium chloride with trace minerals. Himalayan pink salt is the same thing dyed pink by iron oxide. They are chemicals. They are useful — magnesium absorbed through the skin relieves muscle tension, salt draws impurities from the body, mineral-rich salts nourish the skin. But the salt is the *vehicle*, not the *working*. The working is what you add to the salt and what you pray over it.

A jar of bath salts infused with herbs, essential oils, and your intention is a portable spiritual bath. You can make a batch, store it in a jar, and reach for it whenever you need to cleanse, charge, or shift your energy. This is practical magic for people who do not always have time to prepare a full herbal bath from scratch.

**How to make herbal bath salts:**

Choose your salt base. Epsom salt for muscle relief and magnesium absorption. Sea salt for cleansing and purification. Himalayan pink salt for mineral nourishment. You can use a single type or mix them. I usually go half Epsom, half sea salt.

Choose your dried herbs. Crush them lightly — you want small pieces that will release their properties in the bath but not so fine that they clog your drain. A mesh strainer bag or cheesecloth pouch solves the drain problem entirely.

Combine in a bowl. Two cups of salt to a quarter cup of dried herbs is a good starting ratio. Toss to mix evenly.

Add essential oils. Five to ten drops per two cups of salt. Add the oil and mix thoroughly with your hands — the salt absorbs the oil and distributes it evenly. This is a good moment to pray over the mixture. Your hands are in it. Your breath is over it. Speak your intention into the salt as you mix.

Optional: add a tablespoon of carrier oil — coconut, jojoba, almond — for additional skin nourishment.

Store in a glass jar with a tight lid. Label with the intention and the date. The salts will keep for months.

To use: half a cup to a full cup dissolved in a warm bath. Soak for twenty to thirty minutes.

## Spiritual Baths

The spiritual bath is the brujo's most fundamental tool. It is older than any grimoire. It is older than any tradition I carry. Every culture on earth that has practiced spiritual hygiene has used water, herbs, and prayer to cleanse the body and the spirit simultaneously. When I say that a spiritual bath is not just a bath — when I say it is a ritual — I am not exaggerating. This is how I cleanse myself after heavy work. This is how I prepare myself before ceremony. This is how I reset when the world has gotten on me and I can feel its weight in my skin.

**Setting your intention.** Before you prepare anything, get clear on what the bath is for. Purification — washing off what does not belong. Protection — sealing your energy field against intrusion. Attraction — drawing love, money, opportunity toward you. Healing — soothing a wound, physical or spiritual. The intention determines everything that follows — which herbs you choose, which prayers you speak, which direction you pour the water.

**Preparing the herbs.** You have three options and all of them work:

*Directly into the bath.* This is the most potent method. Throw your fresh herbs directly into the tub along with your essential oils. As you sit in the water, the herbs release their properties around you. You are soaking in a living bath. The downside is cleanup — you will have plant material in your tub. Worth it for important workings.

*Make an infusion and add it.* Steep your herbs in a large bowl or pot of boiling water. Let it cool to a comfortable temperature. Strain out the herbs. Add the herbal infusion to your bath water. Cleaner than the direct method, almost as potent.

*Bath bundle.* Tie your herbs in a piece of muslin or cheesecloth. Hang the bundle under the faucet as the tub fills, letting the running water extract the herbs. Or steep the bundle directly in the bath like an enormous tea bag. Easiest cleanup.

**The ritual.** Fill your tub. Add your herbal preparation and your essential oils — three to five drops mixed with a carrier oil or salt to help them disperse. If you work with crystals, place water-safe stones around the rim of the tub or in the water. Light a white candle in the bathroom. Light incense if you want — frankincense for ceremony, lavender for calm, sage for cleansing.

Enter the bath and focus on your intention. This is not the time to scroll your phone. This is ceremony. Visualize what you are doing — if cleansing, see the negativity leaving your body and dissolving into the water. If drawing, see the energy you want entering through your skin. If healing, see the wounded place being touched by the herbs and the water and the prayer.

**Use sound.** Say your prayers out loud. The Our Father. The Hail Mary. The 23rd Psalm. A prayer from your tradition. A prayer you wrote yourself. Or sing — a hymn, a chant, a song that moves your spirit. Sound carries power in water. Your voice vibrating through the bath is one of the most potent tools you have. Do not waste it by sitting in silence unless silence is what the working requires.

**Closing.** When you are ready, drain the tub. As the water goes down, visualize everything you are releasing going with it. If the bath was for attraction or drawing, some traditions say you should collect a small amount of the water before draining and save it — pour it toward your front door, or sprinkle it on your path. If the bath was for cleansing, let it all go down the drain and do not look back.

If you used fresh herbs, gather them respectfully. Return them to the earth — bury them in your yard or place them at the base of a tree. This closes the ritual and honors the plants that did the work.

**Air dry if you can.** After a spiritual bath, try not to towel off aggressively. Let the herbal water dry on your skin. The residue — invisible but present — continues working after you leave the tub.

## Sachets and Talismans

A sachet — also called a mojo bag, a gris-gris, a resguardo, a paquet, depending on which tradition you are working in — is a small bag filled with herbs, stones, and other materials, charged with intention, and carried on your person or placed in a strategic location. It is portable magic. It is the working you take with you when you leave the house.

I carry a resguardo. I have carried one for years. Mine is specific to my initiations and my spirits and I am not going to tell you what is in it because that is between me and my dead. But I will tell you how to make one for yourself.

**How to make a sachet:**

Choose your fabric. Natural fibers — cotton, linen, muslin. Color matters: red for love and passion, green for money and growth, white for purification and protection, black for banishing and absorbing negativity, yellow for success and mental clarity, blue for healing and peace. Cut two pieces about four to six inches square.

Sew three sides, leaving one open. Turn it inside out so the seams are hidden. You now have a small pouch.

Choose your contents. Dried herbs aligned with your intention. A small crystal or stone. A personal concern if the working involves a specific person — a name written on paper, a hair, a photograph. A drop or two of condition oil on a small piece of cotton. A prayer written on paper and folded small. Everything that goes in should serve the intention. Do not fill the bag with random items to make it feel full. Less is more if what you put in is precisely chosen.

Fill the pouch. As you place each item, speak its purpose. "Rue, I place you here for protection." "Cinnamon, I place you here for prosperity." "This stone, I place for strength." Name what each thing is doing. This is not performance. This is programming — you are telling the sachet what its job is.

Sew or tie the opening closed. If tying, use ribbon or cord in a color that matches the intention. Tie it with an odd number of knots — three, five, seven, or nine. With each knot, speak the intention or a prayer.

Feed it. A sachet is alive — it needs to be maintained. Once a week, add a drop of the appropriate condition oil and speak to it. Some traditions blow cigarette smoke or whiskey on the bag. Some anoint it with Florida water. Some hold it to their breath and breathe their prayer into it. Find the method that matches your tradition and maintain the practice. A sachet that is never fed is a sachet that stops working.

Carry it or place it. On your person — in a pocket, in your bra, in your bag. Or in a strategic location — under your pillow for dream work, above your front door for protection, in your cash register for business, under your mattress for love. Where it lives depends on what it is doing.

**Refreshing your sachet:** Every few months, assess whether the sachet is still working. Squeeze it — the scent should still be present. If it has gone flat, open it, replace the dried herbs, re-anoint with oil, and re-charge with prayer. Some practitioners make a new sachet seasonally, retiring the old one by burying it or burning it with thanks.

## A Final Note on the Kitchen

Everything in this chapter is simple. Not one preparation in these pages requires special training, expensive equipment, or hard-to-find materials. A kitchen. A stove. Glass jars. Cheesecloth. Herbs from the store or the garden. Oil from the grocery aisle. Salt from the shelf. Wax from a craft store. Fabric and thread.

The complexity is not in the method. The complexity is in the *attention* — the prayer you speak while your hands are in the herbs, the intention you hold while the tincture steeps in the dark cabinet for four weeks, the nakshatra you chose for the night you made the batch, the planetary hour during which you strained and bottled and labeled your work.

Don Pedrito healed with water and the number nine. He did not need a laboratory. He needed clean water, clear intention, and a relationship with the divine that he tended every single day of his life. Your preparations are the same. The materials are humble. The attention is everything.

Make your own medicine. Pray over it with your own mouth. Time it to your own sky. Put your hands in the work. The botánica will always be there when you need a quick candle or a bottle of Florida water. But the preparations that carry the deepest charge — the ones that work because *your* hands made them, because *your* prayers are in the water, because *your* relationship with the plants runs through every drop — those come from your kitchen.

Go make something.
