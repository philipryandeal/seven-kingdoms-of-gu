# A History of Male Witches in Latin America

## Before the Word

Before the Spanish came with their word, the men were already there.

In the forests and cities of Yorubaland, the Babalawo — the father of secrets — sat before his divining board and spoke with Orunmila, the orisha of wisdom, through the sacred corpus of Odu. He memorized thousands of verses. He prescribed sacrifice. He read the architecture of fate. He was not a brujo. He was a priest of a civilization older than anything Europe had built since Rome fell.

In the kingdoms of the Kongo, the Nganga — the word means both the practitioner and the sacred vessel he works — held the crossroads between the living and the dead. He packed his minkisi with earth from graves, with stones from rivers, with bones and iron and vegetable matter charged with the force of the ancestors. He healed. He protected. He destroyed what needed destroying. He was not a brujo. He was a technology specialist in a spiritual science the Portuguese could not begin to comprehend.

In the limestone forests of Yucatán, the h-men — "he who knows," "he who does" — read his zaz tun, the divining crystal, and presided over the agricultural ceremonies that kept his community fed. He mixed Maya prayers with fragments of Latin he had absorbed from the friars, not out of submission but out of pragmatism. Power is power. He was not a brujo. He was a Maya shaman whose lineage predated Christ.

In the highlands of central Mexico, the nahualli shifted his shape under cover of darkness. He became the owl, the dog, the bat. His power was tied to the calendar, to his tonal — the animal companion assigned at birth — and to a knowledge system that mapped the cosmos onto the human body with a precision that European astrology could only approximate. He was not a brujo. He was a priest of the old religion persisting underground.

The Europeans brought the word "brujo" the way they brought smallpox. It infected everything it touched. It collapsed the Babalawo and the Nganga and the h-men and the nahualli into a single category: the male witch, the sorcerer, the criminal, the savage who refused to accept the true God. It erased the distinctions between healing and harming, between priest and magician, between systems of knowledge that had nothing to do with each other except that they were all practiced by people with dark skin in places the Crown wanted to control.

This chapter is an attempt to recover what existed before the name. And to track what happened after it was imposed.

## Cuba — The Brujo in the Crucible

No island in the Caribbean compressed more African spiritual traditions into a smaller space than Cuba. The colony's sugar economy devoured human beings — hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans from dozens of ethnic groups, arriving across three centuries, carrying in their bodies and their memories the religious technologies of civilizations the Spanish slaveholders considered subhuman. What those people built in captivity was not a single religion. It was an entire spiritual ecology.

By the nineteenth century, Cuba held at least five major African-derived traditions layered on top of each other and braided with popular Catholicism: Regla de Ocha, more commonly called Santería or Lucumí, rooted in Yoruba theology and the worship of the Orisha. Ifá, the prestige divination branch of the Yoruba system, with its exclusively male babalawo priesthood. Palo Monte, also called Regla de Congo or Regla Conga, rooted in Bakongo cosmology and built around the nganga — the spirit cauldron. The Abakuá secret society, an all-male fraternity with origins among the Efik and Ibibio peoples of the Cross River region in what is now southeastern Nigeria and southwestern Cameroon. And Arará, the traditions of the Ewe and Fon peoples, closely related to what became Haitian Vodou. Flowing through and around all of these was Espiritismo — Kardecian Spiritism, imported from Europe by the educated white and mixed-race middle class, then "crossed" (cruzado) with African elements until it became something Allan Kardec would not have recognized.

A single Cuban man could move through several of these systems simultaneously. He might be a santero crowned to Changó, a babalawo with his hand of Ifá, a palero working his nganga in the back room, an Abakuá member in good standing, and a practicing Catholic who saw no contradiction in any of it. That multiplicity is not confusion. It is competence.

## The Founding Babalawos

Cuban Ifá traces its lineages back to founding figures whose names are still spoken with reverence in every ilé where Ifá is practiced. Adechina — whose given name was Remigio Herrera, and whose Odu of initiation was Obara Meji — was a West African-born freedman who is regarded as the first great babalawo in Cuba. He carried the knowledge across the Atlantic in his body. He did not learn Ifá from a book. He learned it in Africa, before the ships, and he rebuilt it in Havana from memory, prayer, and the determination to preserve what the slaveholders wanted to destroy.

His successor, Tata Gaitán — Eulogio Rodríguez, Ogunda Fun — became the first to receive the title of Oluwo in Cuba, the highest rank a babalawo can hold. Bernardo Rojas, Irete Tendi, founded what became the largest branch of Cuban babalawos. These men did not have seminaries or university chairs. They had houses, memory, and the Orisha. They built the priesthood that still stands.

What made the babalawo unique among Cuban male spiritual practitioners was the exclusivity of the role. Only men could be initiated. William Bascom, the American anthropologist who conducted extensive fieldwork across Yorubaland — in Ife, Oyo, Abeokuta, Osogbo, Ondo, Ijebu Ode and other cities — stated flatly that he never encountered a single female Ifá priest acting as a diviner. The exclusion is justified theologically through the mythology of the orisha Odu, a power so dangerous that women may not look upon it. To receive Odu — the culminating step of babalawo initiation — is to accept a covenant that is, by definition, male. This remains one of the most contested gender boundaries in Afro-diasporic religion. In the twenty-first century, a very small number of women have been initiated as iyanifá in various parts of the diaspora, a development that is hotly debated and that traditional Cuban babalawos largely reject.

## The Palero and His Nganga

If the babalawo is the aristocrat of Cuban male spiritual practice, the palero is the soldier.

Palo Monte — Regla de Congo — is built around a single central object: the nganga, also called la prenda. This is the cauldron, the spirit vessel, the engine. It is packed with earth from a grave, sticks (palos, which give the tradition its name), herbs, stones, chains, railroad spikes, animal remains, and — in traditional practice — human bones, particularly the skull and the tibia, which is used as a ritual scepter. The nganga houses a spirit of the dead, the Nfumbe, with whom the palero enters a contractual relationship: you feed the Nfumbe, the Nfumbe works for you. You neglect it, it consumes you. Some practitioners refer to the nganga itself as "el brujo" — the sorcerer — because it is the nganga, not the man, that does the work.

The palero's toolkit is blunt, physical, and industrial. Where the babalawo works with palm nuts and powder on a wooden board, the palero works with iron, bone, and fire. Cuban popular culture has always treated Palo as cruder, wilder, and more dangerous than Santería. This reputation is not entirely undeserved — Palo's willingness to engage in combative spiritual work, its use of human remains, and its directness about the capacity for harm have periodically brought practitioners into conflict with law enforcement. But "crude" is a judgment made by people who do not understand the sophistication of Kongo cosmology. The nganga is not a random pile of objects. It is a precisely constructed microcosm, a portable crossroads between the living and the dead, and the palero who builds one is performing an act of sacred engineering.

Crucially, the relationship between the palero and his spirits is inverted compared to Santería. In Santería, the oricha chooses the practitioner — the oricha "calls" you, "crowns" you, mounts you in possession. The human is, in a sense, the passive vessel. In Palo, the human initiates the relationship. The palero goes to the cemetery. The palero negotiates with the dead. The palero commands. This active, assertive stance — the practitioner as agent rather than vessel — maps onto a particular kind of masculinity that many male practitioners find appealing. You do not wait to be chosen. You go and get what you need.

## The Abakuá Brotherhood

The Abakuá — also called Ñáñigos — represent something unique in the Afro-Cuban landscape: an all-male secret society with direct institutional continuity to the Ekpe/Mgbe leopard societies of the Cross River region. Founded in Havana in 1836, the Abakuá is the only African-derived fraternal society in the Americas that maintained the structure and mythology of its African parent institution in recognizable form.

Membership in the Abakuá is a statement about manhood. The society has been described by scholars and practitioners alike as defined by an exaggerated and fiercely proclaimed masculinity. To be Abakuá is to be a man in a specific, demanding sense — loyal, courageous, bound by oath, willing to die for your brothers, and absolutely unwilling to betray the society's secrets. Women are categorically excluded, not as an afterthought but as a structural feature rooted in the founding mythology: the secret of the society was originally discovered by a woman, Sikán, who was sacrificed for having heard the sacred sound. Her death is the origin point. The society exists, in a theological sense, because a woman's knowledge was taken from her and given to men.

This is uncomfortable to state plainly, and it should be. The Abakuá's gender politics are not separable from its spiritual power. They are constitutive of it. A brujo who studies these traditions honestly does not get to edit out the parts that make modern sensibilities flinch. You sit with it. You understand why it exists. And then you decide what you carry forward and what you leave behind.

## The Brujería Panics

In 1904, a toddler named Zoila was kidnapped and murdered in Havana. The case ignited a moral panic about brujería — about Black men and their magic — that the white Creole establishment had been stoking for years. An Afro-Cuban man named Domingo Bocourt, known as "Bocú," was arrested, tried, and executed in connection with the crime, along with a co-defendant named José Molina. They were the last people executed in Cuba before the country abolished the death penalty. The timing was not incidental. The state needed a spectacle. It needed to demonstrate that civilization — white, Catholic, modern civilization — was in control, and that the Black brujo was its enemy.

That same year, 1906, Fernando Ortiz published the book that would define how Cuba's intellectual class understood Afro-Cuban religion for a generation. *Los negros brujos* — *The Black Sorcerers* — carried a preface by Cesare Lombroso, the founder of criminal anthropology, the man who argued that criminals were evolutionary throwbacks identifiable by physical features. Ortiz applied Lombroso's framework to Afro-Cuban religious practitioners. The brujo was not merely a practitioner of a different religion. He was a born criminal. A biological defective. A remnant of African primitivism that modern Cuba needed to eradicate.

To his credit, Ortiz later repudiated much of this framework. His subsequent career moved toward a genuine appreciation of Afro-Cuban culture, and his concept of "transculturation" — the mutual transformation that occurs when cultures collide — became one of the most important ideas in Caribbean studies. But the damage was done. *Los negros brujos* established "brujo" as a legal, medical, and social category of deviance. It gave the state a vocabulary for criminalizing Black men's spiritual practice. And it produced a template that would be replicated across the Caribbean: the brujo as public enemy, the brujo as danger to children, the brujo as obstacle to progress.

It took decades for practitioners to reclaim the word. But they did. Paleros and other practitioners began wearing "brujo" as a badge of pride, a mark of power, a refusal to be ashamed of what they knew and what they could do. That reclamation is ongoing. This book is part of it.

## Lydia Cabrera's Men

No discussion of Afro-Cuban religion is complete without Lydia Cabrera, whose 1954 masterwork *El Monte* — now available in English through Duke University Press — is rightly called the bible of Afro-Cuban studies. But *El Monte* is not Cabrera's knowledge. It is the knowledge of her informants. And her informants were, in large part, men.

The most renowned among them was José Calazán Herrera, known as "Bengoché," a diviner and elder who gave Cabrera access to a world that no white Cuban woman would otherwise have been permitted to see. The unnamed men of *El Monte* — the paleros and babalawos and herbalists who spoke to her across decades of fieldwork — are the true authors of that book's authority. Cabrera had the pen. They had the knowledge. And here is the painful truth: we know Cabrera's name. Most of theirs are lost.

## The Gender Tension

Underneath all of this runs a current of gender politics that is essential to understanding the male brujo in Cuba. Scholar Mary Ann Clark has described Santería as "female-oriented and female-normative." In Santería, all initiates — male and female — are ritually "made" by the oricha in a process that involves being mounted, penetrated, possessed. The body is the vessel. The oricha is the agent. There is, in some Cuban communities, a persistent stereotype that male santeros are homosexual — that the passivity of being mounted by the oricha is fundamentally feminizing, and that men who accept that role are, in the coded language of machismo, less than men.

Palo Monte and the Abakuá represent, in part, a masculinist response to this perception. Palo's emphasis on the practitioner as commander — the man who goes to the cemetery, the man who packs the nganga, the man who directs the Nfumbe — is a reassertion of male agency. The Abakuá's categorical exclusion of women and its elevation of masculine loyalty and courage to spiritual virtues are another. And Ifá's male-only gate — justified through Odu, enforced through tradition — is yet another.

This does not mean that Santería is "for women" and Palo is "for men." That would be a grotesque oversimplification. There are female paleras of extraordinary power. There are male santeros of unimpeachable masculinity. But the tensions exist, and they shape how men move through the Afro-Cuban religious landscape. The brujo navigates these tensions. He does not pretend they are not there.

## Puerto Rico — The Underground River

If Cuban brujería is a crucible — intense, compressed, traceable to specific African ethnic origins — Puerto Rican brujería is an underground river. It runs beneath the surface of the island's culture, emerging in unexpected places, impossible to trace to a single source, and remarkably difficult to document.

This difficulty is the first thing you need to understand. The academic literature on Puerto Rican brujería is thin compared to Cuba, and the literature specifically on male practitioners is thinner still. Named male brujos are almost invisible in the published record. This is not because they did not exist. It is because the forces aligned against their documentation — colonial racism, state modernization campaigns, the practitioners' own secrecy, and the academic tendency to study institutions rather than individuals — were extraordinarily effective.

What we can reconstruct comes from a handful of brave ethnographers, from oral history, and from the traditions themselves as they are practiced today.

## The Blend

Puerto Rican brujería is a creole synthesis. Its ingredients include popular Mediterranean Catholicism — not the Catholicism of the Vatican but the Catholicism of the Spanish countryside, with its santos de palo, its novenas, its holy water, and its belief in the evil eye. Kardecian Espiritismo, introduced to the island by the European-educated liberal elite in the nineteenth century — a "scientific" system of communication with the dead that, in Puerto Rico, quickly merged with African and Indigenous elements to become something Kardec would not have recognized. Afro-Latin magic — the remnants of Yoruba, Kongo, and other African traditions carried to the island by enslaved people, attenuated by Puerto Rico's smaller plantation economy but never extinguished. Taíno-attributed elements — herbs, practices, and spiritual concepts that the island's Indigenous heritage contributed to the mix, though scholars debate how much is genuinely Taíno and how much is post-colonial invention. And folk Protestantism, which entered after 1898 when the United States took the island and Protestant missionaries followed the flag.

Out of this blend emerged overlapping systems. Mesa Blanca Espiritismo, the "white table" tradition of Kardecian séances with candles, water, and prayers. Espiritismo Cruzado, the "crossed" spiritism that integrated African elements. Santerismo, the Cuban Santería influence that arrived with Cuban immigrants and practitioners. And Sanse — a twentieth-century synthesis of Dominican 21 Divisions, Espiritismo Criollo, and Puerto Rican brujería, attributed to a Dominican priest known as "Papa Boko" who came to Puerto Rico around 1918 and planted the seeds of a tradition where male and female practitioners serve the Misterios through an initiatory framework.

In Sanse, a male practitioner is a sancista or sancero. He may also be called papa boko. The tradition flows through the Misterios — the spirits of the 21 Divisions — and the practitioner's authority comes not from a human institution but from the spirits themselves, who choose, test, and crown their servants.

## The Elite Male Espiritistas

One of the most surprising facts about Puerto Rican spiritual history is how many of the island's most prominent male intellectuals were practicing espiritistas.

Rosendo Matienzo Cintrón, born in 1855, was not a marginal figure. He was a lawyer, a Freemason, a political leader — one of the most important Puerto Ricans of the early twentieth century. And he was a militant Kardecian spiritist whose worldview was, by his own account, first and foremost that of a spiritist. He was not hiding in a back room with candles. He was a public intellectual who argued openly that communication with the dead was real, provable, and essential to the moral development of Puerto Rican society.

He was not alone. The poet Francisco Matos Paoli practiced Espiritismo. Federico Degetau, Puerto Rico's first Resident Commissioner in the United States Congress — the island's representative in Washington — was an espiritista. These were not campesinos or marginalized folk healers. These were men of letters, men of politics, men of the professional class, and they saw no contradiction between their public lives and their spiritual practice.

The Federación de Espiritistas de Puerto Rico, founded in 1903, claimed — through its honorary president Doña Guillermina Massanet — that ninety of every hundred Puerto Ricans were espiritistas. That number is almost certainly an exaggeration, a partisan claim rather than census data. But it tells you something about the cultural saturation of spiritism on the island. This was not a fringe movement. It was the water everyone was swimming in.

## The Hermanos Cheos

In the rural highlands of Puerto Rico, in the aftermath of the American invasion of 1898, something remarkable happened. The Catholic Church had been wounded — its property expropriated, its clergy depleted, its authority challenged by Protestant missionaries following the American flag. Into this vacuum stepped laymen.

The Hermanos Cheos were a movement of lay male preacher-healers founded around 1902 by two campesinos who were both named José — hence the nickname "Cheos," a diminutive of José. José de los Santos Morales, born around 1886 in Barrio Arrozal, Arecibo, began preaching at the age of sixteen. José Rodríguez Medina, born around 1880 in Adjuntas, joined him. Other missionaries followed: Vicente Avilés González, Juan González, and others whose names survive in local memory if not always in print.

These men assumed roles that were supposed to belong to ordained priests. They preached. They taught. They defended folk Catholicism and the cult of the santos de palo — the carved wooden saints that were the heart of rural Puerto Rican devotion. They were tolerant of espiritistas and curanderos, recognizing them as part of the same spiritual ecosystem rather than enemies to be rooted out. They identified with San Juan Evangelista. And they claimed the occasional cure.

The Church accused them of heresy. The Cheos persisted. And in 1927, the Catholic Church formally recognized them — absorbing a movement it could not destroy. The standard history is Esteban Santaella Rivera's *Historia de **los** Hermanos **Cheos*, published in 1979 and updated in 2003.

What makes the Hermanos Cheos essential to this chapter is what they represent: men who saw that the structure had fallen and decided to become the structure. Not priests by ordination. Priests by necessity. Priests because someone had to hold the spiritual line for their communities, and they were the ones who showed up. That is a lineage that every brujo in this book would recognize.

## The Brujo of Loíza

The best-documented named male brujo in Puerto Rican ethnography is Tonio Lacén of Loíza Aldea, known as "El Brujo Número Uno de Loíza."

We know his name because of the anthropologist Raquel Romberg, who embedded with Lacén and his community over years of fieldwork, producing two essential books: *Witchcraft and Welfare* (2003) and *Healing Dramas* (2009). Without Romberg, Tonio Lacén would be a memory in Loíza and nothing more. That fact should haunt every scholar and every writer who works in these traditions.

Lacén held veladas — nighttime spiritual gatherings — and maintained an altar that reportedly drew people from every corner of the island. They came before dawn. They came because he could do what they needed done: heal, divine, protect, and — when necessary — fight on the spiritual plane. He served as a spiritual father in the practitioner network of Loíza, a community with the deepest African roots on the island.

In his old age, Lacén asked his protégée, Haydée Trinidad, to take over his altar. The knowledge passed from a man to a woman — not because gender didn't matter, but because competence mattered more. Romberg writes that she is "forever indebted to Tonio Lacén and Haydée Trinidad, both now deceased, for letting me share their lives and work."

That sentence — "both now deceased" — is the reality of this chapter. The clock is always running. The men die. The altars go dark. And unless someone writes it down, the knowledge goes with them.

## The Santiguador

The most normalized and least dramatic male folk healer in Puerto Rico is the santiguador — the man who performs the santiguo, a belly massage accompanied by Catholic prayers. The santiguador treats culture-bound syndromes: empacho (intestinal blockage), mal de ojo (the evil eye), susto (fright sickness), and mal de aire (illness caused by bad air or spiritual contamination).

The santiguo is performed with the hands and the voice. No nganga. No divining board. No cauldron. Just a man's palms on a child's belly and the words of the Ave Maria. It is the gentlest form of male spiritual practice in the Caribbean, and it is so embedded in Puerto Rican daily life that many families did not and do not consider it "brujería" at all. It is simply what you do when the baby is sick and the doctor is too far away or too expensive.

Named individual santiguadores are scarce in the published academic record. The richest untapped source is María Benedetti's oral-history work — *¡Hasta **los** **baños** **te** **curan**!* and its English counterpart *Earth and Spirit* — which features interviews with traditional curanderos and spiritual healers as they practiced up through the 1980s. These are the voices of the men who healed with their hands and their prayers and never thought to call themselves brujos. They were just doing what needed to be done.

## The Colonial Aftershock

Puerto Rico's male practitioners were not only criminalized during the Spanish colonial period. The American colonial period brought its own campaign of erasure.

In the mid-twentieth century, the Puerto Rican government — through agencies like DIVEDCO (the Division of Community Education) and the Department of Health — launched public campaigns against brujería and curanderismo. In 1951, the Department of Health declared a public "war" against these practices in print. The message was clear: Puerto Rico was modernizing, and the brujo was an obstacle to progress. He was superstition. He was backwardness. He was everything the new, American-aligned, industrializing Puerto Rico needed to leave behind.

The brujos did not leave. They went underground. They kept their altars in back rooms. They served their communities quietly, away from the state's gaze. And when the anthropologists finally came looking — Romberg in the 1990s, others later — they found that the river had never stopped flowing. It had just gone deeper.

## The Gap in the Record

I want to be honest about something. The section you just read is shorter than the Cuba section and shorter than the Mexico section that follows. That is not because Puerto Rico's male practitioners were fewer or less important. It is because they were less documented.

The reasons are multiple. Puerto Rico's African population was smaller than Cuba's, so the African-derived traditions were more attenuated and harder to trace. The island's practitioners were more syncretic and less institutionalized, making them harder for scholars to categorize. The mid-century modernization campaigns drove practices underground. And the academic gaze — when it came — tended to focus on Cuba and Brazil as the "major" sites of Afro-diasporic religion, treating Puerto Rico as a secondary case.

The result is a silence in the archive. Named male brujos, curanderos, and folk healers in Puerto Rico are almost invisible in the published literature. Tonio Lacén is the exception that proves the rule. For every Lacén whose name was recorded, there were hundreds whose names were not.

This silence is itself a form of violence. And filling it — through oral history, through community memory, through books like this one — is part of the brujo's work.

## Mexico — The Land of Sorcerers

If Cuba is the crucible and Puerto Rico is the underground river, Mexico is the continent. The sheer scale of Mexican folk healing and sorcery traditions — stretching from the Sonoran desert to the Yucatán jungle, from pre-Columbian civilizations to twenty-first-century pilgrimage sites, from Aztec nahualism to border-town curanderismo — defies any attempt at neat summary. What I offer here is not comprehensive. It is a map of the major landmarks, focused on the men who built them.

## Curandero vs. Brujo

In Mexico, the distinction between curandero and brujo is officially clear and practically fuzzy.

The curandero heals. His practice — curanderismo — is a syncretic healing system that blends Indigenous Mesoamerican medicine, Spanish humoral folk medicine (the theory of hot and cold), and popular Catholicism. The curandero treats culture-bound syndromes that Western medicine does not recognize: mal de ojo (the evil eye), susto (fright sickness, when the soul is startled out of the body), empacho (intestinal blockage, often in children), caída de mollera (fallen fontanelle), and mal puesto (a hex or curse placed by another practitioner). His specialist subtypes include the yerbero (herbalist), the huesero (bonesetter), the sobador (massage healer), the partera (midwife, usually female), and the oracionista (prayer healer).

The brujo, by contrast, is the sorcerer. He is the one who casts the mal puesto, who sends illness, who works the dark. He is feared, avoided, and — when necessary — hired. In the popular imagination, the curandero and the brujo are opposites: the healer and the harmer, the saint and the sinner.

In practice, the same man is often both. A curandero who can remove a hex must understand how the hex was cast. A man who can cure susto must know what frightened the soul in the first place. The line between healing and harming is not a wall. It is a threshold that the competent practitioner crosses in both directions. Many curanderos insist publicly that they are not brujos — emphasizing the positive, healing function of their work, protecting themselves from legal prosecution and social stigma. But the knowledge runs both ways, and everyone in the community knows it.

## Don Pedrito Jaramillo

The most revered male curandero in the history of the Texas-Mexico borderlands never charged a cent for his services. When he died, they found more than five thousand dollars in fifty-cent pieces in his home — the accumulated offerings of thousands of grateful patients that he had never spent on himself.

Don Pedrito Jaramillo was born in Guadalajara, Jalisco, to Indigenous Purépecha (Tarascan) parents. The date of his birth is uncertain; the date of his death — July 3, 1907 — is not. He settled at Los Olmos, a ranch near Falfurrias in South Texas, in 1881, and over the next quarter century he became what the people called "el mero jefe" — the real chief — of curanderos.

His origin story is the origin story of a healer. He suffered a severe nose injury. He pressed his face into the mud and felt the pain subside. A divine voice spoke to him and granted him the don — the gift — of healing through faith. From that day forward, he healed.

His prescriptions were simple to the point of absurdity. Water. Baths. Herbs. And the number nine — nine days of treatment, nine glasses of water, nine prayers. His methods baffled the medical establishment, which was not accustomed to being baffled. The American Medical Association and the United States Postal Service brought a federal legal challenge against him, accusing him of peddling fake cures and defrauding his patients through the mail. His attorney, José Tomás Canales — a prominent South Texas politician — won the case's dismissal by pointing out that Don Pedrito had never charged a single cent for his cures. You cannot defraud someone from whom you have taken nothing.

Don Pedrito's legacy did not die with him. His shrine at Falfurrias remains a pilgrimage site. His prayer candles are sold in H-E-B grocery stores across Texas — right there in the aisle between the beans and the birthday cards. He is a folk saint in everything but Vatican recognition, and the people who light those candles do not need the Vatican's permission.

The first biography was Ruth Dodson's *Don Pedrito Jaramillo: Curandero*, published in 1934. The key modern scholars are historian Jennifer Seman, whose *Borderlands Curanderos* (2021) places him in the broader context of border healing traditions, and anthropologist Tony Zavaleta, who has spent decades documenting the curandero tradition of the Lower Rio Grande Valley.

## El Niño Fidencio

If Don Pedrito Jaramillo is the saint of the borderlands, El Niño Fidencio is the messiah of the Mexican desert.

José de Jesús Fidencio Constantino Síntora was born in 1898 — the same year the United States took Puerto Rico from Spain — in the state of Guanajuato. Around 1921 he arrived at the hacienda of Espinazo, a dust-blown settlement in northern Nuevo León, and he never left. Over the next seventeen years, until his death in 1938, he transformed that settlement into the most important site of folk healing in modern Mexican history.

His methods were extraordinary, even by the standards of curanderismo. He performed surgeries without anesthesia, using shards of broken glass. He healed the sick in a sulfurous mud pool, immersing them in the earth's body. He threw fruit at crowds of pilgrims, and the people who caught the fruit reported being cured. His Campo del Dolor — his "Field of Pain" — grew so large that the tiny village acquired a post office and a telegraph station just to handle the traffic.

His fame reached its peak when the President of Mexico came to see him. On February 8, 1928, President Plutarco Elías Calles — the most powerful man in the country, the architect of the Cristero War, the man who was at that moment waging war against the Catholic Church — stopped his presidential train, the "Olivo," at Espinazo. Calles was reportedly afflicted by a skin disease. The consultation lasted about three hours. On February 22, the *New York Times* ran a story about the visit under the headline "Cures by Healer Stir Mexico City."

The irony was exquisite. Calles was suppressing organized religion across Mexico while simultaneously seeking healing from a folk saint. But that is how power and faith have always operated in Latin America: the state persecutes what it cannot control, and then the men who run the state go to the brujo's door when the doctors fail.

Fidencio's high-pitched voice and beardless appearance — sometimes attributed by modern commentators to Klinefelter syndrome — earned him the name "Niño," the child. State authorities accused him of illegally practicing medicine. The medical establishment branded him a fraud. He continued healing until he died.

After his death, the Iglesia Fidencista Cristiana — the Fidencista Christian Church — formed around his memory. He is now channeled by mediums called cajitas or materias, who receive his spirit and continue his healing work. The annual fiesta at Espinazo, held around October 19, can draw tens of thousands of pilgrims. Anthropologist Tony Zavaleta conducted a multi-decade study of the Fidencista movement. Harvard historian of religions Davíd Carrasco has studied Fidencista communities. The man the state called a criminal became a saint that the state could not erase.

## Pachita — A Gender Caveat

I need to pause here and name a figure who belongs in any discussion of Mexican folk healing but who complicates the focus of this chapter. Bárbara Guerrero, known as Pachita, was one of Mexico's most famous psychic surgeons. Operating in Mexico City with a hunting knife in near-total darkness, she reportedly performed surgeries — organ transplants, tumor removals — while channeling the spirit of Cuauhtémoc, the last Aztec emperor, whom she called "Hermanito," Little Brother.

Pachita was a woman. She does not belong in a chapter about male practitioners. But I include her as a caveat because the spiritual authority she invoked was explicitly masculine. Cuauhtémoc — the fallen emperor, the last defender of Tenochtitlán — was the source of her power. Even in a female healer's practice, male spiritual authority remained the engine. That pattern — the male spirit empowering the female body — appears across many traditions and deserves honest examination rather than either celebration or dismissal.

I should also note that psychic surgery is regarded as fraudulent by mainstream medicine, and the claims made about Pachita's operations — studied by the parapsychologist Jacobo Grinberg, the filmmaker Alejandro Jodorowsky, and the controversial researcher Andrija Puharich — remain contested. The brujo's road runs through territory where faith and fraud are not always distinguishable, and honesty requires saying so.

## The Sorcerers of Catemaco

On the shores of Lake Catemaco, in the southern reaches of Veracruz, there is a town where sorcery is an industry.

Catemaco is Mexico's "land of sorcerers" — a designation that is part spiritual inheritance and part tourist marketing, and the line between the two is not always clear. The town's magical traditions draw on Olmec and Popoluca Indigenous roots, African elements brought by enslaved people during the colonial period, and Catholic ritual. The practitioners are overwhelmingly men, organized into informal hierarchies and, since 1970, a formal brotherhood.

In that year, the brujo mayor Gonzalo Aguirre — who had inherited the role from his predecessor Manuel Utrera — founded the Congreso Nacional de Brujos, held on the first Friday of March. The event opens with a misa negra — a black mass — conducted on Mono Blanco, a hill overlooking the lake. The Catholic Church objected to the name, and the congress was officially renamed "Ritos, Ceremonias y Artesanías Mágicas" — "Rites, Ceremonies, and Magical Crafts" — though everyone still calls it the Congress of Sorcerers.

The local hierarchy distinguishes three types of practitioner. The curandero heals with herbs and prayer. The chamán (shaman) does white-magic spiritual work. The brujo is willing to go further — to do the dark work, the combative work, the work that the curandero publicly disavows. Presiding over this hierarchy is the contested title of Brujo Mayor, the chief sorcerer, a position that has been fought over, split, and multiplied in the decades since Aguirre's death.

The signature ritual of Catemaco is the limpia — the spiritual cleansing. The practitioner sweeps the client's body with bundles of herbs, passes an egg over the skin to absorb negative energies, and prays. It is the simplest and most universal of all Latin American folk healing techniques, and in Catemaco it is performed thousands of times a year, mostly by men, mostly for money.

Journalist José Gil Olmos, in his book *Los Brujos del Poder*, documented the political clientele of Catemaco's sorcerers — the governors, the party bosses, the narcos who come seeking spiritual protection. He also cautioned that the Congreso de Brujos is more of a regional Veracruz event than the national spectacle it is marketed as. The tourism question is real: how much of Catemaco's brujería is living practice, and how much is performance for visitors? The honest answer is both, and the brujo who cannot tell the difference is the brujo who is most likely to be deceived.

## The Nagual

The nagual — also spelled nahual, from the Nahuatl nāhualli — is the oldest and most feared figure in the Mexican male magical tradition. He is the shapeshifter. The man who becomes animal.

In pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, the nahualli was a practitioner tied to the tonalpohualli, the sacred calendar. Every person was born with a tonal — an animal companion determined by the day of their birth. The nahualli was the person who could activate that connection, who could project his consciousness into his animal form, who could walk the night as owl, dog, bat, wolf, or turkey. The ability was tied to priestly training and to specific ritual technologies that the Spanish friars worked very hard to destroy.

In modern rural Mexico, "nagual" is often simply a synonym for brujo — a male sorcerer who transforms at night to drink blood, steal, cause disease, and terrorize. The figure has absorbed European werewolf lore (scholars like Kaplan have traced the parallels), and it is not always possible to separate what is genuinely pre-Columbian from what was imported by the colonizers and grafted onto Indigenous frameworks. But the core concept — a man who can become something other than human, who holds a power that operates outside the boundaries of normal existence — is Mesoamerican to its roots.

In some communities, the nagual is integrated into the religious hierarchy. He is known, feared, tolerated, and hired — a spiritual specialist who can remove curses cast by other naguales, who can protect crops, who can find lost objects. In other communities, accusation of nagualism brings violent reprisal. The Yucatec variant, the Huay Chivo — a male sorcerer who becomes a half-man, half-goat creature — carries the same duality of fear and functionality.

The nagual was popularized globally — and controversially — by Carlos Castaneda, whose books about the Yaqui sorcerer Don Juan Matus became bestsellers in the 1970s. Castaneda's work is now widely regarded as largely or entirely fabricated, a literary invention presented as ethnography. But the figure he drew on — the male sorcerer-teacher, the shapeshifter, the man who stands between worlds — is real. The nagual exists. He simply does not belong to Carlos Castaneda.

## The Maya H-men

In the Yucatán Peninsula, the oldest continuous tradition of male spiritual practice in the Americas persists quietly, away from the tourist zones of Cancún and Tulum, in the villages where Maya is still the first language.

The h-men — also written aj-men, meaning "he who knows" or "he who does" — is a Maya shaman-healer. His primary tool is the zaz tun, a divining crystal through which he reads the spiritual causes of illness and misfortune. He presides over agricultural ceremonies — the cha-chaac rain ceremony, the hetz mek naming ceremony — and he heals using a mixture of Maya herbal knowledge and Catholic prayers absorbed over five centuries of colonial contact. He does not consider this mixing to be contamination. It is adaptation. It is survival.

The ethnographer Michel Boccara documented the h-men Juan Cob of Yaxcabá over a span of forty years, from 1976 to 2016. That is an extraordinary ethnographic commitment — four decades of relationship with a single practitioner — and the resulting scholarship offers one of the deepest portraits of a living male healer in the Mesoamerican tradition. Juan Cob's practice mixed Maya and Latin invocations, herbal remedies and Catholic saints, ancient calendar knowledge and modern pragmatism. He was not a museum piece. He was a working healer in a changing world, and his tradition — the suhuy art, the virgin art, as Boccara called it — changed with him without breaking.

Older Maya taxonomies distinguished the h-men (the healer) from the ah pul yaah (the sorcerer, the illness-charmer), just as Mexican tradition distinguishes the curandero from the brujo. But in practice, as always, the same man often held both capacities. The h-men who could cure could also cause. The knowledge does not come in halves.

## What the Brujo Carries — Cross-Cutting Themes

Across Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Mexico — across Yoruba and Kongo and Maya and Nahua and Catholic and Spiritist traditions — certain patterns emerge. They are not identical. They are not interchangeable. But they rhyme, and the brujo who recognizes the rhyme is the brujo who understands where he stands in history.

## The Colonial Wound

Every male practitioner in this chapter was shaped by colonialism. Not metaphorically. Literally. The Spanish, the Catholic Church, the United States, and the modernizing post-colonial states all used variations of the same playbook: criminalize the practice, pathologize the practitioner, and erase the tradition from public life.

In Cuba: the brujería panics, Ortiz's criminology, and the execution of Bocourt. In Puerto Rico: the mid-century public health campaigns, the "war" on brujería in 1951, and the systematic erasure of practitioners from the historical record. In Mexico: the prosecution of Don Pedrito and Fidencio for "illegally practicing medicine," the state's alternating persecution and co-optation of folk healers, and the Catholic Church's centuries-long campaign against nagualism.

The playbook works. It has always worked. But it has never finished the job. The brujo survives because the community needs him. The state can criminalize the practice, but it cannot cure the sick child, cannot remove the hex, cannot speak to the dead, cannot read the signs. As long as there is a need that the state cannot meet, there will be a man at an altar doing the work.

## Masculinity as Spiritual Credential

The most gated, most secretive, and most prestigious roles in these traditions are consistently male-only or male-dominated. Ifá. Abakuá. The Catemaco brotherhoods. The h-men. The nagual. This is not incidental. These traditions understood masculinity as a specific kind of spiritual qualification — not merely the absence of femininity but a positive capacity: the ability to command, to confront, to enter dangerous spiritual territory and return intact.

This does not mean women were excluded from spiritual power. They were not. Female olorishas in Santería, female espiritistas across the Caribbean, curanderas throughout Mexico, Mambos in Haitian Vodou — women have always been formidable practitioners. But the male-gated roles served a specific function: they were the roles that involved the most direct contact with danger, death, and the combative dimensions of spiritual work. The babalawo who reads the full corpus of Odu, the palero who packs the nganga with human bones, the nagual who walks the night — these are roles that the traditions themselves designate as requiring a specific kind of fortitude that the communities associated with manhood.

Whether that association is biological, cultural, or theological is a question I will leave to scholars. As a practitioner, I will say only this: the traditions say what they say. The brujo's job is not to edit the tradition to fit modern comfort. It is to understand the tradition deeply enough to carry it with integrity.

## The Healer-Sorcerer Paradox

Every tradition in this chapter insists on a duality that Western thinking struggles to hold: the man who heals and the man who harms are the same man.

Don Pedrito healed for free and never turned anyone away. But the curanderismo tradition he belonged to also includes the man who casts mal puesto. The h-men who cures is distinguished from the ah pul yaah who curses, but the same person may hold both roles. The Palero heals and the Palero destroys, and the nganga does not care which.

This duality is not hypocrisy. It is competence. A surgeon understands anatomy well enough to save a life and well enough to take one. A brujo understands spiritual forces well enough to heal and well enough to harm. The ethical question is not whether the brujo *can* do harm — of course he can. The ethical question is whether he *should*, in this case, at this time, for this reason. That judgment — not the power itself — is what separates the brujo from the brute.

## Reclamation

The word "brujo" has traveled an extraordinary arc. From the colonial courtroom to the practitioner's altar. From Ortiz's "born criminal" to the man who wears the word with pride and writes it on the cover of his book.

That arc is not complete. There are still communities where being called a brujo is an accusation, not a title. There are still legal systems that criminalize folk healing. There are still scholars who treat these traditions as curiosities rather than cosmologies. The reclamation is ongoing, and every brujo who practices openly, who teaches, who publishes, who refuses to be ashamed, is advancing it.

## What Was Lost

I want to end this section with honesty rather than triumphalism.

The unnamed men in this chapter outnumber the named ones by orders of magnitude. For every Adechina whose name survived, there were a thousand babalawos whose names did not. For every Tonio Lacén documented by an anthropologist, there were hundreds of Puerto Rican brujos who healed and fought and prayed and died without anyone writing a word about them. For every Don Pedrito who became a folk saint, there were countless curanderos whose remedies and prayers evaporated with their last breath.

Colonialism did not just criminalize these men. It erased them. It burned the codices. It demolished the temples. It scattered the communities. It broke the lineages. And then modernity came and paved over the ruins and called it progress.

What survives is miraculous. But we should not let our gratitude for what survived blind us to the scale of what was lost. The brujo's road is paved with the bones of practitioners whose names we will never know. The least we can do is acknowledge that they walked it before us.

## The Men Who Made the Road

These are the men who walked before you.

Their names are in this chapter — some of them. Adechina and Tata Gaitán, who built Cuban Ifá from memory and exile. Bocourt, who died for the crime of being a Black man with spiritual power. Matienzo Cintrón, who practiced spiritism in the halls of government. The Hermanos Cheos, who preached because the priests were gone and someone had to. Tonio Lacén, who kept his altar burning in Loíza until his body gave out. Don Pedrito, who healed with water and never took a dime. El Niño Fidencio, who drew a president to his desert camp. Gonzalo Aguirre, who organized the brujos of Catemaco into a brotherhood. Juan Cob, who tended the Maya suhuy art for forty years in Yaxcabá. And the nagual — nameless, shapeshifting, ancient — who walks the night in forms the daylight world cannot hold.

Most are lost. Most were never recorded. The archive has holes in it the size of civilizations.

But the road is still here. The brujo's road was not paved for him. He cut it with a machete and a prayer. He cut it with sticks and bones and iron. He cut it with Florida water and a white candle and the names of saints that weren't really saints and gods that weren't really gone.

And now you are standing on it.
