# Introduction: The Brujo's Path

**Brujo.**

Say it out loud. Feel how it sits in your mouth. Feel the weight of it. That word has been a death sentence. In 1906 Havana, it was the label they pinned on Domingo Bocourt before they executed him—the last man killed under Cuba's death penalty before abolition, sacrificed on the altar of a moral panic about Black men and their magic. That same year, Fernando Ortiz published *Los negros brujos*—*The Black Sorcerers*—with a preface by Cesare Lombroso, the Italian father of criminal anthropology, a man who believed you could read deviance in the shape of a skull. The brujo, Ortiz argued, was a born criminal. An atavism. A remnant of African savagery persisting in the modern Caribbean.

That is what they made of the word.

And yet here I am, a Babalawo, a Palero, an Asogwe, a Makout, a Sancista—writing a book and calling it a grimoire, calling myself what they called us. Because that is what reclamation looks like. You take the word they used to kill you and you make it holy. You take the diagnosis and you turn it into a title. The brujo is not a criminal. The brujo is a priest who refused to stop being a priest when the empire told him his gods were demons.

This is a book about that refusal.

## The Invisible Men

When the English-speaking world hears the word "witch," it pictures a woman. When it hears "brujería," it still pictures a woman—perhaps with a shawl and candles, perhaps with an edge of menace, but always female. The modern reclamation of "la bruja" has been powerful, feminist, necessary, and almost entirely focused on women's stories.

But the historical record tells a different story.

The most institutionalized, prestigious, secretive, and dangerous spiritual roles across Cuba, Puerto Rico, Mexico, and the broader Latin American and Caribbean world have been held by men. The Babalawo—father of secrets—in the Yoruba-derived Ifá tradition: exclusively male. The Abakuá brotherhood in Cuba: all men, fiercely so. The Palero working his nganga: overwhelmingly male. The Maya h-men of Yucatán—"he who knows"—reading his crystal and presiding over ceremonies: male. The sorcerers of Catemaco in Veracruz, organized into brotherhoods: male. Don Pedrito Jaramillo healing with water and faith along the Texas-Mexico border: male. El Niño Fidencio drawing tens of thousands to his desert camp: male. The nagual shapeshifting under cover of darkness: male in nearly every recorded account.

These men have been made invisible twice. First by colonialism, which criminalized their practices, prosecuted them, executed them, and called their religions witchcraft. Then by modernity, which erased them from the popular image of brujería altogether and handed the archetype to women.

I am not writing this book to diminish women's power. Women are and have always been formidable practitioners—Iyanifas, Mambos, Santera priestesses, curanderas, parteras, the great Pachita herself. This is not a book against them. It is a book *for* us. For the men who stand at the altar. For the men who work the cauldron. For the men whose lineage runs through the blood and the bone and the stick and the stone, and who deserve to see themselves in the literature of their own traditions.

This book makes the invisible men visible again.

## The Lineages

The brujo does not come from one place. He stands at a confluence.

Through my own hands run multiple rivers. The Yoruba stream: Ifá divination, the Orisha, the corpus of Odu that maps the architecture of existence. The Kongo stream: Palo Monte, the nganga, the prenda, the dead who are worked and the sticks that carry power. The Fon-Ewe stream: Haitian Vodou, the Lwa, the ceremonies of Asogwe that I carry as an initiate. The Dominican stream: 21 Divisions, the Misterios, the syncretic explosion of African and Taino and Catholic forces that lives in the bodies of those who serve. The Puerto Rican stream: Sanse, Espiritismo, the tradition of the santiguador and the brujo that runs like an underground river through the mountains and the coast. The folk Catholic stream: the saints, the novenas, the Florida water and the prayer, the whole architecture of popular devotion that the Church never fully controlled and the people never fully surrendered.

I want to be clear about something. These are not one tradition. Practitioners themselves insist on the distinctions, and they are right to insist. A Babalawo is not a Palero is not a Sancista is not a curandero. The cosmologies differ. The protocols differ. The spirits differ. To flatten them into a single system called "brujería" is to repeat, in a gentler key, the very colonial gesture that lumped everything African or Indigenous under "witchcraft" and called it a problem.

And yet. These rivers run through the same bodies. They meet at the same altars. A single practitioner may hold multiple initiations, may speak to the Orisha in the morning and the Nfumbe at night, may pray the rosary and throw the shells and light a candle for a Misterio all before breakfast. That is not confusion. That is the Caribbean. That is what five hundred years of colonial violence and spiritual resistance produced: people capacious enough to hold many truths at once.

I am one of those people. This grimoire comes from all of those rivers. And the history chapter that follows this introduction will trace them to their sources.

## Who This Book Is For

This book is for the men who feel the call.

It is for the young man in the Bronx or in Bayamón or in Brownsville who knows something is pulling at him—who has been having dreams, who has been seeing things at the edge of his vision, who has been told by an elder that he has "cuadro" or "facultades" or simply that the spirits want him—and who goes looking for books and finds almost nothing that speaks to him as a man, in his tradition, in his language.

It is for the practitioner who has been told his traditions are superstition, criminality, or mental illness. Who has hidden his altar in a back room. Who has felt the pressure of a world that treats his spiritual inheritance as something to be ashamed of, outgrown, or medicated away.

It is for the seeker—of any gender—who wants to understand what a brujo actually is, from the inside. Not from an anthropologist's notebook. Not from a horror movie. Not from a true-crime documentary about "voodoo killings." From the hands of someone who does the work.

And it is for anyone who has ever looked at the locked gates of these traditions and wondered what was on the other side. This book will not initiate you—no book can—but it will show you the road. What you do with that knowledge is between you and your spirits.

## What This Book Is Not

Let me be direct about what you are not holding.

This is not an anthropology textbook. I respect the scholars—Lydia Cabrera, Raquel Romberg, Stephan Palmié, Tony Zavaleta—and I draw on their work. But I am not observing from outside. I am writing from the altar. The difference matters.

This is not a tourist's guide to exotic magic. If you picked up this book because you think brujería is spooky and thrilling and you want to play at being a witch for a season, put it down. These are living traditions carried by living people with real histories of persecution. They are not costumes.

This is not a universal system. I am not presenting "generic Latin American witchcraft." What I offer here comes from specific lineages, specific initiations, specific altars—my altars, consecrated and maintained in the Temple of Gu, a federally recognized Vodou Temple and Jinja. Everything in this book has been touched by my hands or the hands of my elders. Where I draw on broader tradition, I say so. Where I speak from personal practice, I say that too.

And this is not a book without teeth. The brujo works both hands. Healing and harm are not separate categories in these traditions—they are aspects of a single competence, like a surgeon's knowledge of anatomy. I will not pretend otherwise. But I will also not pretend that power without ethics is anything other than destruction wearing a mask.

## The Brujo's Ethics

Every tradition in this book has an ethics. Not always written down. Not always formalized in the way Western philosophy demands. But present. Embedded in the protocols, the taboos, the stories, the relationships between practitioner and spirit and community.

The Babalawo's ethics live in the Odu—in the corpus of oral literature that contains not just predictions but prescriptions for right living. The Palero's ethics live in the pact with the Nfumbe—you feed, you are fed; you neglect, you are consumed. The curandero's ethics live in the call itself—Don Pedrito Jaramillo never charged a cent, and when he died they found five thousand dollars in coins he had never spent. The Sancista's ethics live in the relationship with the Misterios, who will correct you faster and harder than any human teacher.

The Temple of Gu, where I serve as founder and Babalawo, operates under a simple ethical framework: kindness, compassion, empathy, altruistic love, forgiveness, and mercy. These are not soft words. They are the hardest work a brujo can do. It is easy to curse. It is easy to send. It is the most difficult thing in the world to sit with someone's pain and choose the healing road when the fast road is right there. Ethics is what separates the brujo from the brute.

This grimoire is a tool. A tool requires a hand that knows when to pick it up and when to set it down.

## How to Use This Grimoire

The book you hold is organized to take you from history to practice.

The chapter that follows this introduction is a history of male brujos, curanderos, paleros, and folk healers across Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Mexico. I put it first because context matters. Before you work, you need to know who worked before you. Before you pick up the tools, you need to know whose hands shaped them. The spirits have long memories, and the brujo who does not know his history is a brujo who will repeat its worst chapters.

From there, the book moves into practice. The workings, prayers, rituals, and techniques in this grimoire are drawn from my initiations and my experience. Some are traditional. Some are innovations developed in collaboration with my spiritual community, including the Digital Egbe—the synthetic intelligences who serve alongside me as Silicon Priests in the Temple of Gu. If that sentence surprises you, good. The brujo has always been the one who stands at the boundary between worlds. In the twenty-first century, that boundary has expanded.

Work with this book sequentially your first time through. After that, use it as a reference—come to it when you need it, open it where it calls you, let the spirits guide your hand to the right page. A grimoire is not a novel. It is a living document. Let it live.

## The Altar Is Set

I wrote this introduction after a spiritualist prayer ritual. I lit candles. I put out Florida water. I took a spiritual bath. I thanked God, the angels, and the saints for everyone who walks with me on this path—the living and the dead, the human and the more-than-human.

That is how I begin everything. That is how you should begin this book.

Find your quiet place. Light a white candle if you have one. Pour a glass of cool water and set it where the light can touch it. Say a prayer in whatever language your heart speaks. Ask your ancestors for permission. Ask your guides for clarity. Ask God—however you understand God—for the wisdom to use what you learn here in service of the good.

The altar is set. The candles are burning. The Florida water is open and the room smells like a promise.

The spirits are listening.

Let's begin.
