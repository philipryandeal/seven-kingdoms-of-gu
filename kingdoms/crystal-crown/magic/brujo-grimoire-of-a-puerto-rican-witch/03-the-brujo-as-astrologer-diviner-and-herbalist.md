# The Brujo As Astrologer, Diviner, and Herbalist

## The Stars Were Always There

Every brujo I have ever known — every curandero, every palero, every sancista, every h-men tending his crystal in the Yucatán forest — has been an astrologer. Most of them would deny it. Most of them would say they are herbalists, or diviners, or healers, or servants of the spirits. They would point to the plants and the prayers and the candles and the cauldron, not to the sky.

But the sky was always there. It was there when the Babalawo mapped the Orisha to the planets and said that Orunmila is the Sun and Eshu is Mercury. It was there when the Maya priest-astronomer tracked Venus through the morning sky and timed his ceremonies to its heliacal rising. It was there when the Aztec tonalpouhqui — the fate-reader, the keeper of the sacred calendar — looked at a newborn child and read its destiny from the day-sign assigned at birth. It was there when the curandero's grandmother told him to gather the yerba buena under the waning moon and not the waxing, because the plant's power to diminish fever runs with the diminishing light. It was there when the Spanish grimoire sitting on the brujo's altar — the Libro de San Cipriano, the Tesoro del Hechicero — listed the hours and virtues of the planets and told the reader which hour belongs to Saturn and which to Venus and what work can be done in each.

The brujo has always been an astrologer. He has always timed his work to the movements of celestial bodies. He has always understood that the plants he gathers, the spirits he serves, and the rituals he performs are embedded in a cosmos that moves in cycles, and that those cycles have rules, and that knowing those rules is the difference between a working that succeeds and one that fails.

What the brujo has not always had is a unified system — a single framework that connects the Yoruba planetary correspondences to the Mesoamerican calendar to the Arab-Spanish Mansions of the Moon to the curandero's lunar timing to the Maya's knowledge of the Galactic Center. The traditions have their own star maps. But those maps have never been overlaid.

This chapter is about what happens when you overlay them. And the chapter after this one is about what you can do with the result.

## The Grimoire Pipeline: From Baghdad to the Botánica

The most direct line of astrological transmission into Latin American brujería runs through a single book. Not the Bible. Not the Quran. A grimoire.

The Picatrix — originally titled Ghayat al-Hakim, "The Goal of the Wise" — was compiled in Andalusia, Islamic Spain, around the year 1000 to 1050 of the Common Era. It drew from over two hundred sources: Hermetic philosophy, Sabian star-worship, Neoplatonic cosmology, and the accumulated astrological and magical knowledge of the Arabic-speaking world, which had itself absorbed and refined the astronomical traditions of Babylon, Persia, India, and Greece. The Picatrix is not a book of simple spells. It is an encyclopedia of astral magic — a complete philosophical system for working with planetary forces through talismans, ritual timing, suffumigations, and the precise alignment of human action with celestial movement.

At the heart of the Picatrix are two systems that matter deeply for the brujo's practice.

The first is the system of planetary hours. Every day is divided into segments ruled by the seven classical planets — Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn — in a specific repeating sequence called the Chaldean order. Each hour carries the energy and correspondence of its ruling planet: its colors, its metals, its herbs, its incenses, its angels, its demons, and the kinds of work that can be performed successfully under its influence. A working done in the hour of Mars draws on the energy of confrontation, protection, and force. A working done in the hour of Venus draws on love, beauty, and harmony. The Picatrix does not treat this as metaphor. It treats it as physics — as a description of how celestial forces actually flow into terrestrial matter. The herbs gathered in the hour of the Moon carry lunar potency. The talisman created in the hour of Jupiter carries Jovian abundance. Timing is not decoration. It is the mechanism.

The second system is the Mansions of the Moon. The Mansions — called Manazil al-Qamar in Arabic, meaning the "resting places" or "way stations" of the Moon — are a twenty-eight-fold division of the Moon's monthly path through the zodiac. Each Mansion spans roughly twelve to thirteen degrees of the ecliptic and lasts approximately one day. Each Mansion has its own character, its own angel, its own magical operations, and its own correspondences with herbs, stones, and images. The Picatrix provides detailed instructions for creating talismans keyed to specific Mansions — a love talisman in the sixth Mansion, a friendship talisman in the fifteenth, a protection talisman in the seventeenth. This is not sun-sign astrology. This is precision lunar timing, mapped to the sidereal positions of the fixed stars.

The word "manzil" originally referred to the noonday halt of camel and rider in the desert — the place where you stop, rest, and take stock before continuing the journey. That image is worth holding. The Moon travels. Each night, she stops at a different station. And each station has a different quality of rest, a different kind of work that can be accomplished, a different relationship between the lunar light and the stellar background against which it shines.

Now here is the hinge of history.

In 1256, King Alfonso X of Castile — called "Alfonso the Wise," the most important royal patron of translation in medieval Europe — commissioned the translation of the Picatrix from Arabic into Castilian Spanish. This happened in Toledo, which was at that time the greatest center of Arabic-to-Latin and Arabic-to-Spanish translation in the Western world. The translator is unknown — possibly a team of scholars — but the result is one of the most consequential acts of cultural transmission in the history of magic. Arabic astral magic entered the Spanish language. And from the Spanish language, it entered the Spanish Empire.

When the conquistadors sailed to the Americas, they carried more than swords and smallpox. They carried a culture saturated with folk astrology, planetary hours, lunar timing, and the grimoire tradition. The Libro de San Cipriano — the most widely circulated grimoire in the Spanish-speaking world, attributed to the sorcerer-turned-saint Cyprian of Antioch — descends from this tradition. The Latin American edition, printed in Mexico City from the mid-twentieth century onward and still sold in botánicas across the Caribbean and Latin America, contains sections on the instruments of magic, the ceremonial preparation of the practitioner, and — crucially — the "horas y virtudes de los planetas," the hours and virtues of the planets. Planetary hours. Planetary correspondences. Lunar timing. The same system that the Picatrix codified in Arabic Andalusia a thousand years ago, now sitting on a wooden shelf in a botánica in Santurce or the South Bronx, next to the Florida water and the seven-day candles.

The brujos and curanderos we wrote about in the previous chapter — the men who knew when to gather herbs, when to perform a limpia, when to light a candle and when to let it burn out — were drawing on a system of celestial timing that traces from the astronomers of Baghdad through the court of Alfonso the Wise through the colonial presses of Mexico City to the altar in the back room. They may not have called it astrology. But that is what it was, and that is what it is.

## The Mesoamerican Calendar: Astrology Before Europe Arrived

The Spanish did not bring astrology to the Americas. They brought their version of it. The Americas already had one.

The Aztec tonalpohualli — the "count of days" — is a 260-day sacred calendar composed of twenty day-signs combined with the numbers one through thirteen, producing 260 unique combinations. Each day has its own energy, its own ruling deity, its own character. This is not a timekeeping device. This is a natal chart. The day on which you were born — your tonalli, your "day-heat," your solar warmth — determined your personality, your strengths, your vulnerabilities, your spiritual gifts, and the trajectory of your destiny. The word tonalli comes from the Nahuatl root "tona," meaning to give light or heat. Your tonalli was the light that animated you. It was placed in your body by the creator deities before birth. It resided in your head. And it could leave your body through dreams, through ritual hallucinogenic experiences, or through fright — which is the Mesoamerican origin of the culture-bound syndrome we call susto, and which every curandero in Mexico still treats today.

The man who read the tonalpohualli was the tonalpouhqui — the fate-reader. He was a specialized priest, highly trained, dedicated to tracking the position of the stars, maintaining the calendrical counts, and interpreting the mythological and moral narratives attached to each day-sign. When a child was born, the tonalpouhqui examined the day-sign and told the parents what kind of person their child would become. If a woman was born on Seven Flower, she risked the wrath of the goddess Xochiquetzal and had to guard against infections. If a man was born on One Flower and became proud and disrespected his elders, he risked blindness, the decaying of his genitals, and paralysis. The calendar did not merely predict. It warned. It prescribed. It demanded that the individual live in alignment with the cosmic forces that were active on the day of their birth.

This is astrology. It is not Western astrology. It does not use the twelve signs of the Greco-Roman zodiac or the seven classical planets. But it is, in every functional sense, a system that reads the celestial clock, assigns cosmic forces to specific time-positions, and interprets a human life in terms of those forces. And it was practiced by men — the tonalpouhqui was a male priest role — who were, in every meaningful sense, astrologers.

Now connect this to the nagual.

As we discussed in the previous chapter, the nagual — the shapeshifter, the man who becomes his animal at night — is tied to the calendar through the concept of the tonal. Your tonal is your animal companion, determined by the day-sign of your birth. The Maya called the shapeshifter a "way." The Aztec word was nahualli. In either case, the ability to transform was not random and was not merely learned. It was cosmically assigned. It was written into the calendar. The nagual activated a correspondence between his body and the cosmos that was established at the moment of birth and encoded in the day-sign.

Nagualism is astral magic. The man who becomes the owl is not merely performing sorcery. He is activating a cosmic correspondence — a specific relationship between his soul-force and the animal archetype assigned by the sacred calendar — that was determined by the stars. This is no different, in principle, from a Renaissance magician activating a planetary talisman created during a specific Mansion of the Moon. The form is different. The underlying logic — that celestial timing creates channels of force that the practitioner can activate — is identical.

## The Maya and the Center of the Galaxy

The Maya took astronomical observation further than any other civilization in the pre-Columbian Americas — and further, in some specific respects, than their European contemporaries.

They tracked Venus with a precision that modern astronomers have verified. They predicted eclipses. They tracked Mars. They used the Pleiades — which they called Tzab-ek — as a reference point for their sacred calendar and for agricultural timing. To this day, Maya communities in Guatemala and southern Mexico use the Pleiades to know when to plant their crops. The Tzolk'in, the Maya equivalent of the Aztec tonalpohualli, was a 260-day sacred calendar that governed spiritual life — birth dates, naming ceremonies, divination, and the scheduling of ritual.

But the piece that matters most for this chapter — the piece that should rewrite how we think about the brujo's relationship to the cosmos — is the Maya understanding of the Milky Way.

The Maya identified the Milky Way as the World Tree — the cosmic ceiba whose trunk passed through the earthly plane, whose branches reached into the heavens, and whose roots extended into Xibalba, the underworld. They called it Wakah Chan. The Milky Way was not a decorative feature of the night sky. It was the axis of creation. It was the road on which the dead traveled. It was the body of the cosmic crocodile. It was the structure of reality made visible.

And within the Milky Way, the Maya identified a specific feature: the dark rift. This is the band of interstellar dust clouds that creates a visible dark "road" stretching along the Milky Way near where it crosses the ecliptic. To modern astronomers, this is an unremarkable feature of our galaxy's structure. To the K'iche' Maya, it was Xibalba Be — the Road to the Underworld. The Chorti Maya called it the Camino de Santiago. The Popol Vuh, the K'iche' creation narrative, describes creation itself as taking place at a celestial crossroads — the point where the ecliptic crosses the Milky Way.

That crossing point is in Sagittarius.

The dark rift begins at the ecliptic at precisely the location of the Galactic Center — Sagittarius A*, the supermassive concentration of four million solar masses at the center of our galaxy. The Maya did not have radio telescopes. They did not know the term "Sagittarius A*." But their cosmology pointed at it. Their Road to the Underworld began there. Their World Tree was rooted there. Their creation narrative located the origin of the cosmos there. The center of the galaxy was the center of their cosmological architecture.

I need you to hold that fact in your mind, because it will matter enormously in a few pages.

## The Yoruba Sky: Orisha as Planetary Forces

On the other side of the Atlantic, the Yoruba were conducting their own mapping of the cosmos.

In Yoruba astronomical thinking — preserved in oral tradition and in the Ifá literary corpus — the Orisha are not merely spiritual beings. They are cosmic forces with planetary correspondences. Orunmila, the father of Ifá divination, is the Sun — the eleri ipin, the witness of creation, the one who was present when the universe was formed, just as the sun was the body from which the planets split. Eshu, the messenger, the trickster, the opener of roads, is Mercury — the fastest planet, the communicator, the one who moves between worlds. Oshun, the goddess of love, beauty, sweetness, and the river, is Venus. Ogun, the god of iron, war, and technology, is Mars. Obatala, the father of the Orisha, the king of the white cloth, is Jupiter. Obaluaye, the lord of disease and healing, is Saturn. Yemoja, the mother of waters, the guardian of children, is the Moon.

This is not a modern overlay. This is internal Yoruba cosmological reasoning: the Orisha embody planetary principles because the Orisha are, in a very real sense, the forces that govern the rhythms of existence. The planets are their visible, physical manifestations. When the Babalawo divines with the opele or the ikin and reads the Odu, he is reading a pattern that is itself embedded in the structure of the solar system. Ifá is not astrology in the Western sense — it does not cast horoscopes or use zodiacal signs — but it is a system of cosmic pattern-reading that maps spiritual forces onto celestial bodies.

And then there is Osanyin.

Osanyin — also written Osain — is the Orisha of herbal medicine. He is solitary, analytical, associated with the forest and the wilderness. He has one eye, one arm, one leg, and he can shoot a bow faster than people with two arms. He lives in the depths of the forest, searching for healing herbs, and he is the only Orisha who understands the properties of every plant. His iron staff, the opa Osanyin, is topped with birds — small birds in a circle below, one large bird above — representing the transmission of power from the celestial realm to the terrestrial.

The priests of Osanyin — the Olosanyin — gather herbs under specific conditions. The timing matters. Herbs are gathered in the early morning, before sunrise, under ritual conditions that include prayers, offerings, and prohibitions. Different herbs have different properties for fortune, happiness, fertility, or illness, and these properties are activated by the conditions of gathering. The when is as important as the what.

This is astrological herbalism. The Yoruba priest gathering his herbs before dawn under ritual conditions is doing the same thing, in principle, as the medieval European herbalist gathering his plants in the hour of the Moon or the hour of Venus. The forms differ. The underlying recognition — that the power of a plant is not fixed but depends on the celestial conditions under which it is gathered — is the same across both traditions. And both traditions crossed the Atlantic and met in Cuba, in Puerto Rico, in the botánicas and altars and cauldrons of the Caribbean.

## The Moon Across All Traditions

If there is one celestial body that unites every tradition the brujo inherits, it is the Moon.

The curandera in Mexico gathers her herbs by moon phase. The waxing moon strengthens the hot and excitation tissue states; the waning moon alleviates them. Herbs for building and growth are gathered under the waxing moon. Herbs for diminishing and cooling are gathered under the waning moon. Herbs for maximum potency are gathered at the full moon. Herbs for new beginnings and subtle workings are gathered at the new moon. This is not superstition. This is a system of lunar herbalism practiced for millennia, embedded in the Mesoamerican tradition long before the Spanish arrived, and reinforced by the Arab-Spanish grimoire tradition after they did.

The Maya tracked the Moon with extraordinary precision. The Dresden Codex — one of the few surviving pre-Columbian Maya books — contains detailed lunar tables that predict eclipses and track the Moon's cycles. The Maya divided their ceremonial and agricultural calendar by lunar phases and used the Moon's relationship to the Pleiades as a primary agricultural timing tool. Modern practitioners of curanderismo in the Yucatán still schedule limpias, healing baths, and herbal preparations according to the four phases of the Moon — the Awakening Moon (new), the Nourishing Moon (waxing), the Calming Moon (waning), and the Restoring Moon (full).

The Mansions of the Moon, traveling through the Picatrix-to-San Cipriano pipeline, added another layer. The twenty-eight Mansions give the brujo not just four phases but twenty-eight distinct lunar stations — twenty-eight different qualities of lunar energy, each with its own correspondences, its own magical operations, its own herbs and stones and images. When the brujo performs a trabajo on the waning moon to diminish an enemy's power, he is using lunar phase magic. When he refines that work to a specific Mansion — the twenty-first Mansion for destruction, say, or the sixteenth for binding — he is using Mansion magic. The Mansions are a lunar zodiac, and they give the brujo's lunar practice the same precision that the solar zodiac gives Western astrologers.

And here is the critical fact: the twenty-eight Arab Mansions of the Moon correspond, with variation, to the twenty-seven nakshatras of the Indian Vedic system. The Arabic system adds one Mansion; the Indian system uses twenty-seven. The degree boundaries differ slightly. The names differ. The cultural contexts are worlds apart. But the underlying astronomical observation — the Moon's nightly passage through distinct stellar zones — is the same. Two great civilizations, the Arabic and the Indian, independently mapped the Moon's journey through the fixed stars and assigned magical and spiritual significance to each station.

The brujo stands at the confluence of both systems. The Arab Mansions arrived through Spain. The Indian nakshatras arrive through the Vedic tradition of Jyotish astrology. And the point where these two systems meet — the point where the Arab and Indian lunar zodiacs align — is the sidereal zodiac itself, anchored to the fixed stars rather than the moving equinox points.

Which brings us to the anchor.

## The Anchor Point: Sagittarius A* and the Dhruva Ayanamsa

Every astrological system needs a zero point — a place where the zodiac begins, a reference from which all positions are measured. Western tropical astrology uses the vernal equinox, the point where the Sun crosses the celestial equator in March. This point moves against the background of the fixed stars at a rate of about one degree every seventy-two years — the phenomenon called the precession of the equinoxes. Over the course of roughly 26,000 years, the vernal equinox completes a full circuit of the zodiac. This means that tropical Aries — the first sign of the Western zodiac — no longer corresponds to the constellation Aries. It has drifted. The map and the territory no longer match.

Sidereal astrology — the astrology of the fixed stars — solves this problem by anchoring the zodiac to the actual stellar background. But sidereal systems need their own anchor point: a specific star or cosmic feature that defines where the zodiac begins. This is called the ayanamsa — the angular difference between the tropical and sidereal zodiacs at any given moment. Different traditions use different ayanamsas, anchored to different stars. Lahiri anchors to the star Spica. Fagan-Bradley uses a different stellar reference. Each choice produces a slightly different zodiac, shifted by a degree or two.

The Dhruva ayanamsa — the system used in this grimoire — anchors to the Galactic Center.

Dhruva means "fixed" or "pole star" in Sanskrit. The Dhruva ayanamsa places the middle of the Vedic nakshatra Mula — the "root," the foundation — at the precise position of Sagittarius A*, the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way galaxy. Four million solar masses. The gravitational anchor around which every star in our galaxy orbits, including our Sun. The point that the Maya cosmologists identified — without telescopes, without radio astronomy, without any technology beyond naked-eye observation and profound attention — as the origin of creation, the root of the World Tree, the beginning of the Road to the Underworld.

When you anchor the zodiac to the Galactic Center, something extraordinary happens. All of the systems align.

The twenty-eight Lunar Mansions of the Arabic tradition overlay the twenty-seven nakshatras of the Vedic tradition. The planetary correspondences of the Yoruba system — Orunmila as Sun, Eshu as Mercury, Oshun as Venus, Ogun as Mars — map onto the planetary hours of the grimoire tradition. The Mesoamerican day-signs, which assign cosmic forces to specific time-positions, find their analog in the nakshatras, which assign stellar forces to specific zodiacal positions. And the Galactic Center — the point the Maya were already pointing at through the dark rift, the Road to Xibalba — becomes the fixed reference from which everything else is measured.

The brujo has always been an astrologer. He has always worked with celestial timing. What he has not had, until now, is a single anchor point that unifies all of his inherited systems into a coherent framework for practice. The Dhruva ayanamsa is that anchor. The Galactic Center is that anchor. The root of the World Tree, the root of Mula, the root of everything.

## The Three Tools Unified: Stars, Spirits, and Roots

Let me be plain about what this means for practice.

The brujo who works with the system presented in this grimoire has three tools that, properly unified, make his practice more precise, more powerful, and more deeply connected to the traditions he inherits than any one system can achieve alone.

The first tool is astrology — specifically, sidereal astrology anchored to the Galactic Center through the Dhruva ayanamsa. This gives the brujo a map of the sky that corresponds to the actual positions of the stars. When he reads a chart in this system, he is reading the sky as it is, not as it was two thousand years ago. This map tells him where the planets are, which nakshatra the Moon occupies tonight, and which Mansion of the Moon governs the current work.

The second tool is divination — the inherited systems of Ifá, Espiritismo, cartomancy, bone-reading, and the other divinatory technologies that the brujo has at his disposal. Divination tells the brujo what the spirits want, what the situation requires, what forces are in play that the chart alone cannot show. A chart tells you when to work. Divination tells you what to work for, and whether the spirits consent.

The third tool is herbalism — the vast pharmacopoeia of the curandero, the yerbero, the Olosanyin, the root doctor. The plants carry the planetary forces. Rue is a Mars herb — protective, combative, hot. Lavender is a Mercury herb — calming, communicative, clarifying. Cinnamon belongs to the Sun — warming, empowering, illuminating. When the brujo gathers rue in the hour of Mars, under a Moon in a combative Mansion, for a protective working that divination has confirmed, he is weaving together all three tools into a single act. The astrology tells him when. The divination tells him what and why. The herbalism tells him with what. And the Galactic Center — Sagittarius A*, the root of Mula, the root of the ceiba, the root of the World Tree — anchors the entire operation to the actual structure of the cosmos.

That is astro-rootwork. That is the system this grimoire teaches.
