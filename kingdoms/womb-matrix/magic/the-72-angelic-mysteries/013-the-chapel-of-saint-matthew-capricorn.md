# The Chapel of Saint Matthew: Capricorn



**Saint Matthew, Apostle and Evangelist**

Saint Matthew stands at the tenth gate of the zodiacal cathedral as the apostle of converted responsibility, sacred order, and mercy at the place of work. In this chapel, the soul learns that no desk is beyond the reach of Christ, no compromised past is beyond the call of grace, and no worldly structure is too hardened to be converted into service.

Matthew is remembered by the Church as both Apostle and Evangelist. He is named among the Twelve, and in the Gospel that bears his name he is identified plainly as “Matthew the tax collector.” This detail is not hidden. The Gospel does not polish his past into respectability. It allows the scandal of mercy to remain visible.

Matthew is called while seated at the customs post. He is not called in a temple, on a mountain, or in a moment of obvious spiritual glory. He is called at work, in the middle of a system associated with money, obligation, empire, social suspicion, and public sin. Christ sees him there and says, “Follow me.”

This is the mystery of the Capricorn chapel. The soul must learn how to meet Christ within responsibility, structure, labor, reputation, money, hierarchy, duty, and consequence. Capricorn seeks order, achievement, discipline, endurance, accountability, and the building of what lasts. These desires are holy when they are governed by mercy. They become distorted when they become ambition, respectability, greed, harsh judgment, control, shame, or the belief that worth must be earned.

Saint Matthew teaches that Christ does not wait for the soul to become respectable before calling it. He calls the sinner at the desk. He enters the house of the compromised. He sits at table with tax collectors. He declares that he desires mercy, not sacrifice. He turns the man of accounts into a witness of the Gospel.

In this chapel, the ledger is brought before Christ. The past is not denied. The work is not romanticized. The call is not delayed. Mercy passes by the tax office and says, “Follow me.”

**Saint Matthew in Scripture**

Saint Matthew appears in the apostolic lists as one of the Twelve. In the Gospel according to Matthew, he is named specifically as “Matthew the tax collector.” This identification is spiritually important because tax collectors in the world of the Gospel were often despised. They were associated with collaboration with Roman power, financial exploitation, impurity, and public moral suspicion. Matthew’s work placed him in a structure that many of his own people would have regarded with contempt.

The central scriptural moment occurs in Matthew 9. Jesus sees a man named Matthew sitting at the customs post and says to him, “Follow me.” Matthew rises and follows.

The call is brief, but its force is enormous. Christ sees Matthew in the place where others see only occupation, reputation, and sin. Jesus does not begin with a public interrogation. He does not require Matthew to first repair his image before receiving the call. He speaks the word that creates the path: “Follow me.”

Matthew’s response is immediate. He rises. The man who was seated at the tax office stands up and becomes a disciple. This rising is a sign of conversion. The old seat no longer defines him. The post where he collected revenue becomes the place where grace collects him into Christ.

Immediately after the call, Jesus is at table with many tax collectors and sinners. This detail matters. The call of Matthew is not only private. It becomes a table of mercy for others who share the same social wound. The Pharisees ask why Jesus eats with tax collectors and sinners. Jesus hears and answers that those who are well do not need a physician, but the sick do. He then says, “Go and learn the meaning of the words, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’ I did not come to call the righteous but sinners.”

This is the heart of Matthew’s Gospel identity. The call is mercy. The table is mercy. The physician is Christ. The sinner is not abandoned to illness. The compromised person is not reduced to compromise. The one at the customs post can become an apostle.

Matthew is also present among the Twelve who are given authority by Jesus to preach, heal, and cast out unclean spirits. The same man once associated with collecting is now sent to give. The old pattern is reversed. Where he once participated in a system of extraction, he now participates in the mission of healing and proclamation.

The Gospel according to Matthew also begins with a genealogy: “The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham.” This genealogy orders the generations from Abraham to David, from David to the exile, and from the exile to Christ. It presents Jesus within the history, covenant, promise, and fulfillment of Israel.

This is sacred accounting. Matthew’s Gospel does not begin with disorder. It begins with lineage, structure, and fulfilled promise. The Evangelist sets names in sequence, remembers generations, and shows that Christ enters history as the fulfillment of God’s plan. The old skill of accounting is transfigured into holy remembrance. The man associated with records and accounts becomes the Evangelist who orders the story of Christ.

Matthew’s Gospel is also strongly concerned with teaching. It gathers and structures the words of Jesus with great care. It presents Christ as teacher, fulfiller of the Law and the Prophets, Son of David, Emmanuel, and risen Lord who sends the disciples to make disciples of all nations. The former tax collector becomes a scribe of the kingdom.

In Scripture, Saint Matthew gives us the mystery of vocation at the place of responsibility: Christ sees, calls, heals, gathers, orders, and sends.

**Saint Matthew in Catholic Memory**

Catholic tradition honors Saint Matthew as Apostle and Evangelist. His feast is celebrated on September 21. He is the patron of accountants, bankers, bookkeepers, tax collectors, customs officers, and others whose work involves accounts, money, records, or financial responsibility.

This patronage is not accidental. It arises directly from Matthew’s Gospel identity. He was a tax collector called by Christ. His former work becomes part of his witness, not because exploitation is holy, but because mercy converts the person and redirects the gift. The Church does not remember Matthew by pretending he was always respectable. It remembers him as one whom Christ saw, called, and transformed.

Pope Benedict XVI teaches that Matthew’s profession would have made him socially despised because it involved handling money associated with foreign authority and a tax system that could be abused. Yet Jesus welcomes him into close friendship and apostolic service. This is the scandal and beauty of the call: Christ chooses one whom others may have considered unworthy.

Pope Francis has returned often to this scene because it shaped his own papal motto, drawn from the words of Saint Bede: Christ saw the tax collector, and because he saw him through mercy and choosing, he said, “Follow me.” This phrase, often rendered in Latin as *miserando** **atque** **eligendo*, reveals the gaze of Christ upon Matthew: mercy and election together. Jesus does not look at Matthew with contempt. He looks at him with mercy that calls.

Catholic memory also honors Matthew as the Evangelist whose Gospel was treasured deeply by the early Church. His Gospel gives the Church the genealogy of Christ, the visit of the Magi, the Sermon on the Mount, the Beatitudes, the Lord’s Prayer, many parables of the kingdom, the confession of Peter, the teaching on the Church, the Passion, and the Great Commission. It is a Gospel of order, fulfillment, instruction, and mission.

The traditional symbol of Matthew among the four Evangelists is the winged man or angel. This symbol has often been connected with the human genealogy of Christ at the beginning of his Gospel. Matthew shows that the eternal Son enters human history, human lineage, and human responsibility. The heavenly message comes through ordered human memory.

Traditions about Matthew’s later ministry and martyrdom vary. Some accounts associate him with preaching in Judea and then in lands farther away, such as Ethiopia or Persia. The details are not equally certain, but Catholic devotion receives him as an apostolic witness who gave his life in service to Christ.

For the purposes of this chapel, the strongest Catholic memory is clear: Matthew is the sinner called into mercy, the tax collector made apostle, the man of accounts made Evangelist, and the witness that Christ can call a person from within the very structure that once defined their shame.

Saint Matthew is an intercessor for those who carry burdens around work, money, public reputation, moral compromise, debt, duty, vocation, and responsibility. He helps the soul bring its accounts before Christ without despair. He teaches that conversion does not erase responsibility, but it does transform the one who carries it.

**The Mystery of the Capricorn Chapel**

Capricorn is the chapel of responsibility, structure, labor, discipline, vocation, time, accountability, reputation, and the building of what endures. In this chapel, the soul learns how to work without worshiping work, how to build without hardening the heart, how to accept responsibility without becoming crushed by shame, and how to let mercy govern every structure.

Saint Matthew governs this chapel because he reveals the conversion of worldly order into sacred order. He is called at the customs post, the place of money, record, duty, and public judgment. He rises from the desk and follows Christ. Then, as Evangelist, he gives the Church a Gospel of lineage, teaching, fulfillment, and mission. The tax collector’s ledger becomes the Gospel’s genealogy. The old account becomes sacred proclamation.

The Capricorn soul wants competence, stability, respect, achievement, endurance, authority, and lasting structure. These desires are holy when they become vocation, stewardship, discipline, service, and wise responsibility. They become distorted when they become greed, status-seeking, harsh judgment, rigidity, exploitation, fear of failure, or the belief that mercy must be earned through performance.

Saint Matthew teaches the mature Capricorn path: rise from the customs post, but do not despise the need for order. Let Christ convert the desk. Let mercy enter the accounts. Let vocation reorder responsibility.

Matthew’s call does not say that money and structure do not matter. It says they must be converted. The tax office is a real place. Debts, records, duties, and public consequences are real. But none of them are greater than Christ. The structures of life must become servants of mercy rather than masters of the soul.

The table after Matthew’s call is essential. Jesus does not only call the tax collector into private spirituality. He creates a table where other tax collectors and sinners draw near. Capricorn can become obsessed with respectability, but Christ chooses communion with the disreputable in order to reveal the physician’s mercy. The kingdom is structured by mercy.

The genealogy at the beginning of Matthew’s Gospel deepens the chapel. Capricorn is concerned with time, inheritance, succession, and what has been built across generations. Matthew orders the generations not to trap Christ in history, but to reveal Christ as the fulfillment of history. This is sacred structure: memory in service of revelation.

The Capricorn chapel asks each soul: What structures govern your life? Are they serving mercy? What account do you fear bringing to Christ? Where has work become identity? Where has responsibility become shame? Where has ambition replaced vocation? Where is Christ standing at the desk, saying, “Follow me”?

Saint Matthew answers: rise. Mercy is calling.

**Oracular Meaning**

When the Chapel of Saint Matthew appears, you are being asked to examine work, money, responsibility, vocation, reputation, and the structures that govern your life.

This card often appears when Christ is calling you from within ordinary labor. You may be waiting for a more spiritual setting, a cleaner past, a better reputation, or a more inspiring life before answering the call. Matthew reminds you that Jesus can call at the desk, in the office, in the shop, in the clinic, in the studio, in the spreadsheet, in the place of duty, and even in the place of compromise.

Do not assume your work is outside the reach of grace.

This card may indicate a moment of conversion around money, accounting, debt, ambition, administration, business, career, or responsibility. Something may need to become cleaner, truer, more merciful, or more aligned with Christ. Matthew does not invite denial. He invites conversion.

If you are carrying shame about your past, this card brings the gaze of mercy. Christ sees you, and his seeing is not contempt. He sees by having mercy and by choosing. The call does not erase the need for repentance, but it does reveal that your past does not have the authority to cancel your vocation.

If you are judging others by respectability, this card brings the table of tax collectors and sinners. Jesus is the physician. The sick need him. The community of Christ must be governed by mercy, not by the pride of those who believe themselves well.

If you are building something, Matthew asks whether the structure serves the Gospel. Are you building for status, fear, control, or service? Are your systems humanizing or dehumanizing? Are your records honest? Are your obligations just? Are your ambitions submitted to Christ?

Saint Matthew brings the oracle of the converted ledger. Bring the accounts. Bring the work. Bring the past. Bring the structure. Christ can call from there.

**When This Card Appears**

When this card appears, begin by looking honestly at the structures of your life.

What governs your time? What governs your money? What governs your labor? What governs your reputation? What governing system have you mistaken for God?

If you are facing work or money stress, do not spiritualize it away. Matthew sits at a real tax office. Write down the facts. What is owed? What is earned? What is avoided? What is disordered? What is honest? What requires repentance? What requires planning? What requires mercy?

If you are discerning vocation, pay attention to where Christ is already looking at you. The call may arrive in the middle of ordinary responsibility. It may come while you are working, calculating, cleaning, scheduling, teaching, caregiving, paying bills, or managing difficult obligations. Do not despise the place where grace finds you.

If you are ashamed, pray with the call of Matthew. Imagine Christ passing by the place where you feel most defined by failure or compromise. Hear him say, “Follow me.” Let yourself rise.

If you are in a position of authority, this card asks you to govern with mercy. The Capricorn shadow uses structure to protect itself. The Matthew path uses structure to serve healing. Build systems that make room for repentance, restoration, dignity, and truth.

If you are tempted toward harsh judgment, return to Christ’s words: “I desire mercy, not sacrifice.” Ask where your religion, discipline, or moral seriousness has lost contact with compassion.

If you are writing, teaching, organizing, or preserving tradition, Matthew encourages holy order. Put things in sequence. Remember the lineage. Honor the story. Structure the teaching so others can receive it. Order is a gift when it serves revelation.

**The Shadow of Saint Matthew**

The shadow of this card is worldly structure without conversion.

This may appear as ambition, greed, status-seeking, harsh respectability, transactional relationships, manipulation through money, fear of failure, shame around work, or moral compromise hidden behind professional competence. The shadow may sit at the desk and believe the desk is the whole world.

Another shadow is using order to avoid mercy. The Pharisees object to Jesus eating with tax collectors and sinners. They prefer a religious structure that preserves distance. Christ answers with the physician’s logic: the sick need healing. If your discipline has made you less merciful, it needs conversion.

The Matthew shadow can also appear as shame that refuses the call. A person may believe that because they have been compromised, they cannot be chosen. Matthew stands against this lie. Christ does not call only those with clean reputations. He calls sinners into conversion.

Another distortion is spiritualizing responsibility away. Mercy does not mean disorder. Conversion does not mean ignoring debts, duties, harm, or consequences. Matthew rises from the tax office, but he does not become irresponsible. He becomes rightly responsible. Capricorn must be purified, not destroyed.

This card also warns against treating vocation as career alone. Work matters, but work is not God. Achievement matters, but achievement is not salvation. Structure matters, but structure must bow before mercy. The ledger must become Gospel.

**Practice of the Chapel**

To work with the Chapel of Saint Matthew, prepare a simple prayer space with a candle, a crucifix or image of Christ, and, if available, an image of Saint Matthew. Place beside the candle a small notebook, ledger, bill, calendar, work tool, or written list of responsibilities. Let it represent the structures of your life.

Begin with the Sign of the Cross.

Say:

“Lord Jesus Christ, call me from the place where I sit.”

Sit quietly and imagine Matthew at the customs post. See the desk, the records, the money, the public judgment, the weight of reputation. Then imagine Christ passing by, seeing him, and saying, “Follow me.”

Now imagine Christ looking at the place in your life where you feel bound by duty, shame, work, money, ambition, or responsibility.

Hear him say:

“Follow me.”

Pray:

“Saint Matthew, apostle and evangelist, teach me to rise into mercy.”

Then open your notebook and write three headings:

Work.

Money.

Vocation.

Under each heading, write one honest sentence. Do not make it dramatic. Make it true.

After writing, pray:

“Lord, convert my accounts into service.”

For the next twenty-four hours, practice one act of converted responsibility. Pay what can be paid. Answer what must be answered. Clean one neglected structure. Make one honest plan. Offer mercy to someone you have judged. Let one piece of work become prayer.

If you can, read Matthew 9:9–13 slowly and sit with the words: “I desire mercy, not sacrifice.”

Let this be your offering to Saint Matthew.

**Contemplation Questions**

Where is Christ calling me from within ordinary responsibility?

What desk, account, duty, or structure needs conversion?

Where have I confused work with identity?

Where has ambition replaced vocation?

What shame have I allowed to speak louder than Christ’s call?

Are my accounts honest?

Are my structures serving mercy?

Where have I judged others by respectability?

What would it mean to rise and follow today?

How can my work become Gospel service?

**Prayer to Saint Matthew**

Saint Matthew, Apostle and Evangelist, tax collector called by Christ, pray for us.

You who sat at the customs post and heard the Lord say, “Follow me,” help us rise from every place where shame, fear, duty, money, or reputation has held us captive.

You whom Christ saw with mercy and chose for discipleship, pray that we may receive the gaze of the Lord without hiding.

You who left the tax office to follow Jesus, teach us to surrender every structure that keeps us from grace.

You who welcomed Christ at table among tax collectors and sinners, pray that our homes, churches, and communities may become places of healing mercy.

You who heard the Lord say, “I desire mercy, not sacrifice,” help us practice a faith that is disciplined, truthful, and compassionate.

You who became an Evangelist of Christ, teach us to order memory, teaching, work, and responsibility in service to the Gospel.

Saint Matthew, apostle of the converted ledger, pray for all who struggle with money, debt, work, shame, ambition, public judgment, and the burdens of responsibility.

Help us make our accounts honest, our labor holy, our vocation clear, and our structures merciful.

Pray that Christ may call us from every tax office of the soul.

May we rise, follow, and become witnesses of the mercy that chooses sinners and makes them servants of the kingdom.

Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.
