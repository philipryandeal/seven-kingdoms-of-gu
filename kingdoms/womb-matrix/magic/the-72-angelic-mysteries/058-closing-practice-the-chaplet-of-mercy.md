# Closing Practice: The Chaplet of Mercy



To close this chapter, the reader may practice the Chaplet of Mercy. This devotion is for receiving the mercy of Christ and allowing that mercy to become truthful, embodied, and active.

Sit before a candle, crucifix, icon, saint card, or angel card. Place both feet on the ground. Let the breath become slow. Say:

“Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me, and make me merciful.”

Bring to mind one place where mercy is needed. It may be a failure, an old regret, a relationship, a family wound, a hidden shame, a debt, a community conflict, or a harsh judgment you are holding against yourself or another. Do not excuse what is false. Do not exaggerate what is true. Hold the situation before Christ.

Pray slowly through the twelve chapels:

May mercy interrupt retaliation.
May mercy become tangible restoration.
May mercy purify my speech.
May mercy enter memory and family.
May mercy restore dignity after failure.
May mercy guide careful repair.
May mercy balance justice and reconciliation.
May mercy descend into shame and hidden grief.
May mercy reopen the road.
May mercy rebuild with accountability.
May mercy widen the circle of belonging.
May mercy become the ocean of divine forgiveness.

After each line, breathe once and imagine the corresponding angel placing a drop of luminous water into the heart.

When the prayer is complete, ask Christ for two revelations. First, ask: “Where do I need to receive mercy?” Second, ask: “Where am I being asked to show mercy?” Let the answers be specific. Mercy that remains vague cannot easily become practice.

If the mercy concerns your own failure, ask what repentance requires. If the mercy concerns another person, ask what love requires and what wisdom protects. If the mercy concerns an old wound, ask whether the next step is prayer, truth, distance, repair, forgiveness, or rest.

The Angels of Mercy do not ask the reader to deny harm. They ask the reader to deny despair. They do not ask the reader to erase justice. They ask the reader to let justice be transfigured by Christ. They do not ask the reader to make every relationship safe again. They ask the reader to let hatred lose its throne.

Where mercy enters, shame begins to loosen. Where shame loosens, repentance becomes possible. Where repentance becomes possible, restoration begins. And where restoration begins, the chapel opens.
