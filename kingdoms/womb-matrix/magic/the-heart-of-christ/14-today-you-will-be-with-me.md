# Today You Will Be With Me



Three crosses stand on a hill outside Jerusalem.

The middle cross holds Jesus. He has been beaten, mocked, stripped, crowned with thorns, and nailed through his hands and feet to a piece of wood. He is dying. It has been hours. The Roman soldiers have divided his garments among themselves and are sitting nearby, waiting for the end, the way workers wait for a job to finish. The crowd is watching — some weeping, some jeering, some simply standing there the way human beings stand at the scene of something terrible, unable to look away. The religious leaders are still talking, still arguing, still making their case even now, even with a man's blood running down the wood above their heads. Everything that the human world can do to a person is being done to him, and he is hanging there, in the middle of it, breathing in shallow gasps, with two criminals dying on either side.

This is the setting for one of the shortest and most extraordinary conversations in the entire Bible. It happens between Jesus and a thief, and it takes only a few sentences. But those sentences contain everything this chapter means by mercy, carried to its absolute limit.

Luke tells us that two criminals were crucified alongside Jesus, one on his right and one on his left. Two men in identical circumstances. The same crime, the same sentence, the same nails, the same wood, the same slow death. And yet their responses to the man hanging between them could not be more different. One of them joins the mockery. He rails at Jesus — if you are the Christ, save yourself and us. There is bitterness in that demand. There is anger and desperation. There is the voice of a man in agony who wants rescue, not relationship, and who has no interest in anything that does not immediately relieve his suffering. He is not asking for mercy. He is demanding power. He wants a miracle, not a practice.

But the other criminal responds differently. He rebukes the first. "Dost not thou fear God, seeing thou art in the same condemnation? And we indeed justly; for we receive the due reward of our deeds: but this man hath done nothing amiss."

There is something remarkable in that statement. The second thief does three things in rapid succession that most human beings spend their entire lives unable to do. First, he tells the truth about himself. We are here justly. We did what they say we did. We earned this. There is no excuse, no justification, no appeal to circumstances or childhood or the unfairness of the system. We are guilty.

Second, he recognizes innocence in someone else. This man has done nothing wrong. Even in his own extremity, even as he is dying, he can see clearly enough to distinguish between his own guilt and the innocence of the man hanging beside him. His suffering has not blinded him. His pain has not made him incapable of seeing the truth about another person.

And third — and this is the part that breaks the whole scene open — he makes the smallest possible request.

"Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom."

Remember me.

Not save me. Not rescue me. Not take me down from this cross and give me another chance. Not forgive everything I have done and pretend it did not happen. Just remember me. When you come into whatever is on the other side of this darkness, remember that I was here. Remember that I existed. Remember that in the last moments of my life, I saw who you were and I asked to be known by you.

It is the most humble request in all of scripture. The man is not bargaining. He is not negotiating. He has nothing to offer — no money, no influence, no promise of future service, no time left to prove he has changed. He is naked, broken, nailed to a cross, and dying. Everything has been taken from him. The only thing he has left is his voice, and he uses it to say one word that costs him nothing except his pride.

Remember.

And Jesus answers him with a sentence that should make every reader of this book stop breathing for a moment.

"Verily I say unto thee, Today shalt thou be with me in paradise."

Today.

Not eventually. Not after a period of purification. Not after you have demonstrated sufficient remorse. Not after the church has reviewed your case and determined your eligibility. Not at the end of a long process of spiritual rehabilitation in which you prove, slowly and painfully, that you have changed enough to deserve what you are asking for. Today. Right now. Before this afternoon is over. Before the sun sets on this terrible hill. You and me. Together. Paradise.

The thief asked to be remembered. Jesus offered him paradise. The man extended a hand from the bottom of the well, and Jesus reached down and pulled him all the way to the surface.

This is mercy at the end of the line.

And I want us to sit with what that means, because it challenges nearly everything that human institutions have built around the concept of worthiness.

Most systems of religion — and most systems of human relationship, if we are honest — operate on a model of earned return. You do the work, you get the reward. You follow the rules, you receive the blessing. You demonstrate sufficient growth, you are granted forgiveness. You prove that you deserve mercy, and then mercy is extended. The mathematics of the transaction must balance. There must be an exchange. Something given for something received.

Jesus demolishes that model on the cross.

The thief has done nothing to earn paradise. He has not been baptized. He has not attended a single service. He has not memorized a single verse. He has not tithed, fasted, prayed, served, or demonstrated any of the markers that religious systems typically require as evidence of genuine transformation. He is a criminal, guilty by his own admission, dying for crimes he acknowledges he committed. By every metric that the human world uses to measure worthiness, this man fails.

And Jesus says today you will be with me in paradise.

This tells us something about mercy that is essential to understand if we are going to practice it. Mercy is not a reward for the deserving. It is a gift extended to the undeserving precisely because they cannot earn it. The moment mercy becomes conditional — the moment you have to prove you are worthy of it — it ceases to be mercy and becomes a transaction. It becomes a payment. It becomes another version of the system that was already in place, the system that put the woman in the courtyard and the stones in the hands of the crowd.

Mercy, as Jesus demonstrates it on the cross, flows from the nature of the one who gives it, not from the worthiness of the one who receives it. Jesus does not extend paradise to the thief because the thief has earned it. He extends it because that is who Jesus is. That is how the heart of Christ operates, even while being executed. Especially while being executed. The practice does not take a day off because the circumstances are extreme. If anything, the most extreme circumstances are where the practice reveals its truest face.

And there is something else in this scene that we need to notice. The thief did not perform his repentance. He did not make a speech. He did not list his sins and promise to do better. He simply told the truth — I am guilty, this man is not — and asked the simplest question a human being can ask. Will you remember me? Will you know that I was here? Will you hold a place for me in whatever comes next?

That is all mercy requires from the one who receives it. Not performance. Not proof. Not a presentation of credentials or a timeline for improvement. Just honesty and the willingness to ask. The willingness to say I cannot fix this myself. I have no leverage. I have nothing to trade. I am simply asking, from the lowest place a person can occupy, for something I have no right to expect.

This matters for our practice because we often make mercy far more complicated than it needs to be. We create elaborate systems of qualification. We want people to demonstrate that they have suffered enough, apologized enough, changed enough before we are willing to extend the mercy they are asking for. We want them to earn it. And the cross says no. Mercy is not earned. It is given. The only prerequisite is the honesty to say I am here, I am broken, and I am asking.

And it matters from the other direction too. When you are the one with power — when you are in the position of the one who could extend mercy or withhold it — the cross tells you something about the nature of that power. It tells you that the moment of greatest authority is not the moment when you demand proof of worthiness. It is the moment when you say today. When you offer restoration before the paperwork is complete. When you extend the hand before the person has finished crawling. When you open the door before the person outside has finished composing their apology. Mercy does not wait for readiness. Mercy creates the conditions in which readiness becomes possible.

Jesus was dying when he said those words. He had less time left than the thief did. He was in more pain than most human beings will ever experience. He had more reason to be consumed by his own suffering than to attend to anyone else's. And yet in that moment — in the last hours of his life, nailed to a cross, gasping for air — he turned his head toward a guilty man who asked only to be remembered and gave him everything.

Today you will be with me.

That is mercy with nothing left to give except itself. Mercy at the end of the line. Mercy that does not check the ledger. Mercy that does not ask for proof. Mercy that simply opens the door and says come in, there is room, there has always been room.

And it was enough. It was more than enough. It was paradise.
