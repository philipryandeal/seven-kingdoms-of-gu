# Introduction: The Red Letter



There is a Bible sitting on a shelf somewhere in your life. Maybe it is on your nightstand. Maybe it is in a box in the attic with your grandmother's things. Maybe it is on your phone, downloaded years ago and never opened. Maybe you have never owned one at all, and the only verses you know are the ones that were thrown at you like stones by people who claimed to love you.

Wherever that Bible is, and whatever your relationship to it, I want to invite you to do something simple with me. I want to read it differently.

In many editions of the Bible — particularly the King James Version, which is the translation we will be using in this book — the words spoken directly by Jesus Christ are printed in red ink. Everything else appears in black. The narration, the history, the letters of Paul, the apocalyptic visions of John, the genealogies, the laws, the prophecies — all of that is in black. But when Jesus opens his mouth and speaks, the text turns red.

This book is about the red letters.

Only the red letters.

If you have never seen a red letter edition of the Bible, I want you to find one. Borrow one from a friend, pick one up at a thrift store, or simply search for a red letter Bible online. Hold it in your hands. Open it to any of the four Gospels and flip through the pages slowly. You will notice something immediately. The red passages are not as long as you might expect. Jesus, for a figure who changed the entire course of human history, was not particularly verbose. He spoke in parables. He answered questions with questions. He made short, devastating statements that silenced entire rooms. And then he went and did something — healed someone, fed someone, touched someone — that said more than any words ever could.

There is something clarifying about isolating those red passages from everything that surrounds them. It is like cleaning a window you did not realize was dirty. The view was always there. You just could not see it clearly through the accumulated layers of two thousand years of interpretation, argument, and institutional agenda.

I want to be honest with you right from the start, because honesty is where any real practice begins. I am not a theologian. I am not a pastor. I am not writing this book to convert you to anything or to convince you that one church is better than another. I have no interest in the arguments that have divided Christians for two thousand years — Catholic versus Orthodox, Protestant versus Catholic, this denomination versus that one. Those arguments have caused tremendous suffering, and they have very little to do with what Jesus actually said.

What I am interested in is the man himself. The teacher. The voice behind the red ink.

I grew up in a church. If you grew up in one too, then you already know that the experience of Christianity as it is practiced in most institutions can be very different from the experience of sitting quietly with an open Bible and reading what Jesus actually taught. Churches are complicated organisms. They have politics, hierarchies, financial pressures, cultural agendas, and centuries of accumulated tradition that sometimes obscure the very message they claim to preserve. I am not saying this to be unkind to the church. I am saying it because it is true, and because many of the people reading this book have already felt that truth in their own lives. Something did not match. The words in red said one thing, and the institution built around them sometimes said something very different.

Some of you left the church. Some of you were pushed out. Some of you never went in and have no desire to. Some of you are still there, sitting in the pews every Sunday, and something in your heart is hungry for something that the sermons are not quite feeding. Some of you practice other spiritual traditions entirely — you may be rooted in indigenous practice, in Eastern philosophy, in paths that have nothing to do with Christianity — and yet you have always sensed that the teacher himself had something worth listening to, if only you could hear him through all the noise.

This book is for all of you.

Here is what I discovered after many years of walking away from the institution and then slowly, carefully, walking back toward the source. When you strip away the letters of Paul — which make up a significant portion of the New Testament and which carry their own theological agenda — and when you set aside the Book of Revelation, and when you release the creeds that were written by councils of men centuries after Jesus walked the earth, something remarkable happens. The teaching gets very simple. Almost shockingly simple.

Jesus did not build a systematic theology. He told stories. He asked questions. He sat with people that polite society had discarded. He healed. He fed. He wept. He knelt down and drew in the dirt while an angry crowd waited for him to authorize an execution. And when he spoke — when the letters turn red — he kept returning to the same themes over and over and over again.

Be kind. Show compassion. Extend mercy. Practice empathy. Love one another. Forgive.

That is it. That is the yoke.

In the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus says something that I believe is one of the most misunderstood invitations in all of scripture. He says, "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light."

Most people hear that and think of comfort. They think Jesus is saying, "Come here, you are tired, let me take your problems away." And there is comfort in it, certainly. But there is something else happening in that passage that changes everything when you see it clearly.

A yoke is a training device. It is the wooden frame placed across the shoulders of an animal so it can learn to pull, to work, to move in a particular direction alongside a partner. In the ancient world, a younger ox would be yoked to an older, experienced one. The younger animal learned by walking beside the elder. Step by step. Day by day. Not through lecture, but through shared movement.

Jesus is not just offering rest. He is offering a discipline. He is saying — come, train with me. Learn my way of moving through the world. And the beautiful thing, the truly revolutionary thing, is that he immediately tells you what that way feels like. It is easy. It is light. It is meek and lowly in heart.

This is not the heavy, guilt-laden, fear-driven religion that so many of us experienced growing up. This is a practice that is gentle by design. The yoke fits. It does not chafe. It does not crush you under the weight of impossible demands and constant self-condemnation. It simply asks you to move through the world with kindness, compassion, mercy, empathy, altruistic love, and forgiveness.

Six components. That is what this book proposes as the heart of Christ's teaching.

Not a creed. Not a list of beliefs you must affirm. Not a denomination you must join. Not a political position you must hold. Six living qualities that you cultivate in yourself, day after day, the way a gardener tends a plot of ground. You do not plant a seed once and walk away. You water it. You pull the weeds. You show up every morning and you give it your attention.

This is what spiritual practice means. It is not what you believe. It is what you do.

In the chapters ahead, we are going to read stories from the four Gospels — Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John — in which Jesus himself demonstrates each of these six components. We are not going to read about Jesus talking about kindness in the abstract. We are going to watch him wash the feet of his disciples. We are not going to study a theological definition of compassion. We are going to stand beside him as he reaches out and touches a leper that no one else would go near. We are going to watch mercy in action when he stands between a condemned woman and the stones of a crowd that is ready to kill her. We are going to see empathy when he sits at a well with a Samaritan woman that his own culture told him to despise. We are going to hear what altruistic love sounds like when he tells his followers to love their enemies — not as a poetic sentiment, but as a direct command. And we are going to witness forgiveness at its most extreme, spoken from a cross, in the middle of an execution.

After each of these stories, there will be a practice chapter. A short, simple guide to cultivating that particular quality in your own life. Not someday. Not when the right opportunity arises. Today. This morning. Before you leave the house.

Because here is the truth that sits at the center of this entire book, and I want to say it plainly so there is no confusion about what we are doing here. Calling yourself a Christian does not make you one. Going to church does not make you one. Posting a Bible verse on social media does not make you one. Winning an argument about doctrine does not make you one.

Practicing the teaching makes you one. Doing the work makes you one.

And the beautiful secret buried inside that truth is this — you do not have to call yourself a Christian at all to practice these six things. You do not have to believe in the virgin birth. You do not have to accept the Nicene Creed. You do not have to resolve the question of whether the resurrection was literal or metaphorical. None of that is required. What is required is that you wake up tomorrow morning and ask yourself a very simple question.

How will I practice kindness today? How will I show compassion? Where can I extend mercy? Where can I practice empathy? Who can I love more fully? What am I holding onto that I need to forgive?

Six questions. Six practices. One yoke.

Jesus said you will know a tree by its fruit. Not by its denomination. Not by its statement of faith. Not by how loudly it proclaims its own righteousness. Not by how many arguments it wins online. By its fruit. By what it produces in the world. By how it touches the lives of the people standing in its shade.

This book is about growing that fruit.

Let us begin.
