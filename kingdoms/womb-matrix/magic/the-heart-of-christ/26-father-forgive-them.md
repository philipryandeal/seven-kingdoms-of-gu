# Father Forgive Them



Everything in this book has been leading here.

Every component. Every story. Every practice. The kindness that kneels. The compassion that touches the untouchable. The mercy that puts down the stone. The empathy that weeps at the tomb. The love that blesses its enemies and tells the truth even when the truth drives people away. All of it has been preparation for this moment — the moment when the practice is tested under conditions so extreme that if it holds here, it holds everywhere. If it breaks here, it was never real to begin with.

Jesus is on the cross.

I am not going to linger on the physical details of crucifixion, though they are well documented and they are terrible beyond what most modern people can imagine. What I want you to understand is simply this — the man who taught everything we have been reading in this book, the man who washed feet and touched lepers and wept at tombs and told us to love our enemies, is being executed. Publicly. Slowly. By the state, with the full cooperation of the religious authorities, in front of a crowd that includes people who waved palm branches for him days earlier and are now watching him die without lifting a hand to stop it. His friends have scattered. One has betrayed him. One has denied knowing him three times. The movement he spent three years building appears to be over. Everything he said and did and taught is hanging on a cross alongside him, bleeding out in the afternoon sun.

And in the middle of that — not before it, not after it, not in the safety of a room with his disciples, but in the middle of the nails and the wood and the slow suffocation that crucifixion inflicts — Jesus speaks.

"Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do."

I have read those words hundreds of times in my life, and they still stop me cold. Every single time. Not because they are beautiful, though they are. Not because they are theologically profound, though they are that too. They stop me because I know myself well enough to know what I would say from that cross. I know what most of us would say. We would scream. We would curse. We would say — look what you have done to me. We would say — you will pay for this, all of you, every single one of you who stood by and let this happen. We would say — God, bring your justice down on these people who are torturing your son. We would cry out for rescue, for revenge, for the world to see what cruelty looks like and to punish the people responsible for it.

Jesus asks for their forgiveness.

Not his own forgiveness. Not forgiveness for himself. He asks the Father to forgive THEM — the soldiers who drove the nails, the authorities who ordered the execution, the crowd that chose Barabbas, the system that processed his death with bureaucratic efficiency, the entire machinery of human cruelty that conspired to put the most loving person who ever lived on a piece of wood and watch him die.

Forgive them.

And then he adds four words that I believe may be the most important words in the entire New Testament.

For they know not what they do.

This is not an excuse. Jesus is not saying what they are doing is acceptable. He is not minimizing the violence, dismissing the injustice, or pretending that the nails do not hurt. He is offering something far more profound than an excuse. He is offering an explanation — not for their benefit, but for ours. He is showing us the lens through which forgiveness becomes possible.

They do not know what they are doing.

The soldiers think they are executing a criminal. The authorities think they are eliminating a threat. The crowd thinks they are watching justice be served. Every person involved in this crucifixion believes they are doing the right thing, or at least the necessary thing, or at the very least the thing they were told to do by someone with more authority than they have. They are not monsters. They are human beings operating inside a system of power and fear and institutional momentum that has brought them to this hill on this afternoon, and they do not understand — cannot understand, from where they stand — what they are actually destroying.

This is what Jesus sees from the cross. Not villains. Not enemies to be cursed. Human beings who are blind to the full reality of what they are participating in. And his response to that blindness is not rage. It is not condemnation. It is a prayer for their forgiveness.

I want you to sit with what this means for your own practice, because it changes everything.

Think about the person who hurt you most deeply. The person whose face appeared when you read the last chapter. The one whose coal you have been holding. Now ask yourself a question that may feel almost offensive in its generosity — is it possible that they did not fully know what they were doing?

I am not asking you to excuse them. I am not asking you to decide that what they did was understandable or forgivable in the conventional sense. I am asking you to consider the possibility that the person who wounded you was operating out of their own blindness — their own wounds, their own fear, their own unexamined pain, their own limited understanding of the damage they were causing. This does not make the damage less real. A car driven by a blind person still destroys what it hits. But it changes the relationship between you and the driver. It opens a crack in the wall of your resentment — not a door, not yet, just a crack — through which something other than anger might eventually enter.

They knew not what they did.

This is the practice of forgiveness at its most extreme and most liberating. It is the willingness to look at the person who harmed you and see, behind the harm, a human being who was not operating with full sight. Who was acting out of damage they may not even be aware of. Who was repeating patterns they inherited from people who hurt them, who were repeating patterns they inherited from people who hurt them, all the way back through generations of blindness and pain and unhealed wounds passing from one set of hands to the next.

This does not make forgiveness easy. Nothing makes forgiveness easy. But it makes forgiveness possible, because it shifts the question from how could they do this to me to what were they unable to see? And that shift — from accusation to understanding, from rage to something approaching sorrowful clarity — is the beginning of release.

Jesus does not forgive from a place of untouchable divine strength. I want to be very clear about this, because there is a version of this story that makes Jesus' forgiveness sound effortless — the serene Son of God calmly dispensing grace from above the suffering, floating above the pain, forgiving the way a king pardons a peasant from the comfort of his throne. That is not what is happening on Calvary. Jesus is in agony. He is suffocating. He is bleeding. He has been abandoned by nearly everyone he loves. This forgiveness comes from the floor of human suffering, not from above it. It comes from a man who is experiencing the worst that human beings can do to another human being and who, from inside that experience, chooses to release his grip on the resentment he has every right to feel.

If he had held onto the anger, no one would have blamed him. If he had called down fire from heaven, many of his followers would have cheered. If he had simply said nothing — if he had died in silence, bearing the pain without comment — that would have been noble enough. But he does not stay silent. He speaks. And what he speaks is forgiveness. Voluntarily. Unprompted. From the very bottom of human suffering.

This is the ultimate test of the yoke. This is where we find out if the practice is real.

And here is what I want you to carry away from this chapter, held gently, without pressure, without any demand that you achieve it tomorrow or next month or even in this lifetime. If the practice held on the cross — if kindness and compassion and mercy and empathy and love and forgiveness survived the worst thing that has ever been done to the best person who ever lived — then the practice is real. It works. It is not a fantasy or an ideal or a beautiful thought that collapses the moment reality tests it. It endured the ultimate test and it did not break.

Which means your practice can hold too.

Not because your suffering is less than his — suffering is not a competition, and your pain is your pain and it is real. But because the yoke he designed was built to withstand the worst. It was forged on the cross. If it held there, it can hold in your living room, in your office, in your marriage, in your family, in the quiet hours of the night when the memory surfaces and the anger rises and the coal starts burning in your hand again.

You do not have to forgive the way Jesus forgave. Not yet. Maybe not ever, not to that degree, not under those conditions. But you can begin. You can look at the person who hurt you and wonder, just for a moment, what they could not see. You can ask yourself whether their blindness, however inexcusable, might be part of the story. You can hold the possibility of forgiveness in your hands the way you would hold something fragile and precious — not gripping it, not forcing it, just holding it. Letting it be there. Letting it do its quiet work.

Father, forgive them. For they know not what they do.

One day you may be able to say those words about the person who wounded you. Not today, perhaps. Not this year, perhaps. But one day. And today, it is enough to hear Jesus say them from the cross and to know, deep in your bones, that the practice is real.
