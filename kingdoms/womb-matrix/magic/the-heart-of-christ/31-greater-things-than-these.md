# Greater Things Than These



We began this book with a question. When you hear the word Christ, what do you see?

In the first chapter, I asked you to set aside the institution, the creeds, the denominations, the two thousand years of argument and interpretation, and to look at the red letters — the words spoken by Jesus himself. In the second chapter, I asked you to reconsider what a yoke is — not a burden but a training device, a technology for learning a way of moving through the world that is easy and light. In the third chapter, I asked you to consider the most radical proposition in this entire book — that Christ is not only a person but a verb. A state of being that is cultivated through practice. Something you can become.

And then, for twenty-one chapters, we practiced.

We watched Jesus kneel with a basin and wash the feet of the men he led, including the one who would betray him. We watched him override his own disciples to welcome children that the gatekeepers tried to turn away. We stood beside him as he reached out and touched a leper that the entire world had declared untouchable. We sat at his feet as he told the story of a Samaritan who crossed the road when the priest and the Levite would not. We watched him write in the dirt while a crowd held stones and waited for permission to execute a woman, and we heard him say the sentence that emptied every hand — he that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone. We watched him turn to a dying thief in the final hours of his own life and promise him paradise with a generosity that defies everything the world teaches about who deserves grace. We crossed a cultural boundary with him to sit at a well with a woman his society told him to despise, and we watched him ask her for water before he offered her living water. We stood at the tomb of his friend and watched him weep — not because he could not fix it, but because he chose to feel it first. We heard him command us to love our enemies, and we watched him look at a rich young man with love and then tell him the one thing he did not want to hear. We listened as he told Peter to stop counting forgiveness and as he prayed from a cross for the people who put him there. And we sat on a beach at dawn and heard him ask the man who denied him three times — do you love me? — and then hand him back his purpose.

Those stories are inside you now. They are not just information you processed and filed away. They are not just verses you read and noted and moved past. If this book has done its work — and I believe it has, because you are still here, still reading, still carrying the yoke — then those stories have been working on you the way water works on stone, the way yeast works in dough, the way a seed works in dark soil long before any green appears above the surface. Slowly. Quietly. Below the level of your conscious awareness. Something in you has shifted over the course of these pages, even if you cannot name what it is yet. Something has softened that was hard. Something has opened that was closed. Something has put down a weight it was carrying so long it forgot the weight was not part of its own body. Something in you is ready for what comes next.

And now we return to the red letter that started it all.

In the Gospel of John, chapter fourteen, verse twelve, Jesus says to his disciples — to ordinary, imperfect, frightened human beings who will fail him before the week is out — the most extraordinary promise in all of scripture.

"Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that believeth on me, the works that I do shall he do also; and greater works than these shall he do; because I go unto my Father."

Greater works than these.

I want you to hear this not as a distant theological promise but as a commission spoken directly to you, in this moment, at the end of this book. You have practiced the yoke. You have sat with the six questions. You have begun — however tentatively, however imperfectly — the daily discipline of activating kindness, compassion, mercy, empathy, altruistic love, and forgiveness before you start your day. And Jesus, speaking across twenty centuries, looks at you and says — you will do greater things than I did.

Not lesser things. Not an approximation of the things. Greater things.

This is not flattery. This is not the empty encouragement of a motivational speaker telling you that you can do anything you set your mind to. This is a teacher who has spent three years demonstrating a practice under every conceivable condition — in the temple and on the hillside, at the dinner table and at the tomb, in the garden and on the cross — and who is now saying to his students, with absolute seriousness, that the practice will go further in their hands than it went in his.

How is that possible? How could anyone do greater things than the man who healed the sick, raised the dead, and forgave his own executioners?

Because the practice multiplies. That is the secret hidden inside the promise.

Jesus was one person, in one body, in one small corner of the Roman Empire, for roughly three years of public ministry. He could only wash so many feet. He could only touch so many lepers. He could only sit at so many wells. He could only be in one place at one time, loving one person at a time, forgiving one person at a time, extending mercy to one person at a time. The body has limits. The practice does not.

The yoke, once taught, once learned, once carried into the world by willing hands, has no such limitation. It multiplies every time someone picks it up. Every time you practice kindness toward the person everyone else overlooks, you are doing what Jesus did. Every time you move toward suffering instead of away from it, you are doing what Jesus did. Every time you put down the sword, cross the boundary, extend love without conditions, or loosen your grip on the coal — you are doing what Jesus did.

And when you do it — when you practice kindness and someone sees it and something shifts in them — and they begin to practice it themselves, and they carry it to someone you will never meet, and that person carries it to someone they will never meet, and the ripple moves outward through families and neighborhoods and cities and generations — then the greater things have begun. Then the yoke has spread beyond anything one person in one body could accomplish. Then the work of Christ is alive, not in a single teacher walking the roads of Palestine, but in every kitchen and office and schoolyard and hospital room and prison visiting area and street corner where someone chooses the practice over the reflex, the verb over the noun, the discipline over the impulse.

That is the greater work. You are the greater work.

Not because you are special. Not because you are more gifted or more spiritual or more worthy than anyone else who might have picked up this book. Because you showed up. Because you were willing. Because you read these stories and sat with them long enough to let them change you. Because you read these stories and let them work on you. Because you asked the six questions and chose one thing. Because you failed and came back. Because you heard the voice on the beach say do you love me and you said yes — imperfect, grieving, uncertain, ashamed of how many times you have denied the practice — and you picked up the yoke again anyway.

That is what it means to become a Christ. Not to achieve perfection. Not to float above the world in serene detachment from human struggle. Not to arrive at some elevated spiritual state where you are no longer touched by anger or grief or confusion or the desire to throw the coal instead of putting it down. To practice. Daily. Imperfectly. Repeatedly. With a willing heart and open hands and the understanding that the yoke is easy because it was designed for human shoulders — your shoulders — and the burden is light because you are not carrying it alone. Every person who has ever practiced these six components walks beside you. Every person who will practice them after you walks behind you. And the teacher who designed the yoke walks ahead, leaving footprints in the dust for you to follow, step by step, all the way home.

In the third chapter of this book, I said that Christ is a verb. I want to return to that now, at the end, because I want you to carry it with you when you close this book and walk back into your life.

You are not being asked to believe anything. You are not being asked to join anything. You are not being asked to declare allegiance to any institution or adopt any creed or resolve any theological question that scholars have been debating for twenty centuries without resolution. You are being asked to do something. To practice something. To wake up tomorrow morning — whatever morning comes after the morning when you finish reading this book — and ask six questions and choose one thing and carry it into the world with you and see what happens when you do.

That is the yoke. That is the verb. That is the heart of Christ, beating in your chest, waiting for you to put it to work.

Jesus said you would do greater things. He said it to fishermen and tax collectors and people who would fail him before the week was over. He did not say it to saints. He said it to practitioners. To people who were willing to pick up the yoke and walk, step by step, furrow by furrow, into a world that desperately needs what they are carrying.

He said it to you.

And he meant it.

Go. Practice. Be the verb. The world is waiting for the greater things.
