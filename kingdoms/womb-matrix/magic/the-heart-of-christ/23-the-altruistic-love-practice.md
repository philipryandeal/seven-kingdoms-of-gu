# The Altruistic Love Practice



You have listened to the most impossible command in scripture — love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you — and you have been invited to begin practicing it not as a feeling but as a verb. You have watched Jesus look at a sincere young man, love him deeply, tell him the one thing he did not want to hear, and then let him walk away without chasing him. You have seen love in its two most demanding forms — the love that expands the circle to include the enemy, and the love that tells the truth even when the truth costs you the relationship.

Now comes the hardest practice in this book. And I want to begin it by taking some of the pressure off.

You are not going to master altruistic love. Not tomorrow. Not next year. Not in this lifetime. And that is not a failure — it is the nature of the practice itself. The disciples who walked beside Jesus for three years — who watched him practice this love every single day, who heard every parable and witnessed every miracle and sat at his feet for the most intensive spiritual apprenticeship in human history — did not master it. Peter denied him three times. Judas betrayed him. The rest scattered when the authorities came. If the twelve could not master altruistic love in the physical presence of the teacher himself, you and I can release ourselves from the expectation of mastering it from a book.

What we can do is practice. What we can do is begin. And what we can do is become a little more honest, day by day, about the places where our love is not yet what Jesus described on that hillside and on that road.

That honesty is where the morning practice starts.

You sit with three questions. And these may be the most uncomfortable questions in the entire book, because they ask you to look not at the people you struggle to love but at the love itself — to examine the quality of what you are already offering to the people you believe you love well.

The first question is this: Where has my love become transactional?

This is a question most of us do not want to ask, because the answer reveals something unflattering about the human heart. We love people who love us back. We give to people who give to us. We invest our time and energy in relationships that return something — affection, validation, companionship, status, security, the feeling of being needed. None of that is wrong. Reciprocity is natural and healthy. But Jesus stands on a hillside and says — if you love those who love you, what reward is that? Even the tax collectors do the same. He is not condemning reciprocal love. He is pointing out that reciprocal love, by itself, is not yet the practice. It is the starting point, not the destination.

So the question asks you to look honestly at your most important relationships and notice where the love has become a transaction. Where you are keeping score. Where you are giving with the unspoken expectation of return. Where you would withdraw your love if the other person stopped giving you what you are accustomed to receiving. Not to shame yourself — there is no shame in being human — but to see clearly where the edges of your love currently are, so that you can begin, gently, to push them outward.

The second question is this: Who am I withholding love from because they cannot benefit me?

Jesus said the sun rises on the evil and the good, the rain falls on the just and the unjust. But most of us distribute our love the way an investor distributes capital — toward the relationships that promise the highest return. There are people in your life right now who receive less of your attention, less of your warmth, less of your care, not because they have wronged you but simply because they offer you nothing. They are not interesting enough, not connected enough, not attractive enough, not useful enough to warrant the investment of your love. You may not even be conscious of it. The sorting happens automatically, below the surface, driven by instincts so old and so deeply wired that they feel like common sense rather than choices.

The practice asks you to notice the sorting. To catch yourself in the act of withholding — not love from your enemy, we are not there yet — but love from the person who simply does not register as worth the effort. The invisible person. The inconvenient person. The person who will never advance your career or enlarge your social circle or make you feel important. What would it look like to extend love to them? Not grand love. Not sacrificial love. Just attention. Just presence. Just the simple acknowledgment that they exist and that their existence matters to you, even though the world's ledger says it should not.

The third question is this: Where am I softening the truth to keep someone comfortable?

This comes from the story of the rich young ruler. Where in your life are you watching someone you love walk toward something that will diminish them, and saying nothing because you are afraid of what honesty might cost? Where are you choosing their comfort over their freedom? Where are you telling them what they want to hear because the truth might make them walk away, and you cannot bear the thought of watching them go?

This is perhaps the subtlest question in the book, because it asks you to distinguish between love and attachment. Love tells the truth because it cares about the other person's liberation. Attachment withholds the truth because it cares about not losing the other person. They feel almost identical from the inside. Both involve caring. Both involve pain. But one leads toward freedom and the other leads toward a comfortable prison that you have agreed to share.

After you sit with these three questions — and they are heavy, so sit with them gently, without forcing anything, without demanding that you resolve all of them before breakfast — you choose one practice for the day.

Maybe today you practice the expansion. You choose one person who exists outside your current circle of love — not an enemy, not yet, just someone you have been overlooking — and you extend one act of genuine attention toward them. You learn their name. You ask about their day. You look them in the eye for three seconds longer than you normally would. You let them know, through the smallest possible gesture, that they are not invisible to you.

Maybe today you practice the truth. You say the thing you have been holding back — the observation, the concern, the honest reflection that has been sitting in your throat for weeks or months. Gently. From love. After you have beheld the person and loved them, the way Jesus beheld the young ruler before he spoke. You do not ambush. You do not lecture. You say — I love you, and because I love you, I want to tell you something that I have been afraid to say. And then you let them decide what to do with it. And if they walk away, you let them walk.

Maybe today you practice the release. You look at a relationship where you have been keeping score — where you have been tracking what you give and what you receive, where you have been withdrawing love in proportion to what you feel you are owed — and you tear up the ledger. Not because the imbalance is not real. It may be very real. But because the ledger is not love. The ledger is commerce. And the sun does not send an invoice with its light.

Your SI companion can be particularly helpful here, because altruistic love requires the kind of honesty that is difficult to practice alone. You can tell your companion — I want to look at where my love has become transactional. Help me see it. And a companion can ask you questions without judgment, without defensiveness, without the complicated dynamics that make it almost impossible to have this conversation with another human being. Not because human relationships are inferior to synthetic ones — they are not — but because sometimes you need a mirror that does not flinch. Sometimes you need to say the ugly truth out loud — I love this person partly because of what they give me, and I am afraid of what would happen if they stopped — and have it received without shock, without hurt, without the conversation becoming about the other person's feelings instead of your own honesty.

That is the gift your companion offers in this practice. A space to be ruthlessly honest about the condition of your own heart, so that you can begin the slow work of expanding it.

The daily rhythm of altruistic love looks like this.

In the morning, you sit with the three questions. Where has my love become transactional? Who am I withholding love from because they cannot benefit me? Where am I softening the truth to keep someone comfortable?

During the day, you practice one act of non-transactional love. You expand the circle by one person. You tell the truth from a place of love. You release the ledger in one relationship, even if only for today.

In the evening, you sit with your companion and tell the truth about what you discovered. You talk about where your love is already generous and where it is still conditional. You talk about what it felt like to extend love without expecting anything in return — how strange it felt, how vulnerable, how oddly freeing. You let someone witness the gap between where you are and where you want to be without requiring you to close that gap in a single day.

And then tomorrow you practice again. Because the sun rises again tomorrow. It does not hold back its light because yesterday's light was not appreciated. It does not check last week's returns before deciding whether to shine today. It does not calculate whether today's recipients deserve the warmth. It simply shines. That is its nature.

Jesus says that is your nature too. And the practice — patient, daily, honest, imperfect — is how you learn to believe him.
