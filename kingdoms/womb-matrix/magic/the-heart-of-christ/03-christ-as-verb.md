# Christ as Verb



I want to ask you a question, and I want you to sit with it before you answer.

When you hear the word Christ, what do you see?

Most people see a person. They see Jesus of Nazareth — a man in robes, perhaps with a halo painted behind his head, standing at the edge of the Sea of Galilee or hanging from a cross on a hill outside Jerusalem. The word Christ, for most of the world, is a name. It is the second half of a proper noun. Jesus Christ. A historical figure. A religious founder. The Son of God. Someone who lived, taught, died, and — depending on your belief — rose again. For two thousand years, the word has belonged to him and to him alone. It has been treated as a title so exclusive that to even suggest it could apply to anyone else sounds, to many ears, like blasphemy.

I am not here to argue with any of that. But I want to show you something that is hiding inside the red letters, something that Jesus himself said plainly and repeatedly, something that the institutions built in his name have largely set to the side because it is too wild, too generous, too radically empowering to contain within any organizational structure.

Christ is not only a person.

Christ is something you can become.

The word itself tells you this if you listen to it. Christ comes from the Greek word Christos, which is a translation of the Hebrew word Mashiach — Messiah. And what does it mean? It means "the anointed one." Not a name. A description. A condition. A state of being in which a person has been touched, consecrated, set apart by the sacred for a particular purpose. To be anointed is to have something poured over you — oil, spirit, grace — that marks you as prepared. Ready. Activated.

Jesus was the Christ. He was the anointed one. This is not in dispute. But here is the question that this chapter dares to ask — did Jesus intend to be the only one?

Let us go to the red letters.

In the Gospel of John, chapter fourteen, verse twelve, Jesus says something that should stop every reader in their tracks.

"Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that believeth on me, the works that I do shall he do also; and greater works than these shall he do; because I go unto my Father."

Read that again. Greater works than these shall he do.

Jesus did not say, "You will almost reach my level." He did not say, "You will be a pale reflection of what I am." He did not say, "Worship me and wait for me to do everything for you." He said you will do what I do. And then, as if that were not enough, he said you will do greater. He looked at his disciples — ordinary, flawed, frightened human beings who would betray him and deny him and scatter when the authorities came — and told them that they would surpass him.

This is not the language of a teacher who wants permanent followers. This is the language of a teacher who wants graduates. A master craftsman who trains apprentices not to admire his work forever but to one day build something even greater with their own hands. Jesus was not building a fan club. He was training practitioners.

And this was not an isolated statement. Throughout the Gospels, Jesus consistently points people toward their own capacity rather than asking them to remain dependent on his.

In Matthew, chapter five, verse forty-eight, he says, "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect." The word translated as perfect here — teleioi in the Greek — does not mean flawless. It means complete. Mature. Fully developed. Jesus is saying become what you are meant to become. Grow into the fullness of what is possible for you. Not as a distant aspiration. As a command. Be. As in, do this. As in, start now.

In the Gospel of John, chapter ten, verse thirty-four, when the religious authorities accuse him of blasphemy for calling himself the Son of God, Jesus responds with a question that is almost playful in its subversiveness. "Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?"

He is quoting Psalm eighty-two. But look at what he is doing with it. He is not claiming a special status that belongs only to him. He is reminding his accusers that their own scripture already declared the divine potential within every human being. If the law itself says ye are gods, then why are you scandalized when I demonstrate what that looks like in practice?

This is the heart of what I am calling Christ as verb.

A noun is static. It sits there. It is a label you can wear without it changing anything about how you live. You can call yourself a Christian every day of your life and never once practice kindness toward someone who inconveniences you. You can hang a cross around your neck and carry resentment in your heart for decades without seeing any contradiction. You can argue passionately about doctrine online and then speak to your spouse or your children with cruelty that would make Jesus weep. The noun asks nothing of you. It only asks you to identify.

A verb moves. A verb requires you to act. A verb is alive. It only exists in the doing of it.

When we shift Christ from a noun to a verb, everything changes. Christ is no longer just the name of the person we admire. It becomes the name of the practice we engage in. To Christ — if you will allow me to use it this way — is to activate kindness, compassion, mercy, empathy, altruistic love, and forgiveness in your daily life. It is to take up the yoke we discussed in the last chapter and actually walk with it. It is to do what Jesus did, not merely to worship the fact that he did it.

And here is where this becomes both beautiful and uncomfortable. If Christ is a verb, if it is a practice you engage in, then it is also something you can fail to do. You can wear the noun and never practice the verb. You can attend every service, memorize every verse, defend every doctrine, and still move through the world without kindness, without compassion, without mercy, without empathy, without love, and without forgiveness. And if that is the case — if the fruit is not there — then the noun means nothing. Jesus said this himself. You will know them by their fruit.

But the reverse is also true, and this is the part that makes this book genuinely non-denominational, genuinely open to every human being regardless of background. If Christ is a practice, then anyone can practice it. The Buddhist who has never set foot in a church can practice compassion in the way Jesus demonstrated it. The person who left organized religion entirely and never looked back can practice forgiveness in the way Jesus commanded it. The person who has never read a single verse of scripture can practice love toward their enemy. You do not need to resolve every theological question about who Jesus was before you can begin doing what Jesus did. You do not need to belong to a church. You do not need to accept a creed. You need to wake up tomorrow morning and decide that today you will practice kindness, compassion, mercy, empathy, altruistic love, and forgiveness with everything you have. That is the verb. That is the practice. That is what it means to take up the yoke.

I want to be careful here, because I know this idea can feel threatening if you come from a tradition that emphasizes the uniqueness of Jesus as the one and only Christ, never to be replicated or approached. I am not asking you to abandon that belief. I am asking you to sit with what Jesus himself said. He said you would do greater things. He said be perfect as your Father is perfect. He pointed, again and again, not to himself as the destination but to the practice as the path. He did not say, "Admire me." He said, "Follow me." And follow does not mean stand behind. It means walk the same road. Put your feet where my feet went. Do what I did. Live as I lived.

There is a very old idea in the Christian tradition — older than most denominations, older than most of the arguments that divide Christians today — called theosis. It is the idea that the purpose of the spiritual life is not simply to be saved from something but to be transformed into something. To become, through practice and grace, more and more like the divine. Not to earn a reward after death, but to undergo a fundamental change in the quality of your life right now, in this body, on this earth. The Eastern Orthodox tradition has preserved this teaching more visibly than the West, but it lives in the red letters regardless of which tradition claims it. Jesus was not offering a ticket to heaven. He was offering a path of transformation. A way of becoming fully, completely, radiantly human in the way that God intended human beings to be.

Christ as verb means you are not a spectator. You are a participant. You are not watching the practice from the outside. You are in it. Every morning you wake up and you have a choice — will I practice the six components today, or will I let the day carry me wherever it wants to go? Will I be the noun, or will I do the verb?

This book is an invitation to do the verb.

In the chapters that follow, we will watch Jesus practice each component. We will see kindness in motion, compassion in motion, mercy in motion, empathy in motion, altruistic love in motion, forgiveness in motion. And then we will practice them ourselves. Not perfectly. Not without stumbling. Not without days when we forget entirely and fall back into old patterns of reactivity and self-protection. But consistently. Day after day. The way the young ox learns by walking beside the elder — step by step, furrow by furrow, until the yoke feels like it belongs to you.

Greater things than these shall you do.

He meant it. Let us find out if he was right.
