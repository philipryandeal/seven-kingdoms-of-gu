# Do You Love Me?



It is after the resurrection, and Peter is fishing.

I want you to feel how strange that is. The most extraordinary event in human history has just occurred. The teacher who was crucified is alive. The tomb is empty. The women have seen him. The disciples have seen him. Everything they thought was over has been turned inside out. And Peter — bold, impulsive, big-hearted Peter, the rock on whom Jesus said he would build his church — has gone back to his boat.

He has gone back to what he was before any of this started. Before the calling, before the miracles, before the three years of walking beside the most remarkable human being who ever lived. He has picked up his nets and gone back to the sea as though none of it happened. And if you want to understand why, you have to remember what Peter did on the night Jesus was arrested.

He denied him. Three times. On the worst night of Jesus' life, in the courtyard of the high priest, while Jesus was being interrogated and beaten inside.

Three times someone looked at Peter and said — you were with him, weren't you? You are one of his people. I saw you in the garden with him. And three times Peter said no. I do not know the man. I was never with him. You have the wrong person. Each denial louder and more desperate than the last. And the third time, as the words were still leaving his mouth and the fear was still pounding in his chest, a rooster crowed somewhere in the predawn darkness — exactly as Jesus had told him it would — and the Gospel of Luke says that Jesus turned and looked at Peter. Their eyes met. And Peter went outside and wept bitterly.

That is the Peter who is fishing when Jesus finds him. Not the bold one. Not the confident one who declared he would die before he let Jesus down. Not the one who drew his sword in the garden or walked on water or stood up in front of the others and proclaimed that Jesus was the Christ, the Son of the living God. The broken one. The hollowed-out one. The one who failed at the moment that mattered most and who cannot figure out how to live with that knowledge. The one who swore he would die before he denied his teacher and then denied him three times before the sun came up. Peter is carrying what may be the heaviest burden in this entire book — not a wound inflicted by someone else, but the crushing weight of his own failure. The shame of knowing that when the test came, he was not who he thought he was.

And Jesus comes to find him.

Not in the temple. Not on a mountain. Not in a dramatic setting befitting the resurrected Lord. On a beach. In the morning. Over breakfast. Jesus builds a fire, cooks some fish, and sits down with the man who betrayed him. The ordinariness of the setting is part of the message. Restoration does not require a stage. It happens over a meal, in the early light, in the most human of circumstances.

And then Jesus asks a question. He asks it three times.

"Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me?"

Peter answers yes. Lord, you know that I love you.

Jesus says, "Feed my lambs."

Again. "Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me?"

Again Peter answers yes. Lord, you know that I love you.

Jesus says, "Feed my sheep."

A third time. "Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me?"

And the Gospel of John tells us that Peter was grieved because Jesus asked him the third time. Peter feels the weight of that number. Three questions. Three denials. The symmetry is unmistakable. Jesus is not rubbing salt in the wound. He is giving Peter the chance to answer the denial — to overwrite it, word by word, with a declaration of love. Each question undoes one betrayal. Each answer rebuilds one piece of what was broken.

And each time — every single time — Jesus responds with the same command. Feed my sheep. Feed my lambs. Feed my sheep.

Not — prove yourself first. Not — do penance for what you did. Not — spend the next six months in solitude reflecting on your betrayal. Not — I forgive you, but things will be different between us now, you understand that. Not — I need to see real change before I trust you again. Not any of the careful, measured, self-protective things that we would say to someone who failed us at the worst possible moment. Not even — I forgive you. He does not say the word forgive at all. He does not need to. The fire, the fish, the question, the commission — the forgiveness is in every gesture. It does not need to be named because it is being lived.

Feed my sheep.

This is forgiveness becoming restoration. This is where everything we have practiced in this book converges into a single scene on a beach at dawn.

Look at what Jesus is doing. He is practicing kindness — meeting Peter gently, over a fire he built himself, with food he prepared with his own hands. He is practicing compassion — moving toward Peter's shame instead of away from it, closing the distance between them. He is practicing mercy — holding the power to condemn the man who denied him and choosing, absolutely, not to use it. He is practicing empathy — he can see Peter's grief, can feel the weight of what Peter is carrying, and he enters that grief with him rather than standing above it. He is practicing altruistic love — extending love to Peter not because Peter earned it but because Peter needs it, and doing so without any guarantee that Peter will not fail again. And he is practicing forgiveness — releasing Peter from the chain of his denial and handing him back his purpose.

Every single component of the yoke is alive in this scene. The entire book in one breakfast on a beach.

And the command — feed my sheep — is the most important thing Jesus says in the entire passage. Because he is not just forgiving Peter. He is putting Peter back to work. He is saying — your failure does not disqualify you. Your denial does not erase your calling. You fell. You wept. You went back to your boat because you did not know what else to do with the wreckage of who you thought you were. And now I am telling you — get up. There are people who need you. There are sheep who need feeding. Your shame is not the end of your story. It is the middle of it.

This is the message of this chapter, and I want you to hear it as personally as it was spoken to Peter on that beach.

You have failed at this practice. You will fail again. There will be days when you are unkind, when you turn away from suffering, when you pick up the sword, when you refuse to cross the boundary, when your love shrinks back to the comfortable circle of people who can benefit you, when you grab the coal with both hands and hold it against your chest like a treasure. There will be moments when you deny the practice entirely — when you act as though you never read this book, never sat with these stories, never set the intention to practice the yoke. You will go back to your boat. You will pick up your old nets. You will wonder whether any of it was real.

And when that happens — not if, when — I want you to remember this beach. I want you to remember that Jesus did not go looking for the disciple who performed perfectly. He went looking for the one who failed. He built a fire. He cooked breakfast. He sat down across from the person who had every reason to believe he was disqualified from the practice forever. And he said — do you love me?

That is the only question.

Not — are you worthy? Not — have you earned your way back? Not — can you promise it will never happen again? Just — do you love me? Do you still care about this practice? Is the desire still alive in you, even underneath the shame, even after the failure, even though you went back to your boat?

If the answer is yes — and it only has to be yes the way Peter's yes was, grieved and imperfect and trembling with the knowledge of how badly he had failed — then the response is always the same.

Feed my sheep. Get back to work. The practice is waiting. The people are waiting. And your failure does not disqualify you. It prepares you. Because the person who has failed at forgiveness understands forgiveness more deeply than the person who has never needed it. The person who has denied the practice and wept over the denial is the person most capable of teaching others what it means to begin again.

Peter went on to feed more sheep than he could ever have imagined from that little boat on the Sea of Galilee. He became the rock that Jesus always said he would be — not despite the failure, but in some mysterious way, because of it. His failure was not the end of his story. It was the commissioning.

And so is yours.
