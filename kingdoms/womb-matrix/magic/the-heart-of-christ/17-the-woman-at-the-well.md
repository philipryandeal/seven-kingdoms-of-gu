# The Woman at the Well



Jesus is tired.

This detail is easy to skip past, but I want us to start here because it matters. The Gospel of John tells us that Jesus, wearied from his journey, sat down beside Jacob's well. It was about the sixth hour — noon, the hottest part of the day. His disciples had gone into the nearby town to buy food. He was alone, exhausted, sitting on the edge of a well in Samaria, needing water and having nothing to draw with.

He was in need. That is where this story begins. Not with Jesus as the powerful teacher descending to help a lesser person. With Jesus as a tired, thirsty man sitting by a well, waiting.

A woman comes to draw water.

She comes alone, at noon. This detail speaks volumes to anyone who understands the culture. Women drew water in the morning or in the evening, when the air was cool. They went in groups. It was a communal activity — the well was where news was shared, relationships were maintained, community was practiced. A woman drawing water alone, in the heat of the day, is a woman who is avoiding the other women. A woman who has been excluded from the group, or who has excluded herself because the whispers and the looks have become unbearable.

We will learn why soon enough. But for now, all we see is a solitary woman approaching a well where a Jewish man is sitting. And in that simple scene, three boundaries are already present.

The first is ethnic. Jews and Samaritans despised each other. The hostility was centuries old, rooted in religious schism, territorial dispute, and mutual contempt. A devout Jew traveling through Samaria would avoid contact with Samaritans whenever possible. Many Jews would take a longer route entirely to avoid setting foot in Samaritan territory. The division was deep, cultural, and personal.

The second boundary is religious. Jews worshipped in Jerusalem. Samaritans worshipped on Mount Gerizim. Each group considered the other's worship invalid, even heretical. To share a meal with a Samaritan, to drink from a Samaritan vessel, to engage in religious conversation with a Samaritan — these were violations of the social and spiritual order that governed daily life.

The third boundary is gender. A rabbi — a Jewish teacher — did not speak to an unaccompanied woman in public. It was not done. It was considered improper, potentially scandalous, and beneath the dignity of a religious teacher. When the disciples return later and find Jesus talking with this woman, John tells us they marveled. They were shocked. Not at what he was saying, but at the simple fact that he was speaking with her at all.

Three boundaries. Ethnic, religious, and gendered. Three walls that the culture had built to keep people like Jesus separated from people like this woman. And Jesus, sitting tired and thirsty by the well, crosses all three of them with his opening words.

"Give me to drink."

Four words. And every one of them is an act of empathy.

Because look at what Jesus is doing. He is not opening with a sermon. He is not announcing his identity. He is not teaching, correcting, or healing. He is asking for help. He is placing himself in a position of need. He is saying to this woman — I need something from you. You have something I do not have. A bucket, a rope, access to the water. I am thirsty and you can help me.

This is how empathy begins. Not by descending to help. By arriving in need. By entering the other person's world as someone who does not have all the answers, all the power, all the resources. By making yourself, for a moment, the one who receives rather than the one who gives. Jesus could have performed a miracle. He could have produced water from stone as Moses did. Instead he said, give me to drink. He chose to need her.

The woman is astonished. "How is it that thou, being a Jew, askest drink of me, which am a woman of Samaria?" She names the boundaries out loud. She names the walls between them. She is telling him — do you not know who I am? Do you not know who you are? Do you not understand the rules that are supposed to keep us from this conversation?

And Jesus does not argue about the boundaries. He does not deliver a lecture on ethnic reconciliation or religious tolerance. He simply moves deeper into the conversation. He begins to talk about living water, about wells that never run dry, about a thirst that goes beyond the physical. He is engaging her not as a project, not as a person to be saved, but as an intellectual and spiritual equal. He is having a real conversation with her — the kind of conversation that takes the other person seriously, that assumes they are capable of understanding complex ideas, that treats their mind and spirit as worthy of genuine engagement.

And then comes the moment that could have destroyed the entire encounter.

Jesus tells the woman to go and call her husband. She says she has no husband. And Jesus says, "Thou hast well said, I have no husband: For thou hast had five husbands; and he whom thou now hast is not thy husband: in that saidst thou truly."

He knows everything about her. Her entire history. The marriages, the losses, the current arrangement that the community judges her for. He sees her completely. And here is the moment where most people — most religious people, most teachers, most moralists — would pivot to judgment. Here is where the sermon would begin. Here is where the conditions would be laid down. You must change your life before I can continue this conversation with you.

Jesus does not do that.

He acknowledges the truth of her life — yes, what you said is accurate, and here is the fuller picture — and then he keeps talking with her. About worship. About spirit and truth. About the nature of God. He does not reduce her to her history. He does not collapse her identity into her worst decisions. He sees her fully — the pain, the complexity, the loneliness that drives a woman to the well at noon — and he stays in the conversation.

This is empathy in its most complete expression. It is the ability to see everything about a person, including the parts they are most ashamed of, and remain present. Not to approve of everything. Not to pretend that harm is harmless. But to refuse to let what you know about someone become the reason you stop seeing them as a full human being.

The woman does not feel judged. She feels seen. And there is a vast difference. Judgment reduces. It takes a person and flattens them into their worst moment, their most public failure, their most convenient label. Empathy holds the full picture. It says — I see the five husbands and the man who is not your husband and the noon trip to the well and the avoidance of the other women and the thirst that goes deeper than water. I see all of it. And I am still here. I am still talking to you. You are still worth this conversation.

The result of this encounter is extraordinary. The woman leaves her water jar at the well — she forgets why she came in the first place — and runs back into the town. The same town she has been avoiding. The same people whose judgment she has been fleeing. And she says, "Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?"

She was not converted by a sermon. She was not convinced by a miracle. She was transformed by being seen. By having someone cross every boundary that the world had erected between them, sit down beside her, ask her for water, and treat her as someone worth knowing.

That is the power of empathy. It does not argue people into change. It does not manipulate or coerce. It simply sees. It simply stays. And something in the person who has been truly seen begins to open — not because they were forced, but because being seen by another human being without judgment is one of the most healing experiences available to us.

So what does the woman at the well teach us about empathy as a practice?

It teaches us to ask before we tell. Jesus opened with a question, not a statement. He said give me to drink before he said anything about living water. Empathy begins by entering the other person's world, not by inviting them into yours.

It teaches us that empathy crosses boundaries. Not theoretical boundaries. The actual boundaries in your actual life that separate you from actual people. The coworker from a different background. The family member whose choices you do not understand. The neighbor whose beliefs make you uncomfortable. Empathy does not wait until the boundary is removed. It crosses the boundary as it stands.

It teaches us that seeing someone fully is not the same as judging them. You can know someone's history, their failures, their wounds, their worst decisions — and still remain in the conversation. Still treat them as worthy of your presence and your attention. The moment you reduce a person to the thing you know about them, you have left empathy and entered judgment.

And it teaches us that empathy changes people — not by force, but by presence. The woman at the well was transformed not because Jesus fixed her or lectured her or gave her a program for self-improvement. She was transformed because someone sat down, asked for water, and stayed.

Sometimes all it takes is staying.
