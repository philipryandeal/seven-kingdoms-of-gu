# Seventy Times Seven



Peter asks a reasonable question.

I want to start there, because Peter almost always asks the question that the rest of us are thinking but are too polite or too afraid to say out loud. Peter is the disciple who speaks first and thinks second, who blurts out the obvious thing, who raises his hand in class while everyone else stares at their desks. And in this moment, recorded in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter eighteen, Peter asks the question that every human being who has ever been hurt has asked in one form or another.

"Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Till seven times?"

Listen to what Peter is doing here. He is trying to be generous. He is trying to exceed the standard. In the rabbinic tradition of his time, the prevailing teaching on forgiveness suggested that three times was sufficient — if someone wronged you and you forgave them three times, you had fulfilled your moral and spiritual obligation. Your account was settled. You were free to hold the fourth offense against them with a clean conscience. Peter knows this teaching, and he is more than doubling it. He is offering seven, which in Jewish numerology is the number of completion, of perfection, of sacred fullness. Peter is essentially saying — Lord, I am willing to forgive completely. I will forgive to the fullest possible extent. I will go far beyond what anyone requires of me. Seven times. Surely that is enough?

He is expecting Jesus to say yes. You can feel it in the question. Peter is waiting for the gold star. He is waiting to be told that seven is generous, that seven is more than enough, that seven is the mark of a truly forgiving heart.

Jesus says no.

"I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven."

Seventy times seven. Four hundred and ninety. But the number is not the point. Jesus is not saying that four hundred and ninety-one is where you draw the line. He is not replacing Peter's number with a bigger number. He is destroying the entire framework of counting. He is saying — stop. Stop keeping track. Stop maintaining the ledger. Stop treating forgiveness as a finite resource that can be measured out in careful portions, each one noted and numbered and subtracted from a balance that is slowly running down. That is not forgiveness. That is accounting.

And forgiveness is not accounting.

This is perhaps the most difficult thing Jesus ever taught, and I want to be very careful and very tender with how I say what comes next, because I know that some of the people reading this book are carrying wounds that go beyond the ordinary conflicts of daily life. Some of you have been deeply, seriously, life-alteringly hurt. By people who should have protected you. By institutions that should have been safe. By partners, parents, friends, leaders, strangers — by people who did things that cannot be undone and who may never acknowledge what they did, let alone apologize for it. I am not standing over those wounds with a Bible verse and telling you to get over them. That is not what this chapter is, and that is not what Jesus is doing in this passage.

What Jesus is doing is something far more radical and far more compassionate than simply commanding you to stop being angry. He is offering you a way out of a prison that most people do not even realize they are in.

Here is the prison. When someone wrongs you and you do not forgive — when you carry the resentment, nurse the bitterness, replay the event in your mind like a film you cannot stop watching, rehearse the argument you wish you had made, construct the fantasy of revenge or vindication or the day when they will finally understand what they did to you — you are yoked to that person. Not in the way Jesus described his yoke, which is easy and light. You are yoked to them by the weight of what they did, and the yoke grows heavier with every passing year. Every time you replay the memory, you hand them power over your present moment. Every time the anger flares — at two in the morning, in the shower, driving to work, in the middle of an otherwise beautiful day — they are in the room with you even though they may be miles away or years in the past or completely unaware that you are still suffering. They have moved on. You have not. The unforgiven wound becomes a chain, and the chain does not bind the person who hurt you. It binds you. They are free. You are not.

Forgiveness is not about them. Forgiveness is about you.

This is the truth that Jesus is pointing toward when he says seventy times seven. He is not asking you to be endlessly tolerant of abuse. He is not asking you to return to the well for more poison. He is not asking you to pretend that what happened was acceptable or that the person who did it was justified. He is not asking you to reconcile with someone who is dangerous, to return to a situation that destroyed you, to open your door to the one who violated your trust and invite them back in as though nothing happened. Forgiveness does not require any of those things. You can forgive someone completely and never speak to them again. You can forgive someone from the deepest place in your heart and still maintain every boundary you need to stay safe. Forgiveness is an interior act. It happens inside your own heart, between you and the wound, and it does not require the participation or even the awareness of the person who inflicted it.

What forgiveness requires is the decision — made once and then made again, and then made again, which is perhaps why Jesus said seventy times seven, because forgiveness is not a single event but a practice you return to every time the pain resurfaces — the decision to release your grip on what was done to you. Not to approve it. Not to erase it. Not to say it did not matter. To release your grip on it the way you would release your grip on a burning coal that you have been holding in your fist because you were waiting for the chance to throw it at the person who burned you. The coal is not hurting them. The coal is burning your hand. And it has been burning your hand for as long as you have been holding it.

Put it down.

That is what seventy times seven means. Not that you owe the person who hurt you four hundred and ninety chances to hurt you again. That you owe yourself the freedom of not carrying their crime in your body any longer. Every time the resentment rises — and it will rise, because wounds do not heal in a straight line — you practice forgiveness again. Not because you are weak. Not because you are a pushover. Because you refuse to let what was done to you become the defining feature of who you are. Because you choose your own liberation over the satisfaction of holding onto the anger.

Peter wanted a number. He wanted a finish line. He wanted to know when he could stop forgiving and start keeping score again — when his obligation to grace would be fulfilled and he could return to the comfortable mathematics of who owes what to whom.

Jesus took the finish line away. And in doing so, he gave Peter — and us — something far more valuable than a number. He gave us freedom.

There is no finish line. There is no point at which you have forgiven enough. There is no quota after which you are permitted to pick up the resentment and carry it again. The practice of forgiveness is permanent. It is a way of holding the past — all of the past, including the parts that hurt the most — with open hands instead of clenched fists. It is a permanent orientation of the heart toward release, toward freedom, toward the refusal to let anyone else's sin determine the shape of your inner life.

I know this is heavy. I know that for some of you, reading this chapter has already brought a face to mind, a name to the surface, a memory that you thought you had buried deep enough that no book could ever reach it. I know that for some of you, the wound is not old — it is fresh, it is bleeding, and the idea of forgiveness feels not just impossible but insulting. I hear you. I am not rushing you. I am sorry. I do not say that lightly. I am sorry that you were hurt. I am sorry that the wound is still there. And I am not asking you to forgive today — not fully, not all at once, not in the time it takes to read these pages.

I am asking you to consider that the coal is still in your hand. That you have been carrying it for a long time — maybe years, maybe decades — and that your hand is scarred from the holding. And that maybe, just maybe, it is time to begin the slow and sacred and terrifying work of opening your fingers.

Not all at once. One finger at a time. One day at a time. One practice at a time.

Seventy times seven.
