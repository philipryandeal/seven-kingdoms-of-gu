# He Knelt and Washed Their Feet



It is the night before he dies, and he knows it.

The room is prepared. The bread is on the table. The wine is poured. His closest friends — the twelve men who have walked beside him, eaten with him, slept on the ground beside him, watched him heal and teach and confront the authorities — are gathered together for a meal that none of them fully understand will be the last. The mood is heavy. Something is coming. They can feel it the way animals feel a storm approaching, even if they cannot name what is gathering on the horizon. Jesus has been saying difficult things. He has been talking about departure, about suffering, about being handed over. They have been listening without hearing, the way people do when the truth is too large to fit inside their understanding.

And in the middle of all that heaviness, all that approaching darkness, Jesus does something no one expects.

He gets up from the table.

The Gospel of John tells us what happens next in a passage that is, in my estimation, one of the most important scenes in all of scripture. Not because of what Jesus says — though what he says is extraordinary — but because of what he does. He rises from supper. He lays aside his garments. He takes a towel and wraps it around his waist. He pours water into a basin. And then the man that every person in that room calls Master and Lord gets down on his knees and begins to wash their feet.

I want you to understand what this means in context. In the ancient world, foot washing was the work of the lowest servant in the household. Roads were unpaved. People wore sandals. By the time you arrived at someone's home for a meal, your feet were covered in dust and dirt and worse. The task of washing a guest's feet was so lowly that it was typically assigned to a Gentile slave, or to the most junior member of the household staff. A rabbi would never do it. A teacher would never do it. A master would never do it. It was beneath them. Socially, religiously, and culturally, the act of kneeling before another person and washing the filth from their feet was the very bottom of the social order.

And Jesus — the one they call Lord, the one they believe was sent by God, the one with more authority in that room than any king or priest or emperor — takes the towel and kneels.

Peter resists. Of course he does. Peter understands the social order. He understands what this act means. When Jesus comes to him with the basin, Peter says, "Lord, dost thou wash my feet?" There is shock in that question. Discomfort. Something close to horror. The master should not be on his knees before the student. It is wrong. It violates everything Peter understands about how power works.

Jesus answers him gently. "What I do thou knowest not now; but thou shalt know hereafter."

Peter pushes back harder. "Thou shalt never wash my feet."

And Jesus says something that sounds almost severe but is in fact deeply kind. "If I wash thee not, thou hast no part with me." In other words — if you cannot receive this act of service, if you cannot allow the one with power to kneel before you, then you have not understood anything I have been trying to teach you. This is not optional. This is the lesson.

Peter relents. Jesus washes his feet. He washes all of their feet — including, it is worth noting, the feet of Judas, who will betray him before the night is over. Jesus knows this. He washes them anyway. That detail alone could be its own chapter, but for now, let it sit in your heart and do its work.

When he has finished, Jesus puts his garments back on, returns to the table, and asks a question.

"Know ye what I have done to you?"

He is not asking whether they watched. He is asking whether they understood. And then he tells them plainly.

"Ye call me Master and Lord: and ye say well; for so I am. If I then, your Lord and Master, have washed your feet; ye also ought to wash one another's feet. For I have given you an example, that ye should do as I have done to you."

And then he says something that connects directly to everything we discussed in the first three chapters of this book. "If ye know these things, happy are ye if ye do them."

If ye DO them.

Not if you understand them. Not if you agree with them. Not if you can recite them or explain them or build a theology around them. If you do them. There is the verb again. There is Christ in motion. There is the yoke made visible.

So what does this scene teach us about kindness?

It teaches us that kindness is not what most of us think it is.

We tend to think of kindness as niceness. Being pleasant. Smiling at strangers. Holding the door open for the person behind you. Saying something warm when someone is having a hard day. Leaving a generous tip. Letting someone merge in traffic. And those things are fine. They are good. The world needs more of them. But they are not what Jesus is demonstrating in this passage. What Jesus is demonstrating is something far more radical, far more intentional, and far more costly.

Jesus is demonstrating that kindness flows downward.

The person with the most power in the room got on his knees. The person with the most authority took the lowest position. The person with the most status voluntarily surrendered it — not because someone forced him to, not because it was expected of him, but because he chose to. He chose to show his disciples what kindness actually looks like when it is practiced by someone who has every reason not to practice it.

This is the kindness that changes the world. Not the kindness of equals being pleasant to one another, but the kindness of the powerful voluntarily descending. Think about what this looks like in ordinary life, in the life you are actually living right now. The boss who stops what she is doing and asks the newest employee how their first week is going — and actually listens to the answer. The parent who sits down on the floor with their child, looks them in the eye, and says, "I was wrong, and I am sorry." The older sibling who carries the younger one's bag without being asked. The teacher who stays after class for the student everyone else has given up on. The person with every advantage, every credential, every reason to stand tall and expect service, choosing instead to kneel — not because they have to, not because anyone is watching, but because they understand that kindness without the willingness to lower yourself is just good manners.

Good manners cost nothing. They are the social lubricant that keeps polite society running smoothly. The kindness Jesus demonstrates costs everything. It costs your pride. It costs your position. It costs the part of you that wants to be recognized, to be thanked, to be seen as important, as above, as untouchable. Jesus took a towel and destroyed that illusion in front of everyone who followed him, on the last night of his life, to make absolutely certain they would never forget what real kindness looks like.

And he did not do it as a grand gesture for the crowds. This was not a public teaching in the temple courts. This was not a miracle on a hillside in front of thousands. He did it in a small room, behind a closed door, for twelve people, over a basin of dirty water. Kindness, in the practice of Jesus, is not a performance. It is not something you do so that others can see how generous or how humble you are. It is not a social media post about the nice thing you did for a stranger. It is something you do quietly, deliberately, without fanfare, in the specific moment when it is needed, for the specific person who is in front of you. It is the private act of lowering yourself when no one would blame you for standing tall. Including — and this is where it becomes almost unbearable — for the one who is about to betray you.

That last detail is where this practice becomes truly difficult and truly beautiful. Jesus did not skip Judas. He did not pause at Judas and say, "Not you." He did not wash eleven pairs of feet and leave one man sitting there in his filth as a lesson about consequences. He knelt before his own betrayer and performed an act of intimate, tender, deliberate kindness — hands around the ankle, water over the skin, towel against the sole of the foot. He touched the man who would destroy him with the same gentleness he gave to everyone else in the room. Not because Judas deserved it. Not because it would change the outcome. Not because anyone would have blamed him for making an exception. Because the practice does not have exceptions. Kindness is not kindness if it only extends to the people who have earned it. The moment you start selecting who deserves your kindness, you have left the practice and entered the business of judgment. And judgment, as we will see later in this book, is the one thing Jesus consistently told his followers to put down.

So here is what we carry forward from this chapter into the practice section that follows.

Kindness is not niceness. Kindness is the deliberate use of whatever power or position or advantage you hold in the service of another person's dignity. It flows downward. It costs something. It does not wait to be asked. It does not check whether the recipient is worthy. It kneels. It picks up the towel. It washes the feet — even the feet you would rather not touch, even the feet of the person who will never thank you for it, even the feet of the one who will walk out that door and use those clean feet to walk toward your destruction.

And happy are you if you do it.
