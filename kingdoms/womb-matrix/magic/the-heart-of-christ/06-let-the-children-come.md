# Let the Children Come



There is a moment in the Gospel of Mark that is easy to rush past. It takes up only four verses. It does not involve a miracle, a dramatic confrontation with the authorities, or a sweeping teaching that reshapes the moral landscape. On the surface, it seems like a small, tender scene — Jesus being nice to children. You have probably seen it painted on church nursery walls, rendered in soft pastels with lambs and flowers and blue-eyed toddlers, used on greeting cards and children's Bibles and Sunday school felt boards for generations. It looks gentle. It looks sweet. It looks harmless.

It is none of those things.

Here is what happens. People begin bringing their young children to Jesus so that he might touch them. The word touch here carries weight — in that culture, the touch of a holy person was understood as a form of blessing, a transmission of something sacred from one body to another. These parents are bringing their little ones to be blessed. It is an act of faith and an act of love.

And the disciples stop them.

Mark tells us that the disciples rebuked those who brought the children. Rebuked is a strong word. It does not mean they politely suggested that the teacher was busy. It means they scolded. They pushed back. They positioned themselves between the people with children and Jesus, and they said — in essence — not now. He does not have time for this. He has important work to do. These are just children. Come back later. Or better yet, do not come back at all.

The disciples have become gatekeepers.

This is what happens around power, and it is worth pausing here because this pattern is so universal that you will recognize it immediately in your own life. It happens so naturally that the people doing it rarely recognize it. Someone becomes important — a teacher, a leader, a healer, a public figure, a pastor, a CEO, a social media personality — and almost immediately, a circle forms around them. An inner ring of people who control access. They decide who gets in and who does not. They decide which requests are worthy and which are a waste of time. They develop a hierarchy of importance, and the people at the bottom of that hierarchy — the least powerful, the least connected, the least impressive, the least likely to advance the agenda — get turned away. It happens in churches. It happens in companies. It happens in families. It happens in friend groups. Anywhere power concentrates, gatekeepers appear.

The disciples are not villains in this story. They are doing what any inner circle does. They are protecting their teacher's time and energy. They are managing the demands of a growing movement. From their perspective, they are being helpful. Practical. Efficient. Children are small. Children are unimportant. Children cannot contribute to the mission. There are Pharisees to debate, crowds to teach, demons to cast out, a kingdom to announce. Surely the teacher has more pressing concerns than laying his hands on toddlers.

And then Mark gives us one of the most revealing details in any Gospel narrative. He tells us that when Jesus saw what the disciples were doing, he was much displeased.

Much displeased. Some translations say indignant. This is not mild irritation. This is not a gentle correction delivered with a patient smile. Jesus is angry. Not at the children. Not at the parents. At his own disciples. At the gatekeepers. At the people closest to him who have decided, on his behalf, who is worthy of his attention.

And then come the red letters.

"Suffer the little children to come unto me, and forbid them not: for of such is the kingdom of God. Verily I say unto you, Whosoever shall not receive the kingdom of God as a little child, he shall not enter therein."

There are two things happening in this statement, and both of them are about kindness.

The first is the command itself. Let them come. Do not stop them. Do not stand between me and the people who want to reach me, no matter how small, how unimportant, how inconvenient their presence may seem to you. The word suffer here, in the language of the King James translators, means allow. Permit. Make room. Step aside and let them through.

This is kindness as accessibility. It is the deliberate refusal to let anyone — even your most trusted associates, even the people who love you and want to protect you — decide who is worthy of receiving what you have to give. Jesus is not just being nice to kids. He is dismantling the entire gatekeeping apparatus that has grown up around him. He is saying that the moment you start sorting people into categories of deserving and undeserving, important and unimportant, worthy of my time and not worthy of my time, you have misunderstood everything I have been teaching you.

The second thing happening in this passage is even more radical. Jesus does not simply say let the children come. He says the kingdom of God belongs to people like them. Of such is the kingdom. And then he goes further — whoever does not receive the kingdom as a little child will not enter it at all.

What does a child have that an adult does not? This is the question the passage is quietly, relentlessly asking. And the answer is not innocence, though that is what most sermons will tell you. Children in the ancient world were not sentimentalized the way they are in modern Western culture. They were not seen as pure little angels radiating goodness. They were seen as incomplete. Dependent. Without status, without power, without credentials, without any claim to importance or authority. A child in first-century Palestine had nothing to offer a rabbi. No money, no influence, no political connections, no resume of accomplishments, no theological education, no social capital of any kind. A child could only receive. A child could only show up, empty-handed, and hope to be welcomed.

And that is the point.

The kingdom belongs to those who come with nothing. Who show up with empty hands and no credentials. Who do not try to earn their way in or impress their way in or argue their way in. Who simply come, because they were invited, and trust that the invitation is enough.

The gatekeepers had it exactly backward. They thought they were protecting something valuable by keeping the unimportant people out. Jesus says the unimportant people are the model. The ones with nothing to offer, no status to leverage, no power to wield — they are the ones who understand how this works. You do not qualify for the kingdom. You receive it. Like a child receives a gift. With open hands and no conditions.

And then Mark tells us what Jesus does next, and it is this final detail that breaks the whole scene open. He takes the children up in his arms, puts his hands upon them, and blesses them. He does not just allow their approach. He does not stand at a polite distance and wave them forward with a nod. He does not delegate the blessing to a disciple. He picks them up. He holds them against his body. He touches them with his own hands — the same hands that healed the sick, that broke the bread, that will be nailed to a cross within days. Those hands are being used, right now, in this quiet unremarkable moment, to hold small children that his own disciples tried to send away.

So what does this teach us about kindness as a practice?

It teaches us to watch for the gatekeepers in our own lives. And I do not mean other people — though that is part of it. I mean the gatekeeper inside you. The voice in your head that sorts people into categories before you have even spoken to them. The instinct that decides, in a fraction of a second, whether someone is worth your time, your attention, your energy, your kindness. We all have that voice. It is efficient. It is practical. It saves us from wasting our resources on people who cannot benefit us.

And it is exactly the voice that Jesus rebuked in his own disciples.

Kindness, as Jesus practices it here, means overriding that voice. It means catching yourself in the act of gatekeeping — the act of deciding who is important enough to receive your full presence — and choosing, deliberately, to let the children through. The children might be literal children. They might be the coworker everyone ignores because they are quiet and do not advocate for themselves. The neighbor no one talks to because they are odd, or old, or speak with an accent that marks them as foreign. The person in your life who has nothing to offer you and never will — no connection, no favor to return, no advantage to extend. The stranger who needs something from you at a moment when you feel you have nothing left to give, when you are tired and stretched thin and the gatekeeper in your head is saying, not now, not this one, I have more important things to attend to.

That is the exact moment when the practice calls you. That is the moment when Jesus would have said — let them come.

Let them come. Do not forbid them. Do not let your inner gatekeeper decide who deserves your kindness, because the gatekeeper will always choose wrong. Every single time. The gatekeeper will always prioritize the powerful over the powerless, the impressive over the invisible, the useful over the useless. And Jesus says the kingdom belongs to the useless ones. The ones who come with empty hands and no credentials and nothing to trade. The ones who simply show up and trust that the welcome is real.

This is not easy. It is not efficient. It is not practical in any way that the world understands practicality. But it is kind. It is the kindness of the open door, the cleared path, the arm that reaches past the gatekeeper to welcome in the one who almost got turned away.

And it is the kindness that Jesus demonstrated, right in front of his disciples, when he took those children in his arms and blessed them — every single one of them — with the hands of God.
