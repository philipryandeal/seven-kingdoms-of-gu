# Who Is My Neighbor?



A lawyer stands up to test Jesus.

This detail matters, because it tells us the question that follows is not sincere. It is a performance. The lawyer is not seeking wisdom. He is seeking to demonstrate his own cleverness in front of an audience. He asks Jesus what he must do to inherit eternal life, and Jesus — who understands exactly what is happening — turns the question back on him. What does the law say? How do you read it?

The lawyer answers correctly. Love God with all your heart, soul, strength, and mind. Love your neighbor as yourself. Jesus tells him he has answered well. Do this and you will live.

But Luke tells us something revealing. The lawyer, wanting to justify himself, asks a follow-up question.

"And who is my neighbour?"

This is not an innocent question. This is the question of a man looking for a boundary. He wants to know where the obligation ends. He wants to know who counts and who does not. He wants a list — these people are my neighbors, those people are not — so that he can fulfill the commandment without it costing him too much. He is looking for the edge of the requirement so that he can stand safely inside it.

And Jesus, rather than answering with a definition, tells a story. It is perhaps the most famous story he ever told, and it is told entirely in red letters.

A certain man was traveling from Jerusalem down to Jericho. The road between these two cities was notorious. It descended through rough, desolate terrain — rocky hills, narrow passes, perfect country for thieves. The man falls among robbers. They strip him, wound him, and leave him half dead on the side of the road.

Three people come along that road.

The first is a priest. A man of God. A man whose entire life is organized around the service of the sacred, around the rituals and obligations of the temple. He sees the wounded man lying there. Mark tells us he saw him — this was not a case of failing to notice. He saw, and he made a decision. He passes by on the other side. He physically crosses the road to put distance between himself and the suffering.

Why? We are not told. Perhaps he was concerned about ritual contamination — touching a bloody or potentially dead body would make a priest unclean and unable to perform his temple duties. Perhaps he was afraid the robbers were still nearby. Perhaps he was late for something important. Perhaps he simply did not want to get involved. The parable does not explain his reasons because the reasons do not matter. Whatever justification he constructed in his own mind, the result was the same. He saw a man dying, and he walked away.

The second is a Levite. A member of the priestly tribe. A man dedicated to the work of the temple. He comes to the place, looks at the wounded man — Luke makes sure we know that the Levite actually looked, actually paused, actually registered what he was seeing — and he also passes by on the other side. Two religious men. Two servants of God. Two people whose entire vocation was built around the sacred. And both of them chose the other side of the road.

The third is a Samaritan.

Now, to understand the force of this story, you need to understand who the Samaritans were in relation to the Jewish audience listening to Jesus. The Samaritans were not just strangers. They were the enemy. The hostility between Jews and Samaritans was ethnic, religious, and centuries old. They worshipped at different temples. They followed different versions of the Law. They regarded each other with deep mutual contempt. A Jewish audience hearing this parable would have expected the Samaritan to be the villain. At best, they would have expected him to be irrelevant.

Instead, Jesus makes the Samaritan the hero.

The Samaritan comes where the wounded man is. And Luke uses a phrase that should sound familiar after the last chapter. He had compassion on him. The same deep visceral response that moved Jesus to touch the leper. The Samaritan sees a bleeding stranger on the side of a dangerous road — a stranger who belongs to a people who despise him — and something moves in his gut. Something refuses to let him keep walking.

And then look at what compassion does once it arrives. The Samaritan does not simply feel something and move on. He goes to the man. He binds up his wounds, pouring in oil and wine — the medicine of the ancient world. He sets the man on his own animal, which means the Samaritan walks so that the wounded man can ride. He brings him to an inn. He takes care of him through the night. And the next morning, when he must leave, he pays the innkeeper out of his own pocket and says, "Take care of him; and whatsoever thou spendest more, when I come again, I will repay thee."

This is not a momentary gesture. This is sustained, costly, inconvenient, financially burdensome, physically exhausting compassion. The Samaritan did not just stop. He did not just feel bad. He reorganized his entire journey around the needs of a stranger who would never have done the same for him.

And then Jesus turns to the lawyer and asks a question that closes every escape route the man was trying to build.

"Which now of these three, thinkest thou, was neighbour unto him that fell among the thieves?"

Do you see what Jesus just did? The lawyer asked, "Who is my neighbor?" — meaning, who am I required to care about? Jesus does not answer that question. He reverses it entirely. He does not define who qualifies as your neighbor. He asks who ACTED as a neighbor. The question is not who deserves your compassion. The question is whether you are the kind of person who gives it.

The lawyer cannot even bring himself to say the word Samaritan. He answers, "He that shewed mercy on him."

And Jesus delivers the final red letter command of the parable. Four words that land like a hammer wrapped in silk.

"Go, and do thou likewise."

Go. Do. Likewise.

There is the verb again. There is Christ in motion. There is the yoke made plain. Do not stand here debating who qualifies as your neighbor. Go and become the kind of person who stops when someone is bleeding. Go and become the person who crosses the road toward suffering instead of away from it. Go and do what the Samaritan did — not because the wounded man deserved it, not because it was convenient, not because it was safe, but because compassion is what a human being does when they see another human being in pain and refuse to look away.

This parable teaches us three things about compassion that we need to carry with us.

The first is that seeing is not enough. The priest saw. The Levite saw. Luke is very specific about this. They were not blind. They were not unaware. They saw the man lying in his own blood on the side of the road, and they made a choice. They chose to cross to the other side. They chose distance over proximity. They chose safety over contact. They chose to protect themselves from whatever inconvenience, contamination, risk, or emotional discomfort would come from stopping.

This is indifference. This is the thing compassion interrupts. Not hatred — indifference. The priest and the Levite did not attack the wounded man. They did not wish him harm. They simply decided that his suffering was not their problem. They saw, and they kept walking. That is the most common form of cruelty in the world — not the active desire to hurt, but the passive decision not to help.

The second thing the parable teaches is that compassion crosses enemy lines. The Samaritan did not help a fellow Samaritan. He helped a Jew. A member of the people who despised him and his entire nation. Compassion, as Jesus teaches it, does not check identification before it acts. It does not ask whether the wounded person shares your politics, your religion, your ethnicity, your values, or your worldview. It sees a human being in pain, and it responds. The moment you add conditions to your compassion — I will help, but only if the person is like me, only if they believe what I believe, only if they would do the same for me — you have crossed to the other side of the road. You have become the priest. You have become the Levite.

The third thing is that compassion is not a single moment. It is sustained action. The Samaritan did not simply stop, express concern, and continue on his way feeling good about himself. He bound wounds. He provided transportation. He found shelter. He paid for care. He made a commitment to return and cover whatever additional costs arose. Compassion, in this parable, is not a feeling. It is a series of decisions, each one more costly and more inconvenient than the last, that continue long after the initial emotional response has faded. The feeling gets you to stop. The discipline keeps you there.

And this is where the parable meets us in our actual lives, today, on the roads we actually travel.

Because we pass by wounded people every day. Not always bleeding on the side of the road. Sometimes sitting quietly in a meeting, carrying something they cannot name. Sometimes standing in line at the grocery store, barely holding themselves together. Sometimes living in the house next door, invisible because we have never once knocked. Sometimes posting something online that everyone else scrolls past because engaging with someone else's pain is inconvenient, or uncomfortable, or might require you to set down whatever you were doing and actually be present with another human being's suffering.

The question the parable asks is not whether you have seen suffering. You have. Everyone has. The priest saw. The Levite saw. You have seen. The question is what you did when you saw it. Did you cross to the other side? Did you look, register what you were seeing, and keep walking? Or did you cross the road toward the wounded person and allow your day to be disrupted by the demands of someone else's pain?

The lawyer wanted a definition. Jesus gave him a direction.

Go. And do thou likewise.
