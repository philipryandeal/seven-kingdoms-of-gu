# Love Your Enemies



I want to begin this chapter by being honest with you about something. What you are about to read is the most difficult teaching in this entire book. It may be the most difficult teaching in all of scripture. It is certainly the one that I have struggled with most deeply in my own life, and I suspect the same will be true for many of you.

I am telling you this not to frighten you but to give you permission. Permission to read this chapter and feel resistance. Permission to put the book down for a moment and sit with the discomfort. Permission to say — I do not know if I can do this. Because that is an honest response, and honest responses are the only ones this book has any use for.

Here is what Jesus says, in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter five, beginning at verse forty-three.

"Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy. But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you."

Love your enemies.

Three words that have been sitting in the red letters for two thousand years, and the world has still not figured out what to do with them. Because this is not a suggestion. This is not a nice idea that you can admire from a distance and then return to the comfort of hating the people who hurt you. This is a direct command from the teacher whose yoke we are carrying, and it is the one command that reveals, more than any other, what Jesus actually means when he uses the word love.

He does not mean a feeling. He cannot mean a feeling, because you cannot command someone to feel something. You cannot order your heart to produce warmth toward a person who has damaged you, who has lied about you, who has taken something precious from you and showed no remorse. Feelings do not work that way. They arise on their own, unbidden and often unwelcome. They shift and change according to circumstances beyond your control. If love were a feeling, this command would be cruel — an impossible demand placed on the human heart by a teacher who did not understand how hearts work.

But Jesus understood hearts better than anyone who has ever lived. And he was not asking for a feeling. He was asking for a practice.

Look at the verbs. Love your enemies — that is the orientation, the commitment, the direction you point your heart. Bless them that curse you — that is speech, a choice about the words that come out of your mouth. Do good to them that hate you — that is action, a deliberate choice to extend goodwill through your behavior regardless of what you feel inside. Pray for them which despitefully use you — that is attention, the practice of holding even your persecutors in the light of your highest aspiration for their wellbeing.

Every one of those is something you do. Every one of those is a verb. Every one of those is a choice you make before the feeling arrives — and perhaps in the absence of the feeling entirely. This is what altruistic love means. Love that is not contingent on being returned. Love that is not a transaction — I will love you if you love me back, I will be kind to you if you are kind to me, I will bless you if you stop cursing me first. Altruistic love breaks the chain of reciprocity. It gives without terms. It blesses without conditions. It prays for the person who would destroy it.

And then Jesus says something that reveals why this practice matters so deeply.

"That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust."

This is one of the most quietly radical statements in all of scripture. Jesus is pointing to the sun. He is pointing to the rain. He is saying — look at how God loves. God does not check your credentials before sending sunlight. God does not withhold rain from the fields of people who have behaved badly. The sun rises on everyone. The rain falls on everyone. Divine love does not discriminate. It does not sort. It does not keep a ledger. It pours itself out on the entire world — the worthy and the unworthy, the just and the unjust, the saint and the person who hurt you so badly that you still lose sleep over it.

That is the model. That is what Jesus is asking you to move toward. Not to become God — but to practice love the way God practices it. Without conditions. Without ledgers. Without the constant calculation of who deserves it and who does not.

And then, gently, almost playfully, he puts the mirror in your hands.

"For if ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? Do not even the publicans the same?"

This is not a rebuke. I want you to hear it the way it was spoken — not with contempt, but with warmth. With the voice of a teacher who has watched his students struggle with an equation and is now showing them the part they missed. Everyone loves the people who love them back. There is no practice in that. There is no discipline, no stretching, no growth. The person who is cruel to their enemies and kind to their friends has not practiced love. They have practiced preference. They have drawn a circle and placed certain people inside it and called that love, but it is only comfort. It is only the warmth of being surrounded by people who reflect your own goodness back to you.

The practice Jesus is describing extends the circle until it has no edge. Until there is no one standing outside it. Until the enemy and the friend, the persecutor and the ally, the person who hurt you and the person who healed you all stand inside the same rain and under the same sun.

Now. I promised you warmth in this chapter, not a scolding. So let me say something important.

This is a practice. It is not an achievement. You do not read this chapter and suddenly love your enemies by tomorrow morning. That is not how human hearts work, and Jesus — who wept at a tomb and agonized in a garden the night before his death — knew that better than anyone.

What the practice asks is that you begin. That you take one step. That you choose one person — maybe the person who comes to mind immediately when you hear the words love your enemies, the face that flashed before your eyes just now — and you start. Not with a grand gesture. Not with an emotional breakthrough. With a verb.

Maybe you start with pray. You hold that person in your mind for thirty seconds tomorrow morning and you say — I wish you well. Not because you feel it. Because you are practicing it. The way a musician practices scales before the music comes. The way a dancer practices positions before the grace arrives. You put your heart through the motion of wishing someone well who does not deserve it, and you trust that the motion, over time, will begin to reshape the heart that is making it.

Maybe you start with bless. The next time that person's name comes up in conversation — the coworker, the ex, the family member, the public figure, whoever occupies the space marked enemy in your heart — you choose not to curse them. You choose not to speak the bitter word. You simply remain silent, or you say something neutral, or if you are feeling very brave, you say something kind. That is blessing. It begins in the mouth before it reaches the heart.

Maybe you start with do good. Not for them directly — maybe you are not ready for that, and that is fine. But you take the energy that you would have spent on resentment and you redirect it toward someone else who needs it. You transmute the anger into generosity. Not because the anger is wrong — anger at injustice is appropriate and human — but because you refuse to let it be the final word. You refuse to let the person who hurt you determine the shape of your love for the rest of the world.

This is altruistic love. It is not the absence of pain. It is not pretending that the wound does not exist. It is not excusing what was done or minimizing its impact or telling yourself that it was fine when it was not. It is the slow, patient, daily decision to extend love beyond the boundary of what is comfortable, what is earned, what is deserved. It is the sun rising on the evil and the good alike — not because the evil deserve the sun, but because that is what the sun does. It shines. That is its nature. And Jesus is asking you, gently, to discover that this is your nature too.

You may not believe it yet. That is all right. You do not need to believe it to begin.

The practice believes it for you until you catch up.
