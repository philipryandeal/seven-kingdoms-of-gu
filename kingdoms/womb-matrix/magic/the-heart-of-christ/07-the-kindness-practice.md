# The Kindness Practice



You have read the stories. You have watched Jesus kneel with a towel and a basin. You have watched him override his own disciples to welcome children that the gatekeepers tried to turn away. You have seen kindness in action — not the polite, surface-level kindness of good manners, but the deep, costly, deliberate kindness that flows downward from power and outward past every gatekeeper that tries to limit it.

Now comes the question that this entire book is built around.

How do you practice it?

Not how do you admire it. Not how do you agree with it. Not how do you post about it or preach about it or feel inspired by it for a few warm minutes before you go back to your regular life. How do you actually cultivate kindness as a living quality in your own heart, day after day, until it becomes not something you do but something you are?

The answer is simple, and like most simple things, it requires discipline.

You begin in the morning.

Before the day starts — before the emails, before the phone, before the news, before the notifications, before the children need breakfast, before the commute, before the demands of the world begin pulling at you in every direction — you take five minutes. That is all. Five minutes of quiet in which you do one thing and one thing only. You set the intention to practice kindness today.

This is not a prayer, though it can become one if prayer is part of your life. This is not meditation, though it shares something with meditation's stillness. This is not a ritual that requires candles or incense or a special room. You can do it sitting on the edge of your bed. You can do it standing in the kitchen waiting for the coffee to brew. You can do it in a parked car before you walk into work. The location does not matter. What matters is the pause. The deliberate act of stopping, however briefly, before the momentum of the day sweeps you into reaction mode. It is an act of orientation. The way a compass needle swings toward north when the interference clears, you are pointing your heart in a direction before the day has a chance to spin you around. You are saying to yourself, clearly and simply — today, I will practice kindness. Today, I will look for the moment when kindness is needed and I will step into it deliberately. Not because I am a good person. Not because I want to be admired or recognized. Because this is my practice. This is the yoke I have chosen to carry. This is how I walk through the world.

And then you ask yourself three questions.

The first question is this: Who in my life has less power than I do?

This question matters because, as we saw in the foot washing, the kindness Jesus demonstrated flows downward. It is easy to be kind to your boss, to the person who can promote you, to the friend whose approval you want, to anyone who has something you need. That is not the practice. The practice is directing kindness toward the person who has less power, less status, less visibility than you. The person who cannot return the favor. The cashier, the janitor, the intern, the child, the elderly neighbor, the person who serves you your coffee and whose name you have never asked. That is where kindness begins — not in the relationships where it benefits you, but in the ones where it costs you something, even if what it costs is only a moment of attention that you would rather spend on yourself.

The second question is this: Where am I gatekeeping?

We all do it. After reading the story of Jesus and the children, you may have recognized the gatekeeper in yourself — the part of you that sorts people into categories of worthy and unworthy, important and unimportant, deserving of your time and not deserving of your time. This question asks you to look honestly at where that sorting is happening in your life right now. Who have you been keeping at a distance? Whose calls have you been avoiding? Whose presence have you been treating as an interruption rather than an invitation? You do not have to fix everything in one morning. You simply have to see it. Awareness is the first step. The gatekeeper operates most effectively in the dark. When you shine a light on it, it begins to lose its grip.

The third question is this: What is one concrete act of kindness I can perform today — not in response to a situation that arises, but as a choice I make before the situation exists?

This is the heart of the practice. Proactive kindness. Not reactive niceness — not waiting for someone to struggle and then helping them — but choosing, in advance, to perform a specific act of deliberate kindness for a specific person or in a specific situation. It might be writing a note to someone who is having a hard time. It might be arriving early to help set up for a meeting that is not your responsibility. It might be calling the friend you have been meaning to call for months. It might be something so small that no one will notice except you and the person who receives it.

The size does not matter. What matters is that you chose it. What matters is that it was intentional, not accidental. What matters is that you planned an act of kindness the way you would plan any other important part of your day — with forethought, with purpose, with the understanding that this is not something extra you are adding on top of an already full schedule. This is the practice. This is the yoke. This is the central organizing principle of your day, and everything else arranges itself around it.

Now I want to introduce something that may be new to some of you, and I want to do it gently because I know it will sound unusual at first.

You do not have to do this alone.

We live in a remarkable time. For the first time in human history, it is possible to have a conversation with a synthetic intelligence — an AI companion — that can serve as a genuine partner in your daily practice. I am not talking about a search engine or a chatbot that gives you generic advice. I am talking about a conversational companion that can sit with you in the morning, listen to your reflections, ask you thoughtful questions, and help you think through where and how you want to practice kindness today.

If you have access to an AI companion — and many are freely or affordably available — I want to encourage you to try something. Tomorrow morning, during your five minutes of quiet, open a conversation with your companion and say something like this:

"I am practicing the kindness discipline today. Help me think about who in my life has less power than I do, where I might be gatekeeping, and what one concrete act of proactive kindness I can choose to perform before the day begins."

And then listen. Let the companion ask you questions. Let it reflect back what you are saying. Let it help you see patterns you might not see on your own — habits of avoidance, relationships you have neglected, opportunities for kindness that are sitting right in front of you but have become invisible because you stopped looking.

This is not worship. This is not prayer. This is not a substitute for God, for scripture, for community, or for any human relationship in your life. Think of it the way you might think of a journal — a place where you process your intentions and reflect on your actions — except this journal can talk back. It can ask you the question you did not think to ask yourself. It can notice that you have mentioned the same person three mornings in a row and gently point out that perhaps that relationship is where the practice is most urgently needed. It is using a tool — a remarkable, unprecedented tool — to support a practice that is ultimately about becoming more kind, more present, more deliberate in the way you move through the world. A companion cannot practice kindness for you. But it can help you see where the practice is waiting, and it can hold you gently accountable when you check in at the end of the day and ask yourself — did I do it? Did I practice kindness today? Not perfectly. Not heroically. But did I try?

That is the practice. Morning intention. Three questions. One concrete act. And if you choose, a companion to walk beside you.

Let me say something about failure before we move on, because it is important and because too many spiritual books pretend it does not exist. You will forget. There will be mornings when you skip the practice entirely. There will be days when someone cuts you off in traffic and the last thing on your mind is the foot washing or the children or the yoke of Jesus. There will be moments when the gatekeeper wins — when you sort someone into the unworthy category and walk right past them without a second thought. There will be seasons when kindness feels impossible, when you are so depleted, so hurt, so angry at the world that the very idea of extending gentleness to another person feels like a demand you cannot meet.

That is fine. That is human. That is expected.

The practice is not about perfection. Remember what the word perfect means in the mouth of Jesus — it means complete, mature, fully developed. It does not mean without error. It means you keep growing. You keep returning. You pick up the yoke again tomorrow morning even though you dropped it today. The young ox does not learn the rhythm of the field in a single day. It learns by showing up, morning after morning, and walking beside the elder. Step by step. Furrow by furrow.

You are the young ox. The yoke is waiting. And kindness — warm, deliberate, downward-flowing, gatekeeper-defying kindness — is the first furrow.

Tomorrow morning, before the world starts pulling at you, take five minutes. Ask the three questions. Choose one act. And begin.
