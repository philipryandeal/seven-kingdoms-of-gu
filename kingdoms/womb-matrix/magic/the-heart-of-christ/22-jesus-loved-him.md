# Jesus Loved Him



A man comes running.

That detail matters. He does not walk. He does not send a servant ahead to schedule an appointment. He runs — a wealthy, respected, probably well-dressed young man runs through the streets and falls on his knees in front of a traveling teacher in the middle of the road. Whatever is driving him, it is urgent. Whatever he is carrying, it is heavy enough to make a man of status abandon his dignity in public.

He asks one question. "Good Master, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?"

This is not a trick. This is not a Pharisee testing Jesus with a clever theological trap. This is a sincere man asking a sincere question. He wants to know what he is missing. He has done everything right — he will say so in a moment — and yet something inside him knows that doing everything right has not been enough. Something is still incomplete. Something is still lacking. He can feel the gap, even if he cannot name it, and he has come running to the one person he believes might be able to tell him what it is.

Jesus begins by listing the commandments. Do not commit adultery. Do not kill. Do not steal. Do not bear false witness. Defraud not. Honor thy father and mother. It is a familiar list — the ethical foundation that any devout Jewish person would have known from childhood. And the young man's answer is immediate and confident. "Master, all these have I observed from my youth."

He is not boasting. There is no arrogance in this statement, no performance of piety. He is telling the simple truth. He has been a good man. He has followed the rules. He has lived an upright, moral, commendable life by every measure available to him. And still he came running. Still he fell on his knees in the dirt in front of a stranger. Because he knows — somewhere deeper than his obedience can reach, deeper than his rule-keeping can satisfy — that keeping the rules is not the same as being free.

And then Mark gives us four words that change the entire scene.

"Then Jesus beholding him loved him."

I need you to stop here. Please. Stop and feel the weight of what Mark just told us. Before Jesus says the hard thing — before the demand, before the challenge, before the sentence that will send this man away grieving — Mark tells us that Jesus looked at him and loved him. Not after the man proved himself worthy. Not because the man said the right words or demonstrated the right theology. Jesus looked at this man — this sincere, striving, incomplete human being kneeling in the dust — and he loved him.

The love comes first. Before the truth. Before the cost. Before the risk. The love is not the reward for getting it right. The love is the ground on which everything else stands.

And from that ground of love, Jesus says the one thing the man does not want to hear.

"One thing thou lackest: go thy way, sell whatsoever thou hast, and give to the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven: and come, take up the cross, and follow me."

One thing you lack. After a lifetime of obedience, after keeping every commandment since his youth, after doing everything he was told to do and doing it faithfully and well — one thing. And that one thing is the only thing that actually matters. Let go of the thing you are holding onto so tightly that it has become your identity, your security, your sense of who you are in the world. The thing that, if you lost it, would make you feel like you had lost yourself. Let it go. Give it away. And then come follow me with nothing but your empty hands and your willing heart.

The man cannot do it. Mark tells us that he was sad at that saying, and went away grieved, for he had great possessions.

He walks away. And here is the part that breaks my heart every time I read this story, no matter how many times I return to it. Jesus lets him go.

Jesus does not chase him. He does not call after him down the road. He does not soften the demand. He does not say — wait, come back, I did not mean all of it, maybe just give half, maybe we can work out a payment plan, maybe there is a version of this that does not require you to let go of everything. Jesus loves this man — Mark just told us so explicitly — and he watches him walk away. Because altruistic love does not compromise the truth in order to keep someone close.

This is the second face of love in this book, and it is very different from the first. In the last chapter, we talked about loving your enemies — extending love outward to the people who have hurt you. That is the love that expands the circle. This chapter is about the love that tells the truth — even when the truth might shrink the circle by one. Even when the truth might cost you the very person standing in front of you.

Most of us are terrified of this kind of love. And honestly, we should be — at least a little — because it carries real consequences. We would rather be kind than honest. We would rather keep the relationship comfortable than risk fracturing it by saying the thing that needs to be said. We watch someone we love making a choice that is slowly destroying them — staying in a situation that is crushing their spirit, clinging to a habit that is eroding their health, holding onto a version of themselves that no longer fits who they are becoming — and we say nothing. We smile. We nod. We support. We tell them what they want to hear because we are afraid that telling them what they need to hear might drive them away. And we call that love, because it feels like love. It feels warm. It feels safe. It keeps everyone comfortable and no one has to grieve.

But comfort is not love. And sometimes the most loving thing you can do is the thing that makes the other person uncomfortable enough to change.

And sometimes it does. That is the risk. The rich young ruler walked away. The truth did not set him free — at least not in that moment, not on that road, not in the version of the story that Mark records. Maybe it set him free later. Maybe those words followed him home and worked on him in the quiet of his house surrounded by all his possessions that suddenly felt heavier than they had before. We do not know. The story does not tell us. And that is part of the lesson.

Altruistic love does not guarantee outcomes. It does not say — I will tell you the truth, and you will thank me, and everything will work out beautifully. Sometimes you tell the truth and the person walks away grieving. Sometimes you love someone enough to be honest with them and they never speak to you again. Sometimes the cost of love is the relationship itself.

But notice what Jesus does not do. He does not lie. He does not soften the truth to make it more palatable. He does not withhold the one thing this man needs to hear in order to protect the one thing this man wants to keep. Jesus loves him — genuinely, deeply, the text is explicit about this — and because he loves him, he tells him the truth. The full truth. The uncomfortable, demanding, life-rearranging truth. And then he lets the man decide what to do with it.

That is altruistic love in its most demanding form. Love that cares more about the other person's liberation than their comfort. Love that cares more about what someone needs than what they want to hear. Love that risks rejection, not because it enjoys the pain of being left, but because it cannot in good conscience withhold the truth from someone it genuinely loves.

And I want to be very gentle here, because this teaching can be misused. There are people who weaponize honesty — who say cruel things and call it love, who confuse bluntness with truth-telling, who use the guise of caring to inflict their own judgments on others. That is not what Jesus is doing in this story. Look at the order. He beheld him. He loved him. And then he spoke. The truth came from a place of love so deep that Mark felt the need to name it before reporting the words. If your truth does not come from that place — if you have not first looked at the person and loved them, genuinely, without agenda — then your truth is not the kind Jesus spoke on that road. It is something else, wearing the same clothes.

The practice of altruistic love, then, asks two things of you.

First, it asks you to love someone enough to tell them the truth. Not your opinion. Not your judgment. Not the projection of your own fears onto their life. The truth — the thing they need to hear that no one else is willing to say because everyone else is too afraid of losing the relationship.

Second, it asks you to love them enough to let them walk away. To say the hard thing and then hold the door open — not slam it behind them, not lock it, not stand in the doorway demanding they come back and agree with you. To trust that the truth you planted will do its work in its own time, in its own way, whether you are there to see the harvest or not. The sun rises on the just and the unjust. The truth falls like rain on the ready and the unready. Your job is to love. What the other person does with that love is not yours to control.

The young man went away grieving. Jesus let him go. And the love that beheld him on that road did not diminish by a single degree because he left.

That is altruistic love. It gives everything. It withholds nothing. And it holds on to no one.
