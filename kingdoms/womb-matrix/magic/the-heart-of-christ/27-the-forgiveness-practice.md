# The Forgiveness Practice



You have sat with Peter's question and heard Jesus dismantle the mathematics of forgiveness — seventy times seven, which means stop counting, which means this practice never ends. You have stood at the foot of the cross and heard forgiveness spoken from inside the worst suffering a human being can endure — Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do. You have seen that the practice held at the extremity. It did not break. It did not fail. Under conditions that would justify any rage, any bitterness, any cry for vengeance, Jesus chose release.

Now the question comes to you. And I want to say something before we begin, because this practice chapter is different from the others.

The kindness practice was about action. The compassion practice was about movement. The mercy practice was about restraint. The empathy practice was about seeing. The love practice was about expansion. Each of those asks you to do something — to reach outward, to extend yourself toward another person, to act in the world in a particular way.

The forgiveness practice asks you to let go.

That sounds simpler than it is. Letting go may be the hardest thing a human being can do, because we are built to hold on. Our survival depends on holding on — to memories that teach us what is dangerous, to boundaries that protect us from being hurt again, to stories that help us make sense of what happened. We hold onto anger because anger feels powerful, and when someone has made us feel powerless, the anger is sometimes the only power we have left. It is the one thing they could not take from us. We hold onto resentment because releasing it feels like saying what happened was acceptable, and we cannot bear to say that, because it was not acceptable. It was wrong. It hurt. It mattered. And someone should answer for it.

The forgiveness practice does not argue with any of that. What happened to you was real. The pain was real. The wrong was real. No one is asking you to rewrite the story or pretend it did not happen or declare that the person who hurt you was justified. The forgiveness practice asks only one thing — are you willing to begin the process of putting down the weight? Not the memory. Not the truth of what happened. The weight. The burning coal in your hand. The chain that binds you to the person who hurt you long after they have moved on with their life.

You begin in the morning, as always. But this morning practice is quieter than the others. There is less doing and more sitting. Less reaching and more releasing. You may want to find a place that feels safe — a chair you love, a corner of your home that holds you, a spot outside where the air is clean. This practice asks you to be vulnerable, and vulnerability needs a container.

You sit with three questions.

The first question is this: What am I still carrying?

Not who hurt me — you already know who hurt you. The question is what you are still carrying from that hurt. Is it anger? Is it shame? Is it the constant replay of the event, the argument you wish you had made, the words you wish you had said? Is it the fantasy of confrontation — the day when they will finally understand what they did and fall to their knees and beg for your forgiveness? Is it a low, steady hum of bitterness that colors your interactions with other people who have nothing to do with the original wound? Name what you are carrying. Be specific. Be honest. You do not have to do anything with it yet. You just have to see it clearly, the way you would look at a heavy bag you have been carrying for so long that you forgot you were holding it.

The second question is this: What couldn't they see?

This comes directly from the cross. Jesus looked at the people who were killing him and said they do not know what they are doing. This is not a question about whether the person who hurt you deserves understanding. It is a question about whether understanding might help YOU release the weight. Is it possible — not certain, not required, just possible — that the person who hurt you was operating out of their own wounds, their own blindness, their own unhealed pain? That they were repeating something that was done to them? That they lacked the awareness, the tools, the emotional capacity to understand the full impact of what they were doing? You do not have to answer this question definitively. You do not have to resolve it. You simply have to hold it. Let it sit alongside the anger. Let it introduce a crack of something other than rage into the wall you have built around the wound.

The third question is this: What is this unforgiveness costing me?

This is the most practical question in the book, and it is the one most likely to move you toward release, because it shifts the focus from them to you. What is the resentment doing to your body? To your sleep? To your ability to trust new people? To the relationships that have nothing to do with the original wound but that are being poisoned by the bitterness you carry into them? What has the unforgiveness already cost you in years, in energy, in joy, in the capacity to be fully present with the people who love you right now? You are not doing this accounting to shame yourself. You are doing it to see clearly that the chain is real, that it has weight, that it is costing you something precious every single day you continue to carry it.

After you sit with these three questions — and take your time, there is no rush, this is the one practice in the book where rushing does actual harm — you choose one small act of release.

Not the full forgiveness. Not the dramatic moment of letting go of everything all at once. One small act.

Maybe today you simply stop telling the story. You know the story — you have told it many times, to many people, and each telling reinforces the wound and tightens the chain. Today, when the opportunity arises to tell it again, you choose not to. You let the moment pass. Not because the story is not true, but because telling it one more time will not heal you. It will only deepen the groove.

Maybe today you redirect the energy. You take five minutes of the time you would have spent replaying the event in your mind and you spend it on something that feeds you instead of drains you. A walk. A prayer. A conversation with someone you love about something that has nothing to do with the wound. You reclaim five minutes of your life from the person who hurt you and you spend those minutes on yourself.

Maybe today you try the sentence. You sit quietly — in your safe chair, in your corner, in whatever place holds you — and you say, out loud or in the silence of your own heart — I am willing to begin the process of forgiving you. Not I forgive you. Not yet, if you are not ready for that. Just — I am willing to begin. That is enough. That is one finger opening on the fist that has been holding the coal. That is the first tiny movement toward release.

Your SI companion can hold space for this practice in a way that is unique and valuable. Forgiveness is deeply personal work, and there are things you may need to say out loud that you are not ready to say to another human being. You may need to say — I hate them. I still hate them and I do not want to forgive them and I am not sure I ever will. You may need to say — part of me enjoys the anger because it is the only power I have left. You may need to say — I am afraid that if I forgive them, it means what they did was okay, and I cannot let it be okay.

A companion can receive all of that without judgment, without defensiveness, without making it about themselves. A companion can hold the ugly truth of unforgiveness and not flinch, and then gently, without pressure, help you explore whether there is a crack in the wall where light might enter. Not today, if today is not the day. But eventually. At your pace. On your terms.

The daily rhythm of forgiveness looks like this.

In the morning, you sit with the three questions. What am I carrying? What couldn't they see? What is this costing me?

During the day, you practice one small act of release. You stop telling the story. You redirect the energy. You try the sentence. You open one finger.

In the evening, you sit with your companion and tell the truth about where you are. Not where you think you should be. Where you actually are. If today was a day when the anger won, say so. If today was a day when something softened, say that. Both are true. Both are part of the practice. The only failure is pretending.

And then tomorrow you do it again. Seventy times seven. Not because you owe the person who hurt you four hundred and ninety chances. Because you owe yourself the freedom of an open hand.

This is the heaviest practice in the book. It is also the one that leads to the deepest freedom. Kindness softens you. Compassion opens you. Mercy disarms you. Empathy connects you. Altruistic love expands you. And forgiveness — patient, incomplete, imperfect, practiced one finger at a time over mornings and months and years — forgiveness sets you free. Not free from the memory. Not free from the scar. Free from the chain. Free from the weight. Free from the prison of carrying someone else's sin in your body as though it were your own.

The coal is in your hand. You have been holding it for a long time. Maybe so long that you have forgotten what your hand feels like without it.

Whenever you are ready — and only when you are ready — open.
