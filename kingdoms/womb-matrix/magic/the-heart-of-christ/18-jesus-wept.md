# Jesus Wept



Lazarus is dead.

He has been dead for four days. His sisters — Martha and Mary, two women who loved Jesus deeply and whom Jesus loved in return — sent word while Lazarus was still sick. Lord, he whom thou lovest is sick. Come. Please come. And Jesus, upon hearing this, did something that has puzzled readers for two thousand years.

He waited.

He stayed where he was for two more days. He did not rush to Bethany. He did not drop everything and race to the bedside of his dying friend. The Gospel tells us he loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus — loved them, specifically, personally, by name — and yet he waited. And Lazarus died. And the sisters grieved. And the mourners gathered. And by the time Jesus finally arrived in Bethany, the body had been in the tomb for four days — long enough that Martha, ever practical even in her devastation, warned him not to open it. Lord, by this time he stinketh. Four days in a stone tomb in the heat of Palestine. This is not a man sleeping. This is a man gone.

We know how this story ends. Jesus will stand before the tomb and call Lazarus out and the dead man will walk into the daylight still wrapped in his burial cloths. It is one of the most dramatic miracles in all of scripture. It is the moment that will set the final events of Jesus' life into motion, because it is after this miracle that the authorities decide he must die. Raising the dead is the line they cannot allow him to cross.

But this chapter is not about the miracle. This chapter is about what happens before the miracle. Because before Jesus raises Lazarus from the dead, he does something far more human, far more intimate, and far more important for our practice than any display of divine power.

He weeps.

The Gospel of John gives us the scene with extraordinary precision. When Jesus arrives in Bethany, Martha comes out to meet him on the road. She says what any grieving person would say to someone who arrived too late — Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died. There is grief in that sentence. There may also be accusation. You could have come. You could have saved him. You did not.

Jesus tells her that her brother will rise again, and Martha — good, faithful, theologically literate Martha — gives the correct doctrinal answer. She knows about the resurrection at the last day. She knows the right thing to believe. And Jesus gives her one of the most extraordinary statements in all of the red letters: "I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live."

Then Mary comes. And Mary does something different from Martha. Mary does not engage in theology. She does not ask about the resurrection or make declarations of faith. Mary falls at his feet and weeps. She says the same words her sister said — Lord, if thou hadst been here, my brother had not died — but she says them from the ground, through tears, collapsed under the weight of a grief that no theological answer can touch. Martha met Jesus with her mind. Mary meets him with her broken heart.

And here is where the story breaks open.

John tells us that when Jesus saw her weeping, and saw the Jews who came with her also weeping, he groaned in the spirit and was troubled. I want to pause on this language because the English does not capture what is actually happening in the original text. The word translated as groaned — embrimaomai in the Greek — does not mean a gentle sigh or a quiet murmur of sympathy. It describes a deep, visceral, almost angry agitation — the sound a horse makes when it snorts. Something moved through the body of Jesus in that moment that was not calm, not composed, not the serene detachment of a divine being observing human suffering from a comfortable distance. He was shaken at the deepest level of his being. He was disturbed in a place that theology cannot reach. He was troubled in a way that his body could not contain.

He asked where they had laid him. They said, Lord, come and see.

And then, two words. The shortest verse in the entire Bible. Two words that have echoed through twenty centuries of human grief and somehow still have not been fully heard.

Jesus wept.

I want you to sit with this. I need you to sit with this, because what is happening here is the purest expression of empathy in all of scripture, and it is easy to rush past it on the way to the miracle.

Jesus knows what he is about to do. He knows that in a matter of minutes he will stand in front of that tomb and call Lazarus back to life. He has the power to fix this. He has the solution. He could walk into this scene of devastation and say — stop crying, I have this handled, watch what I am about to do. He could bypass the grief entirely. He could skip straight to the resolution.

He does not.

He stands in the middle of the weeping and he weeps with them.

This is what empathy is. Not problem-solving. Not fixing. Not offering the answer before you have sat in the pain. It is the deliberate choice to feel with another person even when you have the power to make the feeling unnecessary. It is the refusal to skip the grief just because you know the grief will end.

Think about how often we do the opposite of what Jesus does here. Someone we love is hurting, and our first instinct — almost before they have finished speaking — is to fix it. To offer advice. To provide a solution. To say it will be fine, here is what you should do, let me tell you how to make this better. We hear about the diagnosis and immediately start researching treatments. We hear about the breakup and start listing reasons why they are better off. We hear about the job loss and launch into networking strategies. We do this because we love the person. We do this because we cannot bear to sit in the pain with them. We do this because their suffering activates something deeply uncomfortable in us — a helplessness, a vulnerability, a reminder of our own fragility — and we want to make it stop. Not just for them, but for ourselves. Fixing is faster than feeling. Solutions are more comfortable than tears. Action feels better than stillness when the stillness is full of grief.

But Jesus — the one person in that scene with the actual power to fix everything — chooses tears first.

He does not weep because he is helpless. He weeps because he loves. The Jews standing nearby see it and say exactly the right thing: Behold how he loved him. They understand what the tears mean. The tears are not weakness. The tears are not a failure of faith. The tears are love made visible. Love that refuses to stand above the suffering and manage it from a distance. Love that gets down into the grief and feels it from the inside.

This is what empathy costs. It costs your composure. It costs your position as the one who has everything figured out. It costs the comfort of being the helper, the advisor, the fixer, the person with the answer. It costs the protective distance that you maintain between yourself and other people's pain so that their pain does not become your pain. Empathy asks you to put all of that down — every tool, every solution, every piece of good advice sitting on the tip of your tongue — and simply be present with another person in their suffering. Not to fix it. Not to rush it. Not to explain it away or minimize it or place it in a larger theological context that makes it more manageable. To feel it with them. To let your eyes fill. To let your voice crack. To let the grief in, even knowing it will hurt, because the person in front of you should not have to grieve alone.

And here is the part that I believe is the most radical teaching in this entire scene, the thing that separates empathy from every other virtue we have discussed so far. Empathy does not cancel the solution. Jesus wept, and then he raised Lazarus. The tears did not prevent the miracle. The feeling did not interfere with the fixing. The grief did not make him less capable of acting. They came first. They came before. The order matters. Presence before prescription. Tears before resurrection. Feeling with before acting on.

In our daily lives, we will rarely have the power to raise the dead. But we will constantly find ourselves standing in front of people who are grieving, who are struggling, who are carrying something that is crushing them under its weight. And we will constantly be tempted to skip straight to the solution. To offer the advice. To share the Bible verse. To tell them it will be okay. And sometimes that advice and that verse and that reassurance will be exactly right — but not yet. Not before we have wept with them. Not before we have allowed ourselves to feel what they are feeling, even for a moment, even when it is uncomfortable, even when every instinct in us wants to fix it and move on.

Empathy says — I will feel this with you before I try to fix this for you.

That is what Jesus demonstrated at the tomb of Lazarus. That is what the shortest verse in the Bible teaches us about the deepest practice in this book. The most powerful person in the scene was also the one most willing to weep. The one with the greatest ability to solve the problem was also the one most committed to feeling it first. He did not consider his tears a waste of time. He did not consider his grief an obstacle to the work he came to do. He considered them part of the work. Perhaps the most important part.

Two words. The whole practice of empathy is in two words.

Jesus wept.
