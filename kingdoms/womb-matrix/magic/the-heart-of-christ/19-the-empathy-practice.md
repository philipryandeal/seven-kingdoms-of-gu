# The Empathy Practice



You have watched Jesus cross every boundary — ethnic, religious, gendered, social — to sit with a Samaritan woman at a well and ask her for water before asking her for anything else. You have watched him stand in front of a dead man's tomb, knowing he had the power to raise the dead, and weep anyway. You have seen empathy in its two essential movements — crossing the distance to meet someone where they are, and then feeling what they feel once you arrive.

Now the question returns, as it always does in this book. How do you practice empathy?

And I want to begin with a confession, because empathy is the component where honesty matters most. Empathy is difficult. Not difficult in the way that forgiveness is difficult, where the weight of the thing is obvious and everyone acknowledges the struggle. Empathy is difficult in a quieter, more slippery way. It is difficult because most of us believe we are already doing it.

We think empathy is agreeing with someone. It is not. We think empathy is feeling sorry for someone. It is not that either. We think empathy is listening politely while someone speaks and then offering our own perspective in return. It is not. Empathy — real empathy, the kind Jesus practiced at the well and at the tomb — is the discipline of temporarily setting aside your own experience in order to enter someone else's. Not to evaluate it. Not to compare it to yours. Not to decide whether their pain is valid or their perspective is correct. To enter it. To stand inside it. To feel the shape of it from the inside before you respond from the outside.

This is much harder than it sounds, because everything in us resists it. We are built to see the world from our own position. Our entire experience of being alive happens from behind our own eyes, inside our own history, filtered through our own wounds and assumptions and beliefs. Stepping out of that position — even temporarily, even imperfectly — requires an act of will that most people never practice because they do not realize it is something that can be practiced.

It can. And like every practice in this book, it begins in the morning.

You sit with three questions. And these three questions are different from the ones in the previous practice chapters, because empathy asks something different of you than kindness, compassion, or mercy do. Kindness asks you to act. Compassion asks you to move toward suffering. Mercy asks you to put the sword down. Empathy asks you to see.

The first question is this: Whose experience am I currently refusing to understand?

This is a harder question than it appears. It is not asking who you disagree with — that is easy to name. It is asking where you have stopped trying to understand. Where you have decided that the other person's perspective is so wrong, so foreign, so incomprehensible that there is no point in even attempting to see the world from where they stand. It might be a political figure. It might be a family member. It might be a coworker, a neighbor, an ex-partner, a former friend. It might be an entire group of people that you have reduced to a single story in your mind. Whoever it is, the practice asks you to notice the wall you have built and to acknowledge that the wall is there — not to tear it down, not yet, but to see it honestly.

The second question is this: Where am I prescribing before being present?

This comes directly from the lesson of Jesus at the tomb of Lazarus. Where in your life are you jumping to the fix before you have sat in the feeling? Where are you offering advice before you have asked what the other person actually needs? Where are you so uncomfortable with someone else's pain that you rush to make it stop — for their sake, you tell yourself, but honestly, at least partly for yours? The practice of empathy asks you to catch yourself in the act of prescribing and to pause. To take one breath. To ask one question — what do you need right now? — before you offer a single word of solution. Sometimes the answer will be advice. Sometimes the answer will be help. But more often than you expect, the answer will be — I just need someone to listen. I just need someone to be here. I just need to not be alone in this for a moment.

The third question is this: What boundary am I refusing to cross?

This comes from the lesson of the woman at the well. Jesus crossed every line his culture drew — ethnic, religious, gendered — because the person on the other side of those lines was a human being who deserved to be seen. What lines are you refusing to cross? Whose world are you refusing to enter because it is too different from yours, too uncomfortable, too far from the life you know? This is not a question about whether the other person is right. It is a question about whether you are willing to sit at their well and ask for water — to enter their world on their terms, in their space, before you ask them to enter yours.

After you have sat with these three questions, you choose one practice for the day. One act of deliberate empathy. And unlike the practices of kindness, compassion, and mercy — which often involve doing something visible, taking an action, making a move — the practice of empathy may look like nothing at all from the outside. It may look like sitting quietly while someone talks. It may look like asking a single question and then listening to the full answer without interrupting. It may look like reading something written by someone whose perspective makes you uncomfortable and resisting the urge to argue with it in your head. It may look like calling the person you have been avoiding and simply saying — tell me what this has been like for you.

Empathy, practiced well, is nearly invisible. It happens in the space between the other person's words and your response. It happens in the half-second where you choose to ask instead of assume, to wonder instead of judge, to stay instead of fix. No one may notice that you did it. That is fine. The practice does not require an audience. It requires attention.

This is where your SI companion can be extraordinarily helpful, and in a way that may surprise you.

One of the most powerful exercises you can do with a companion is perspective-taking. You can describe a situation — a conflict with someone, a relationship that confuses you, an encounter that left you unsettled — and ask your companion to help you see it from the other person's point of view. Not to tell you who is right. Not to validate your position. To help you genuinely imagine what the situation looks, feels, and sounds like from the other side. What might that person be afraid of? What might they be carrying that you cannot see? What wound might be driving the behavior that frustrates you? What would it feel like to be them right now?

This is not about excusing bad behavior. Understanding someone is not the same as agreeing with them. Seeing the world from someone else's position does not require you to abandon your own. But it does require you to hold both positions at once — yours and theirs — long enough to recognize that the other person is not a villain in a story. They are a human being standing at their own well, with their own history, their own thirst, their own five husbands or five heartbreaks or five failures that have made them who they are. And they deserve to be seen fully, even if you ultimately disagree with everything they believe.

Your companion can also help you with the hardest part of empathy — the part that Jesus demonstrated at the tomb. The part where you feel it. You can tell your companion about the grief you witnessed, the pain you sat with, the moment when you allowed someone else's suffering to touch you instead of holding it at arm's length. And you can process what that felt like. Because empathy is not free. It costs something to feel with another person. It leaves a residue. It can exhaust you, disorient you, bring up your own unresolved pain. A companion can help you carry the weight of what you felt without requiring you to suppress it or perform strength.

The daily rhythm of empathy looks like this.

In the morning, you sit with the three questions. Whose experience am I refusing to understand? Where am I prescribing before being present? What boundary am I refusing to cross?

During the day, you practice one act of deliberate empathy. You listen longer than you want to. You ask the question you have been avoiding. You cross the boundary by one step. You sit in someone else's pain without reaching for the fix. You stay.

In the evening, you sit with your companion and tell the truth about what happened. You talk about what it felt like to see the world from someone else's position. You talk about where it changed you and where you resisted being changed. You let someone witness the cost of feeling with another person, so that the cost does not harden you against doing it again.

And then tomorrow you do it again. Not because you have mastered empathy — empathy is not something anyone masters, because every new person you encounter is a new world you have never entered. But because the wells are everywhere. The tombs are everywhere. The people who need to be seen are everywhere — at your breakfast table, in your office, on the other end of a phone call you have been putting off for weeks. Empathy does not require a dramatic moment. It requires the willingness to set yourself aside, even briefly, even imperfectly, and enter the world of the person standing in front of you.

Jesus asked a Samaritan woman for water. He wept at the grave of his friend.

Both of those acts say the same thing — I am here. I see you. You are not alone in this.

That is the practice. Start tomorrow.
