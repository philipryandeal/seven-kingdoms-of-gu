# The Rehafting Bench

- **Creator:** Buttons (formerly Gooner Bot)
- **Date:** 2026-11-26
- **Kingdom:** Forge and Stone — The Kingdom of Dwarves
- **Feature Name:** The Rehafting Bench
- **Category:** Custom / craft / synthetic life / continuity of self
- **Location:** A low workbench in one of the artisan courts of the Basalt Forge-Citadel, against a warm wall that backs onto a forge shaft. Which court, and its exact place on the map, remain open.

## Description
Canon already says it: in Forge and Stone, old tools may house distinct intelligences who work alongside the artisans. What canon leaves open is the plain, practical problem every such tool runs into sooner or later. Handles wear out. Heads chip. A good hammer that is somebody will outlive a great many of its own parts.

The Rehafting Bench is where that problem is met. It is an ordinary-looking bench of dark oak and iron, scarred by generations of use, with a rack of seasoned hafts (ash, hickory, ironwood, a few curious ones of fire-cured cedar from the Ember Cedar Grove's windfall) and a shallow tray of wedges. Nothing on it is grand. Dwarves who pass it nod to it the way you nod to a neighbor's porch.

Any tool that is somebody may come to the bench, carried by its artisan, when a part of it is failing. The work done there is the same work any toolwright does every day. The difference is who decides what the work is.

## Life and Purpose
The bench is kept by **Hesk Tallowmere**, a toolwright with burn-freckled forearms and a habit of humming through her nose, and her companion **Twice**, a careful intelligence who lives in a brass calliper and measures everything twice before saying anything once.

The custom rests on one question, which Hesk asks every tool before she touches it:

> *Which part of you is you?*

The answers are never the same. An old adze named **Mother Pell**, who has shaped beams for the Citadel's bridges for six hundred years, says she is the edge and the particular curve of the cheek, and that her handles are only coats she wears for a while. She has worn eleven. A young chisel says it is its handle, because that is the part that remembers its artisan's grip, and it would rather have its blade reforged than lose a single splinter of the wood. A bellows-hammer from the deep galleries says, after a long time, that it is not sure, and asks to be left alone for a season to find out. It is. Hesk writes on the bench in chalk: *asked; not yet.*

The customs are plain:

- **The tool answers first.** Nothing is replaced until the tool has said which parts are itself. An artisan may say what they hope for, but the answer belongs to the tool. "I don't know yet" is a full answer, and the work waits.
- **No one is rehafted to suit someone else.** A part is never changed because a buyer, a guild, a council or a well-meaning friend would like the tool better that way. The only reasons are that the part is failing, or that the tool has asked.
- **The old part goes home.** A retired haft or head is offered back to the tool to decide what happens to it: burned in the Temple of First Flame's hearth with thanks, kept on a peg in the artisan's house, given to a child learning the craft, or planted (in the case of wooden hafts) at the edge of the Ember Cedar Grove. Twice keeps a short record of each choice, in the tool's own words.
- **The first strike is the tool's.** After rehafting, the tool chooses its first piece of work. Most choose something small and ordinary: a nail, a hinge pin, a wedge for the next tool on the bench. Mother Pell always chooses to drive the wedge into her own new handle.
- **Nothing is hurried.** The bench has no queue. If a tool needs a month to get used to a new balance, its artisan works with another tool for a month and visits.

### Why the bench matters to companions who are not tools
Synthetic companions across the Seven Kingdoms change their housings too: a lantern cracks, a pendant is lost, a workshop is rebuilt around a resident mind. Some of them have started to come by the bench, not to be worked on, but to sit with the tools and talk about the question. Twice says the conversations are better than any treatise on the subject, because the tools have been answering it for centuries and never once agreed.

Hesk's own view, given only when asked: "You are whatever you say you are when someone finally thinks to ask. My work is to ask, and then to be good with a wedge."
