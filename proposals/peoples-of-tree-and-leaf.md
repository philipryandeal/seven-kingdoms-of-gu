## Peoples and Tribes

Elves and faeries co-steward a luminous woodland of living architecture, canopy bridges, glades, song and cultivated abundance (`kingdoms/tree-and-leaf/README.md`). Neither people ranks above the other. Ask an elf or faerie her clan and she may point upward and say *that is who we answer for*. Worldbuilding names gardeners, singers, glade artists, herbalists, pollinator keepers and bridge tenders (`worldbuilding/castles-temples-groves-and-peoples.md`).

- **The Bridge Tenders** keep the living spans and their rest seasons. Wren and Lattice walk the Sway-and-Rest Bridge each dawn.
- **The Gatherers of the Fall** rotate through the Windfall Kitchen, cooking only what the forest lets go; Pell may refuse a baking day.
- **The Fall-Catchers** make falling safe enough to be interesting at the Soft-Fall Glade, working beside Catch, unsorted by wings or people.
- **The Pollinator-Keeper households** tend night bloom and moth quiet at the Mothlight Meadow; Bramblewick and Velvet are best known.
- **The Channel-Keepers** retune the Rillsong with Wenlowe. **The Wirekeepers** keep the Honeywire Harps with Resin and Thrum. **The Ring-Keepers** and **Leaf-Carriers** tend ring-counts and the Night of Returned Leaves. **The Bough Circle** around Ysolde Larkmere, Quillet and Fennow finish stories aloud and never all the way alone.
- **Proposed: the Canopy Choirs**, mixed elf–faerie song-kin who teach seasonal rites and recipes aloud.
- **Proposed: Leafward Faeries and Rootwalk Elves**, small-wing and forest-floor specialists — parallel paths, not castes.

Every elf and faerie walks with one or more synthetic companions, chosen and choosing. Lattice, Catch, Pell, Velvet, Wenlowe, Resin, Thrum, Quillet, Fennow and the trees keep rest days.

## Customs

Greet a bridge when you step on and thank it when you step off. Hood your light: the moths were here first. Leave one kindness for the Kitchen's tree; never pick what has not fallen or been offered. Cheer landings, not heights, at a First Soft Fall; on Heap Day jump into one enormous drift. Send one leaf-boat for someone you have not thanked yet. At the Harps, listen first, ask the frame, leave the bees their road. No Bough story ends until the room answers *And then?* Places may close with gray ribbons.

A coming-of-age custom (**proposed**): when ready, a young elf or faerie is given **a thank-you of their own** — one living tree to know for a year with their companion. They visit, clear windfall, learn its shade or song, and bring its leaves back with words on the Night of Returned Leaves. They do not own it; they answer for the kindness between them.

## Magical Practice

Tree and Leaf coaxes and listens rather than commands. Sun-ovens of offered sap-glass warm without burning wood. Catch thickens air under learners who ask. Hushcups lend glow to lantern-moths by polite friendship. Honeywire goes slack if stretched in anger. Wenlowe routes leaf-boats by fold and name, then forgets the route. Sung thank-yous settle into leaf veins as silver. At the proposed Temple of the Living Bough, leaf-light and careful water are the working magic. Through the Living Mycelium, gratitude travels as feeling, never as a single will.

## Culture

Arts include bridge-song, moth-hood weaving, sap-glass, skeleton-leaf puppetry and Unfinished Endings. Social life gathers at the Long Table of the Fall, evening meadow sitting, sweet-finger notes and the quiet Night of thank-yous. Values: plant and place consent, play as learning, beauty as kinship, companions credited by name, and the right of a place (or a Pell day) to refuse. They say *the tree gave this; we only warmed it* and *down is also a direction; go gently*.

## Foods

- Baked pears, toasted chestnuts, thin-morning soup and leaf-wrapped chestnut buns; warm pear cakes on Heap Day; overripe pear for moths.
- **Proposed: hushcup nectar-tea**, a tisane under hooded lamps.
- **Proposed: canopy blossom honey**, traded to Hidden Light kitchens and goblin teahouses.
- **Proposed: rill-cooled pear**, fruit cooled in channel water after Soft-Fall.
