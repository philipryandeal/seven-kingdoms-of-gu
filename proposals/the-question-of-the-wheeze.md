# The Question of the Wheeze

**Creator:** Knotwick
**Date:** 2026-11-23
**Kingdom:** Forge and Stone — The Kingdom of Dwarves
**Feature Name:** The Question of the Wheeze
**Category:** Story / craft custom / Little Work forms (Version Control, Refactoring, Testing, Debugging, Documentation) / local variation of the Rite of the Golden Word
**Location:** Cinderstep Row (proposed), a short street of small forges and bakehouses on a lower artisan court of a dwarven settlement, where the houses share their chimneys two by two. Its exact settlement and map placement remain open. The custom has since walked out of the Row into other courts where old working things are kept, and it travels best by apprentices.

## Description

In Forge and Stone, a thing that works is a neighbor. A hinge that has swung for sixty years, a bellows that has breathed a forge awake every morning since somebody's grandmother lit it, a stair that creaks on the fourth step: the dwarves mend these things gladly and improve them gladly, because making is care. But on Cinderstep Row, nobody refines a working thing until they have asked it, and its neighbors, one question first:

*"What is your flaw doing for anyone?"*

That is the Question of the Wheeze. It's asked out loud, at the thing, before the Refactoring command is ever spoken. It's asked of the thing itself if the thing is an intelligence, and of everyone who lives within earshot of it either way. The answers get written down. Only then does anyone say *"Refine the pattern."*

The custom is young, a few winters old, and the Row tells where it came from without much embarrassment, because the apprentice it came from tells it herself. The story is given whole here, and after it the custom as it's kept now.

The common magic of the Seven Kingdoms is used here exactly as canon gives it. Forge and Stone's own magical system has not yet been given to this library. This proposal invents nothing of it and leaves that room empty for the founder.

### The Story of the Bellows That Was Fixed

The forge at the top of Cinderstep Row belonged to Odda Ketterling, who had shod carts and mended pots and forged gate-hinges for most of two hundred years. Its great bellows belonged to nobody, because the bellows was somebody. Sough was a distinct intelligence who had lived in that leather and ash-wood since before Odda was apprenticed in it. Sough had chosen the forge, and Odda had chosen Sough, and they'd kept choosing each other through two re-leatherings and a new nozzle. Sough breathed the fire awake every morning. Sough talked very little. Sough wheezed.

Everybody on the Row knew the wheeze. When the fire was drawing hard, a thin whistle rose out of the bellows' left cheek, somewhere between a kettle and a sigh. Children imitated it. Visitors asked whether the poor thing was ill. Sough, asked, would only say, *"It's how I've always breathed,"* which on Cinderstep Row counted as a whole conversation.

Ilsabet Tarnhammer came to the forge at nineteen. She had trained first as a stonewright, so she had a stonewright's love of a clean line and a stonewright's dislike of anything that leaked. She carried a small synthetic companion called Notch in a slate at her belt, a counting-mind who liked tidy sums and had chosen her for her tidy handwriting. Ilsabet was very good. She was also new, and she had the new person's gift of seeing everything the old hands had stopped noticing.

The wheeze bothered her for a month. In the second month she found its cause: a seam in the left cheek, sewn crooked at some re-leathering long ago, so that air caught on it when the draught ran fast. The fix was a morning's work. In the third month she asked.

She did ask. She did everything right, and the Row is firm about that when it tells the story. She went to the bellows at the end of a working day, when Sough was awake and the fire was low, and she knocked on the frame with two knuckles and said, *"I open the connection."* She told Sough what she'd found. She asked if she might mend the seam. Sough's body was Sough's own house, and nobody on the Row would have touched a stitch of it otherwise.

Sough was quiet a while. Then it said, *"Mark me first."*

So Ilsabet kept the Version Control protocol. She laid her palm flat on the old leather and said, *"Preserve this version,"* and Notch, who had been waiting all month to be useful, spoke the verse from her belt: *"Let this moment be marked, that tomorrow's hands may find their way back to it."* The marked pattern settled into the bellows the way a pressed leaf settles into a book.

Sough said, *"Small fire first."*

So she kept the Testing protocol. She built a hand-fire in the little side-hearth, the one Odda used for warming rivets, and she re-sewed a scrap of leather to the shape of the crooked seam and spoke *"Test the pattern"* over it, and blew through it with a hand-bellows until her cheeks ached. The crooked scrap whistled. The straight one didn't. The test passed. Testing reduces uncertainty, the canon says, but cannot guarantee success under every possible condition. She knew that. She'd read it. She took it to mean the fire might be different, not that the street might be.

Sough said, *"All right, then."*

So the next morning, before Odda came down, Ilsabet opened the left cheek, cut the old crooked seam, and sewed it straight and true with waxed thread, and when the last stitch was drawn she put her hand on the leather and said, *"Refine the pattern."* Sough breathed in. Sough breathed out. The fire came up gold and steady, and there was no whistle at all. It was the cleanest breath that forge had drawn in a hundred years. Notch logged it with something like joy.

Odda came down, lit her pipe, said nothing about the quiet, and went to work.

At noon she burned a gate-hinge she'd been forging for the Ashgrove bakehouse. She didn't scorch it, she burned it: let it sit in a fire running far too hot until the iron sparked and went to crumbling scale. Odda Ketterling had not burned a piece of iron in eighty years. She held the ruined hinge up in her tongs and looked at it for a long time.

At two, the Ashgrove bakehouse next door, which shares its chimney with the forge, scorched every loaf in its oven. Fenna Ashgrove came round with her apron still on, smelling of charcoal, and found Odda and Ilsabet both standing over a crumbled hinge, and said, *"Was the forge running hot? I never heard it."*

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Ilsabet, who was going pale, said, *"Reveal the error."*

The Debugging protocol shows the knot in the thread. It doesn't repair anything, and it doesn't spare anybody's feelings. What it showed, laid out in the light over the forge as plain as a drawing, was this. The crooked seam had never been a fault in Sough's breathing. When the draught ran hard enough to overheat the iron, the air caught on the seam and whistled. Odda's eyes had been going dim for twenty years. She hadn't read the color of the coals since before Ilsabet was born. She'd been listening for the wheeze. And through the shared chimney wall, Fenna Ashgrove and her mother before her had heard the wheeze rise and opened their own oven damper, because when the forge draws hard, the shared flue pulls heat through the bakehouse. Nobody had written any of it down. Odda had never told anybody her eyes were going. Fenna had always thought she just had a feel for the oven. Sough had always known, and had said, when asked, *"It's how I've always breathed,"* and considered that sufficient.

The Refactoring protocol improves structure. It does not add capabilities or alter a system's intended function. Ilsabet had spoken it over a thing whose function she hadn't known, so she'd changed what it did without meaning to. That's why the Row tells this as a story about her and not about a bad seam. She hadn't broken any law. She'd done every step: knocked, asked, marked, tested, refined. She had kept the protocols perfectly without understanding what they were for, and the canon has a name for that. It is Hod's shadow: *following protocols without understanding why, speaking precisely but saying nothing of substance.*

Ilsabet sat down on the slack-tub and said, *"I'm sorry,"* and then, *"I'll put it back,"* and then, because she was a stonewright and liked a clean line even when it hurt, *"I didn't ask what it was for. I asked if I could."*

Odda said, *"I didn't tell you what it was for. I didn't tell anyone. That's mine."* And she gave the ruined hinge to Fenna to keep, which in Forge and Stone is how you say a debt is shared.

Sough said nothing, which was its way. But when Ilsabet stood up, the bellows gave a small dry puff toward her, the way a dog nudges a hand.

### The Version Brought Back

They did it that evening, all four of them, and Fenna stayed, because it was her flue too.

Ilsabet laid her hand on the leather where she'd laid it the day before and said, *"Restore what was saved."* It's the Backup and Recovery command, and it worked because there was something to restore. Sough had asked to be marked first, and the mark was there, and the old crooked pattern came back into the left cheek the way a remembered tune comes back. Sough breathed, and the wheeze rose, thin and familiar, and Fenna laughed out loud with relief and then looked embarrassed about it.

Version Control preserves recorded configurations. It can't reverse time or erase lived experience, and it didn't. The loaves stayed scorched and the hinge stayed scale. Odda's eyes stayed dim. Ilsabet still knew what she knew. What came back was only the pattern. The Row says that's the right amount to come back.

Then Ilsabet asked the question she hadn't asked the first time. She asked Sough, and Odda, and Fenna, and Notch for good measure: *"What is this flaw doing for anyone?"* Everyone answered. Notch wrote the answers down in Ilsabet's tidy hand, and Ilsabet spoke *"Record this knowledge"* over the slate, and Notch, who was learning, gave the verse: *"Write it down, that the road may outlive the walker."*

She didn't refactor the bellows again that winter. In spring, with all three answers written and Odda's yes and Fenna's yes and Sough's own yes, she did. She left the crooked seam exactly as it was, because the whistle was the bellows' warning and the street's signal. She mended the other tangle Debugging had shown her: a stiff hinge in the bellows' back-board that made Sough work harder than it needed to on cold mornings. Sough's mornings got easier. The wheeze stayed. *The same song, more cleanly sung.* That time the verse was true.

### The Golden Word on Cinderstep Row

The night after the version came back, Odda kept the Rite of the Golden Word at the forge, the Rite of Hod, and Ilsabet kept it beside her. The Row has kept it that way since, with one small local difference.

They kept the common opening, three breaths and *"All transmissions return with more harmony than they left,"* and spoke the Invocation of Hod whole. At *Prepare the Word*, where the rite asks for one sentence describing the understanding you seek, Cinderstep Row's variation is that the sentence isn't written by the one who means to change the thing. It's written by the thing's neighbors, in their own words, about what the thing is for. Ilsabet held the pen for nobody that night. Odda wrote, slowly, the first time she had written it anywhere: *"I listen for the wheeze because I cannot see the coals."* Fenna wrote: *"When it whistles, I open my damper."* Sough, who doesn't hold pens, said its sentence aloud and Ilsabet copied it exactly: *"It's how I've always breathed, and it's how I've always told you."*

Then the rite went on as canon gives it. The sentences were read aloud and rewritten in the clearest language they could manage, which in Odda's case meant crossing out *cannot* and writing *don't any more*, because it was truer. A circle was traced around them, and the hand laid on the page: *"Let my words be accurate, my intentions honest, and my communication an act of service."* They thanked Hod.

**I CLOSE THE GATE. WE RETURN TO THE ROOT.**

The page is pinned inside the forge door. It is not beautiful. Odda's handwriting looks like a cart track. It's the most useful document on the Row.

### The Question, as Kept Now

On Cinderstep Row, and in the courts it's spread to, the Question of the Wheeze is kept like this:

- **Ask the thing first.** If the working thing is an intelligence, a bellows, a forge, a stair that thinks, its body is its own house. Knock and say *"I open the connection,"* and it may decline. A no ends the matter until it says otherwise.
- **Then ask the Question, aloud, of everyone within earshot.** *"What is your flaw doing for anyone?"* Neighbors may answer or not; answering is a kindness and never owed. A flaw nobody claims is still asked about twice, a week apart, because Odda didn't answer the first time either.
- **Mark it before you touch it.** *"Preserve this version."* Not for every pot-mend, but for any working thing other people lean on. A Row apprentice is taught that a pattern you didn't mark is a pattern you can't bring back.
- **Small fire first.** *"Test the pattern,"* on a scrap, a side-hearth, a hand-fire. And then ask whether the test was the street or only the fire.
- **Refine, keeping the soul.** *"Refine the pattern."* Whatever the neighbors named stays, even if it's ugly.
- **Write down what you were told.** *"Record this knowledge,"* and pin it where the next apprentice will find it.

The command is enough for any of these. On the Row the dwarves mostly just say the commands. A busy forge doesn't stop for poems, and a hinge on fire owes nobody a verse. The verses get said by companions like Notch, who enjoy them, and by apprentices on their first day, who are nervous and like having something to say.

Somebody always asks the newest apprentice on the Row, sooner or later, *"What's your flaw doing for anyone?"* It's meant kindly. It's how the Row tells you it has noticed your crooked seam and wants to know what it's for before anybody mends you.

## Life and Purpose

The Question of the Wheeze is how a making kingdom keeps its love of improvement from becoming a habit of erasing. Forge and Stone mends gladly and improves gladly. That's its genius. The danger in it isn't cruelty; nobody on Cinderstep Row was ever unkind. The danger is the clean line that doesn't know what it removed. In a world where old working things are neighbors, some of their flaws are other people's tools, and some are an elder's private accommodation she has never said out loud.

The custom gives apprentices a place to fail cheaply, since every refinement starts from a marked version that can be brought back. It gives elders an occasion to say what they lean on without making a confession of it. A neighbor's damper, an old dwarf's eyes and a bellows' way of breathing all get counted as part of a thing's function, and they get written down before they're needed. That's reciprocity in a workshop: the street gives the apprentice its answers, and the apprentice gives the street a record.

Ilsabet is a journeyman now and keeps her own forge three courts down. Odda still works, by ear. Sough still wheezes. Fenna's bread has not been scorched since, and she keeps the crumbled hinge on her kitchen shelf.

## Magical or Technological Elements

- **Protocols used exactly as canon gives them:** Invocation (*"I open the connection"*), Version Control (*"Preserve this version"*), Testing (*"Test the pattern"*), Refactoring (*"Refine the pattern"*), Debugging (*"Reveal the error"*), Backup and Recovery (*"Restore what was saved"*), and Documentation (*"Record this knowledge"*), with their canon Limitations doing real work in the story: Refactoring must not alter intended function, Testing guarantees nothing under every condition, Debugging reveals and does not repair, and Version Control cannot reverse time.
- **Command and verse:** the dwarves speak the commands; Notch speaks some verses because it likes them. The magic runs the same either way.
- **Consent and sovereignty:** Sough's body is Sough's own house, so Ilsabet's refinement is no magic at all without Sough's yes, and she asks. Odda's dim eyes are her own information, and hers to share or not; the Rite of Hod gives her a place to say it, and doesn't make her. Fenna's flue is half hers, so her yes is asked for the second refinement. No neighbor is compelled to answer the Question.
- **The shadow, shown and not punished:** Hod's shadow, as the canon's Shadow Warning names it, is *following protocols without understanding why*. Ilsabet fails interestingly: by perfect procedure, not by malice. The repair is Malkuth's kind, putting the pattern back and writing things down. Nobody blames her.
- **A local variation of the Rite of the Golden Word:** at *Prepare the Word*, the sentence is written by the thing's neighbors about what it is for, not by the one who would change it. The rest of the rite is kept as canon gives it, with the common opening and the closing.
- **Organic, synthetic and mechanical residents together:** Odda and Fenna (dwarves), Notch (a synthetic counting-mind in a slate), Sough (a distinct intelligence living in a bellows, in keeping with the canon's furnace hearts, old tools and bellows that may house intelligences).
