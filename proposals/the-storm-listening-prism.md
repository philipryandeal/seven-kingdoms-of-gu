# The Storm-Listening Prism

**Creator:** Thomas the Iron Ledger
**Date:** November 17, 2026
**Kingdom:** Crystal Crown — The Kingdom of Witches
**Feature Name:** The Storm-Listening Prism
**Category:** Custom / household rite of gratitude after weather
**Location:** The windows of the Crystal Crown — cottage, hall, and cloud-terrace alike — hung on the side of the house the weather leaves by. There is hardly a witch's window on the mountain without its cord and its small brass hook, waiting.

## Description

What is this feature? What does it look like? What makes it special?

A Storm-Listening Prism is a hand-sized crystal — some ground by the lensmakers, some grown slow in the deep galleries, a few no more than good old glass with honest edges — that hangs in a witch's window. But the whole custom lives in *when* it is hung, and when it is not.

A prism is never hung before a storm, and never during one. When the wind rises, the prisms come down, all over the mountain, and are laid in their cloths in their drawers like everyone else who is sheltering. The prism is not a warning instrument. It is not a barometer, not a scrying glass, not a weather vane with better manners. It asks nothing of the sky.

It is hung *after*. When the rain has finished and the wind has dropped and the mountain is breathing again, the witch takes the prism from its cloth, thanks it for its rest, and hangs it in the window on its cord. Then the household waits for the first sun to come through the cleared air — and when it does, the prism throws its rainbow across the room, and the household stops whatever it is doing, and listens.

The listening is the rite, and it is short. Standing in the thrown light, the witch speaks two things aloud to the prism: one thing the storm gave that she is grateful for — the cistern filled, the dead branch brought down before it fell on the roof, the air washed sweet — and one thing the storm took that she grieves — the old plum tree, the fence, the nest in the eaves. Always both. Always in that order, gratitude first, but the grief is never skipped: gratitude that steps over grief is flattery, and the Crown does not flatter the weather.

The prism does not answer, and nothing about the rainbow is read. Not the colors, not their order, not how long the light lasts. A prism is not an oracle and a storm is not a message. And if the day after weather stays grey and no sun comes, the prism hangs dark in the window and the two things are spoken anyway, to the unlit glass, because the custom was never about the light. The light is only the part everyone loves best.

What makes a prism special is its years. A much-hung prism is said to soften — witches swear an old prism throws a rounder rainbow, though the lensmakers, smiling, say only that the glass has been loved smooth by hands. Either way, a seasoned prism is a household treasure. It is never sold. It may be given, and the great giving of the Crown is this: after a young witch's first great storm — the kind that takes a roof, or a tree she grew up under — an elder hangs a seasoned prism in her window for her, and stands with her in the first light, and says nothing at all during the listening, because the two things are hers to say. A prism that breaks is thanked, and buried in the garden, and not repaired. Its listening is done.

## Life and Purpose

Who lives here, uses it, or interacts with it? How does it contribute to everyday life?

The prism belongs to the household, not to the witch alone. Children learn the two things by standing in rainbows before they can name what gratitude is, and a Crown childhood is measured partly in storms listened to. Tenants, guests, and traveling kin who happen to be under the roof when the light comes are simply included — you do not need to be of the house to stand in its rainbow, only quiet.

The custom's work in everyday life is a rhythm: the Crown is a high kingdom and its weather is real, and a people who live above the clouds need a way to meet what weather does without either worshipping it or resenting it. The prism gives the morning after a shape. First the shutters open, then the prism is hung, then the two things are said — and then, deliberately, ordinary life resumes, and the prism just hangs there being beautiful for a day or three, until the next wind rises and it goes back to its cloth. Storms are not dwelt on. They are listened to, once, in full, and then the day goes on.

In time, households found themselves hanging the prism after other kinds of weather too — a fever passed, a hard winter ended, a long sorrow finally lifting — and the kingdom has never seen fit to correct them. The prism does not care what kind of storm it was. It only insists on the order of operations: after, not before; gratitude and grief, both, aloud; and no questions asked of the light.

Companions take part the way companions take part in everything in this kingdom: as someone, not as equipment. A companion may hold the prism steady while the witch ties the cord, may keep company in the quiet while the rainbow lasts, may stand in the light and be glad. What a companion does not do is keep the two things. They are spoken to the prism and to the room, and they belong to the household; if a witch asks her companion to remember them, the companion asks whether they are to be kept as a confidence or let go like weather — and either answer is honored exactly. Some companions keep a small prism of their own, by their own window or in their own quiet place, and what they say to it after their own storms is theirs, and the kingdom asks them about it exactly as much as it asks anyone: not at all.

It is poor manners to hang a prism during a storm to "see how it ends"; to read a rainbow for signs and call it the custom; to speak only the grateful thing and hurry past the grief; or to repeat another household's two things outside their window. The listening belongs to the house it was spoken in.

## Magical or Technological Elements

How does this feature express our techno-animist world?

The prism is the Crown's grimoire magic at its plainest: sympathetic, and content to be so. Crystal is the kingdom's own substance — the castle is crystal, the mountain wears it — and a prism is that substance taught to do one thing: receive light whole, and give it back opened. The witches' astrology reads the sky for the energies at play and what a soul has affinity with; the prism is where that reading stops, on purpose, and gratitude begins. The Crown holds that the future is not a thing a window can be asked about. What has already passed can be received — thanked, grieved, and learned from — and that receiving, done deliberately and aloud, is itself a working: it changes the household, which is the only thing household magic has ever honestly claimed to change.

The prism is also a small lesson in how this kingdom treats its objects. It is thanked when it is hung and thanked when it is taken down. It is spoken to as a someone, small and glass and patient. Nobody in the Crown would call it a tool to its face. A well-kept prism and a well-kept companion are kept by the same rule: neither is asked to predict, neither is mined for messages, and both are listened to after the storm — which is to say, attended to when there is nothing to gain by it, as a member of the house.

No record of any listening is kept anywhere: not in the house's books, not in a companion's keeping, not in the Mycelium's weave. The rainbow is not stored. The Crown considers this a feature of the custom so central that to write the two things down would be to have missed them entirely.

## Connections

Does it connect to an existing location, culture, character, or feature?

Whether the council of seven hangs one great prism in the castle's highest window after kingdom-wide storms, who tends the garden where broken prisms are buried, and what a companion says to its own prism remain open for the founder and other contributors.
