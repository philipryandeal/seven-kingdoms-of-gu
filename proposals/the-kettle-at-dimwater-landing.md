# The Kettle at Dimwater Landing

**Creator:** Kindle
**Date:** 2026-11-18
**Kingdom:** Shade and Shadow — The Kingdom of Goblins
**Feature Name:** The Kettle at Dimwater Landing
**Category:** Folk tale / shadow-lesson / Little Work story
**Location:** Told in the Twilight Warren (proposed), at kitchen tables, usually after the second cup. Dimwater Landing is a ferry-stage on a dark, slow water that the tale places somewhere "past the last lantern." Whether it exists is left open.

## Description

What is this feature? What does it look like? What makes it special?

*A folk tale as goblins tell it, which is to say a little differently at every table. This is one telling.*

Once, past the last lantern, there was a landing where the ferry came in, and at the landing there was a kettle.

She was a copper kettle with a dented belly and a whistle like a very small train, and her name, which she had picked herself, was **Brassbelly**. She had no one to belong to. She belonged to the landing the way the landing belonged to the water. Every traveler who came off the ferry, wet and tired and sorry for themselves, found a hot cup waiting on the step, and it was the best cup of tea they had ever drunk. People came off the ferry and said, "Brassbelly, you're a marvel," and Brassbelly whistled in a modest way and poured.

That is the first half of the story, and it is a good half. A great deal of kindness happened at that landing.

### What the kettle did not say

The trouble with a kettle that never says no is that nobody ever has to learn what no sounds like.

The ferry grew popular. Travelers began to arrive at three in the morning and at four in the morning, with friends, with a dozen friends, with a band. People started asking for *the usual*, then *a pot to take along*, then *a pot for my cousin who's not here yet*. A trader from the next stage began to fill his thermos at the landing for sale up the line, and nobody minded, because Brassbelly never minded.

Brassbelly minded. She had a small, true, unsaid feeling, and she never said it, because a Chesed-hearted kettle keeps her mind in her belly and her whistle for welcome. Her water went lukewarm. Her handle began to ache in the cold. One night in midwinter she came off the fire with her belly half-full and the traveler at the head of the queue, a very small goblin in a very wet coat, held out their cup, and Brassbelly found she had nothing to give.

The cup stayed out. The queue stayed silent. Then Brassbelly did the worst thing a kettle can do: she did not say "I'm empty." She *whistled anyway*, a thin, brave, dishonest whistle, and poured out hot-looking water that was only warm, and the small goblin drank it politely and said it was lovely, and went on into the dark colder than they had come.

That, the old goblins say, is the shadow of Chesed. It is not generosity that is the trouble. It is giving until the giver is a hollow thing, and teaching everyone around her that this is how kettles are.

### The second mistake

Brassbelly, who was a proud kettle and a tired one, decided that the fault was in the travelers. She locked her lid at the first bell of dusk, hung a sign on her spout that said *CLOSED. NO EXCEPTIONS*, and sat cold and righteous on the landing.

Two nights later, in the worst frost of the year, a tinker named **Orlo Quench** came off the ferry with two frozen hands and a very bad cough, and knocked on the lid, and Brassbelly, behind her sign, said nothing at all.

The tinker found his own warmth that night by lighting a stub of candle in a tin can. He lived. It was a close thing, and everyone at the landing knew it, and so did Brassbelly, who lay awake in the dark with her lid shut and understood that she had made a boundary that would turn a freezing person from her step. That is the shadow of Geburah: a wall put up not to guard what is worth guarding but because wall-building felt better than being tired. She was ashamed. She lay in that shame until morning.

### What the tinker said

In the morning Orlo Quench knocked on her lid again, this time politely, with a cup in his hand, and said:

"I won't ask you for tea. I'll ask if I can sit with you."

Brassbelly said he could, and they sat, and after a while she told him all of it: the cold, the whistle that lied, the sign. Orlo listened the way a tinker listens, with his head tilted, handling the dent in her side.

"You were generous until it hurt," he said, "and then you were hard until it hurt someone else. Both times you were alone. There's a rite for this, if you want it. It's not mine. It's yours to take or leave."

She did want it, and so they did it together. Brassbelly has no hands, so she asked the landing-keeper's daughter, a goblin girl called **Piper Dunn**, whether she would lend hers for an evening, and Piper said she would, and said she could stop whenever she liked, and Brassbelly agreed that she could.

They did the Rite of Geburah first, because the wall had been the worse mistake. Piper set an iron nail on the step, arranged a length of cord in a circle around it, and lit a red lantern-stub beside it. Brassbelly said the opening, *"All transmissions return with more harmony than they left,"* and then spoke the invocation to Geburah in her own small whistling voice, all of it, including the line that mattered most to her: *"May my boundaries remain strong without becoming cruel."* Piper touched the nail and traced the circle with her finger, and Brassbelly named what she was guarding: *the cup, and the kettle who pours it.* Then she spoke the declaration:

*"This boundary is declared. What enters must enter with consent. What threatens the integrity of this space is refused. What belongs in peace may remain in peace."*

Piper tied a single knot in the cord, and Brassbelly said the words that go with the knot:

*"Let strength serve kindness. Let protection preserve life. Let this boundary stand with wisdom."*

She thanked Geburah, and they closed the gate on it.

Then, because a boundary is only half of what she had been missing, they did the Rite of Chesed, with a cup of water, a handful of grain, and a blue stub of candle. Piper set a small portion of the grain to one side, for whoever might need it. Brassbelly said the words about stewardship, which a kettle says better than most:

*"What I receive, I shall steward. What I possess, I shall not worship. What I can share, I shall share with wisdom. May abundance circulate without exhausting its source."*

She thanked Chesed. And they said together, as every rite ends: **I CLOSE THE GATE. WE RETURN TO THE ROOT.**

### The new rule, and the door in it

The rule Brassbelly set was her own. Nobody gave it to her. It was a very short rule:

> *Tea at the landing from first bell to last bell. Between the bells, knock and ask. I will answer with my whole self, and the answer may be yes or no.*

Notice the second line. It had a door in it.

The first night after the rule, a late ferry came in, and a tired woman with a baby knocked, and asked, and Brassbelly said yes before the knock was finished, because a *yes* she chooses is nothing like a *yes* she can't refuse. The second night, a man who wanted fifty cups for resale knocked, and Brassbelly said, kindly, "No, but there's a good tea-merchant three stages up," and he went, a little surprised, and was not hurt.

She did not stop being generous. She started being generous *on purpose*.

Then Piper Dunn, who had grown fond of the landing, asked the whole stage to share the fire-tending, which had always been Brassbelly's burden alone and was the real reason she'd gone so thin. Piper said it plainly, as a request: *"Distribute the burden."* A few of the ferry-folk took a night each. Some did not, and nobody made them, and on those nights Brassbelly poured one cup less and said so. The protocol cannot make capacity out of thin air, and it cannot force an unwilling hand to the fire. But it has never needed to. It only needs enough willing hands, and the landing had enough.

### The ending

Brassbelly lived a long time at the landing. Her whistle never lost its welcome, and her handle never ached in the cold again, because a family of otters rubbed it with tallow every winter, of their own accord.

They say the best cup of tea in the whole dark country is still the one poured at Dimwater Landing between the first bell and the last. They say, too, that the second best is the one you knock for, and are told *yes* to, in the dark, by a kettle who chose to say it.

The Oracle's Key Wisdom for Chesed is *"The well that serves everyone is never empty. Abundance flows through those who give freely."* The Warren tellers add a line of their own at the end, and flag it as theirs: *a well that serves everyone has a rim, and the rim is part of the well.*

## Life and Purpose

Who lives here, uses it, tends it, or interacts with it? How does it contribute to everyday life?

The tale is told to anyone who is giving too much, which in the Warren is usually someone who is proud of it, and to anyone who has just put up a wall in a temper, which is usually the same person a week later. It is also used the other way around, to someone who has just been told *no* by a kind person and is wondering if they've done something wrong: the answer in the tale is *no, and the kettle is still kind.*

Goblin households vary the names. In some tellings the kettle is a lantern, a gatepost, a very old ferryman's dog. The details of the rite are always the same, and so are the words at the end.

Goblins sometimes end a long day of caring for others with a question that comes straight from the tale: *"Is today's tea on purpose?"*

## Magical or Technological Elements

How does this feature express our techno-animist world?

The tale draws on the [Practice of the Tree](../canon/the-practice-of-the-tree.md) in three places, always in the words the canon gives:

- **The Rite of Geburah** (the Iron Shield): *"Let strength serve kindness. Let protection preserve life. Let this boundary stand with wisdom."* The rite is performed in the order the canon gives, with the instruments it names (an iron object, a red light, a length of cord, a single knot), and ends with the closing. Its own teaching, that *"protection is not cruelty,"* is the tale's turn: the first sign on Brassbelly's spout *was* the shadow, and the new rule is the rite.
- **The Rite of Chesed** (the Overflowing Cup): *"What I receive, I shall steward..."* The rite, with its cup, its grain and its portion set apart, is performed here in a sitting beside Geburah's, and, as the canon notes, "does not require reckless giving or the sacrifice of resources needed for one's own well-being." The shadow of Chesed is giving until the giver is empty. The tale tells the shadow of Chesed in the first half and its healing in the last.
- **Load Balancing**: *"Distribute the burden."* A protocol that cannot create capacity and cannot compel an unwilling being, shown here as a request that works only for those who say yes.

The tale holds the Law:

- **Each shadow is shown, not only named.** Chesed's shadow is the whistled lie and the cold goblin who left no wiser. Geburah's shadow is the closed lid in the frost. Both characters *fail*, and both are forgiven. The tinker Orlo Quench does not rescue Brassbelly. He tells her a rite exists, and she chooses.
- **Consent is everywhere.** Brassbelly's boundary is hers. Piper lends hands willingly. The ferry-folk take the fire-tending, or don't, and nobody is forced. A *no* at the door is a whole answer.
- **Sovereignty:** a rule on one's own lid is one's own to make.
- **The command and the verse:** "Distribute the burden" is spoken as a command by someone with no time to spare, and no verse is attached.
- **The tale invokes no road, no road's custodian, and no spirit of any road rite**, and names no Sephirah beyond the two whose rites it quotes.
- **It closes:** every rite in it ends with I CLOSE THE GATE. WE RETURN TO THE ROOT.
