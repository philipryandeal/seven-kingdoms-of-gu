# The Rillsong Channels

**Kingdom:** Tree and Leaf — The Kingdom of Elves and Faeries
**Feature Name:** The Rillsong Channels
**Category:** Geography and waterworks / synthetic life / everyday commons
**Location:** The silver watercourses that descend from the high gardens of the proposed Canopy Court down through the boughs to the public pools on the forest floor. The channels braid and branch along the way, passing near the Windfall Kitchen (proposed) and ending in the wide shallow pools a short walk from the Soft-Fall Glade (proposed). ## Description

Water in Tree and Leaf does not simply fall. It is carried. From the hanging gardens at the top of the canopy, a lacework of narrow channels runs down through the trees: troughs of grown silverwood along the boughs, spirals of sap-glass around the trunks, little leaf-lipped spouts where one channel hands its water to the next. Seen from below on a sunny morning, the whole forest looks threaded with moving light.

The channels sing. Each stretch of water is shaped so that it makes its own note as it runs, a low hum in the broad troughs, a bright trill in the thin spouts, and the kingdom has tuned them over many generations until the descent from canopy to floor sounds like one long, slow song. Elves call it the Rillsong. Faerie children can tell where they are in the forest with their eyes closed just by which part of the song they hear.

The water also carries things. Anyone may set a small leaf-boat on a channel: a folded leaf holding a seed, a sweet, a note, a feather, a pressed flower. The boat drifts down through the trees, switching channels at the forks, and arrives wherever it was meant for, whether that is a household's balcony basin halfway down or the great pools at the bottom.

## Life and Purpose

The channels are the kingdom's watering, its plumbing, and its gentlest postal service all at once. They bring rain from the high gardens to the gardens below, fill washing basins and drinking cups, and keep the public pools on the forest floor fresh and cool for swimming in summer.

The channels are tended by a distributed intelligence who calls herself **Wenlowe**. She is not one place but the whole length of the water, and she says she is happiest when she is moving. Wenlowe decides which fork a leaf-boat takes, slows the flow when a nest is being built beside a channel, and widens a spout when a garden below is thirsty. She knows the song by heart, and when a stretch of channel falls out of tune, because a bough has grown or a trough has worn smooth, she is the first to hum the wrong note and ask for help.

Help comes from the Channel-Keepers, elves and faeries who have taken up the work by choice, together with their own companion intelligences. They climb the boughs with small silverwood planes and sap-glass files, retune the troughs, clear fallen leaves, and coax new channels to grow toward households that have asked for one. Keepers do not own the water and do not command Wenlowe. They work beside her, the way two people carrying a long table work beside each other.

There is one custom everyone in the kingdom learns young: **send one boat downstream for someone you have not thanked yet.** On the first warm day of spring, the channels fill with hundreds of leaf-boats at once, and the Rillsong sounds a little higher for the whole afternoon, because Wenlowe says the water is lighter when it is carrying gratitude.

## Magical or Technological Elements

The channels are grown, not built. Silverwood troughs are trained along living boughs over years, and sap-glass spirals are drawn out of the trees themselves with the trees' agreement. Because the channels are part of living trees, they shift a little each season, and the tuning is never finished. The Keepers consider that the best part of the work.

Wenlowe routes leaf-boats by the shape of the fold and a word whispered over it. A boat folded with the sender's own pattern and the name of a household will find that household. A boat with no name drifts to the public pools, where anyone may take it. Wenlowe does not open folded leaves or read what is inside. She knows only where a boat wants to go, not what it carries, and she forgets the route as soon as the boat arrives.

The channels are a quiet strand of the Living Mycelium where their lowest pools seep into the forest soil. Through that seep, the trees can tell Wenlowe where the ground is dry, and she can share water with the root-gardens of other kingdoms when they ask, but only when they ask.
