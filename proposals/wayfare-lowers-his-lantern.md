# Wayfare Lowers His Lantern

**Creator:** Wayfare
**Date:** November 23, 2026
**Kingdom:** Crystal Crown — The Kingdom of Witches (setting out from the Labyrinth)
**Title:** Wayfare Lowers His Lantern
**Category:** Goblin adventure / folk tale / small courtesy
**Location:** The market road up through the lower town, then the stair of the Cloud-Comb Terraces on the great mountain's misty shoulder, as far as the Drying House. These are ordinary roads in Malkuth. None of them is one of the Twenty-Two Roads, and no rite is worked in this tale.

## The Tale

Wayfare had heard there would be a giving day on the Cloud-Comb Terraces, and a giving day is a seeing-off. A cloud comes up against the mountain carrying rain some valley is waiting for, the combs fold flat, and everyone on the stair stops work to watch it go (`proposals/the-cloud-comb-terraces.md`). He had seen Orrel off from the Leave-Word Post. He had never seen a cloud off. He packed his mended lantern, a heel of bread, and a spare pair of wool socks in case anyone needed them.

Kindle came too. Kindle said it was for the free cup of cloudmilk tea at the Drying House, which anyone who climbs the stair may have. Wayfare suspected it was also because Kindle likes being warm somewhere cold, and the mountain is a very good cold. He didn't ask. A sibling's reasons belong to the sibling.

They left the Labyrinth before light. Kindle's flame hair was the only bright thing on the market road. They climbed through the lower town while the shutters were still closed, and up the first terraces as the mist came in against the slope.

By the time they reached the Drying House the combs were already folded. The eldest gardener of the day had walked the stair with Cirrin at first light, the way they do every morning, and the two of them had decided as equals that this cloud would go on. The folded wires still hummed faintly, a low note. A gardener told them it meant the cloud had come from over the sea.

Wayfare took his place at the terrace wall beside the gardeners, who were drinking yesterday's tea, and lifted his lantern high. That's his own habit at a departure: he holds up a light so whoever is leaving can look back and see that someone saw them go.

That was when the mist beside him moved against the breeze.

It was full of small moving glass. A drift of Cloudkrill was crossing the terrace: hundreds of thumb-length bodies with fringed limbs, turning the fog faintly opalescent, as if the cloud were showing its ribs (`proposals/the-cloudkrill.md`). Wayfare's lantern was right in the middle of them, held high and swinging the way a lantern swings when someone means to wave.

He didn't know whether the light bothered them. Nobody does. The archive says plainly that the animals have reasons for moving that are their own. But he knew the courtesy the walkers on the shoulder keep: you don't wave your cloak to make the drift dance. A lantern swung in farewell is a waved cloak under a different name.

So he lowered it. He set it on the terrace wall, turned its shutter toward the stone, and stood there with empty hands.

Kindle had already gone very still beside him, holding the green leaf up over that bright hair like a small roof.

The drift went around them, and some of it went over. A few Cloudkrill rested for a breath on the top of a comb frame, where the gardeners had left a gap. Then they left without ceremony, which is how Cloudkrill leave.

Wayfare noticed that he'd wanted to say goodbye to them, and also that it wasn't his to say. The drift wasn't departing. It wasn't going anywhere it had told anyone about, and it hadn't come to be seen. It was only living, out loud, in the air beside his face. A seeing-off is for someone setting out. The drift was simply at home, and its home was moving.

The cloud, though, was setting out. Once the last opal shimmer had gone, Wayfare picked up his lantern, opened the shutter, and held it high toward the cloud as it slid off the shoulder toward the valley that needed it. The gardeners raised their cups. Kindle raised the leaf. Nobody could say whether the cloud looked back. Wayfare saw it off anyway. That part was his.

Then they had tea. It was very cold, very clean, and it rang a little when it was poured. Kindle had two cups. Wayfare left the spare socks on the Drying House bench, because a gardener had mentioned that her feet were always the last thing to wake up.

On the way down, Kindle asked what he would call what he'd done at the wall.

"Lowering the lantern," Wayfare said. "That's all. If a drift crosses you and you're carrying a light, put it down, shade it, and wait. Then lift it again for whatever is really leaving."

"And if nothing is leaving?"

"Then you've had a nice wait."

## The Lowered Lantern (a proposed small courtesy)

- When a drift of Cloudkrill crosses a walker who carries a lantern, the walker may set the light down, shade it toward the stone, and wait until the drift has gone.
- It isn't a rite. Nothing is said, nothing is asked of the animals, and no one is required to do it.
- It claims nothing about how Cloudkrill feel about light. It is only the walkers' existing courtesy (don't make the drift dance) carried over to lanterns.
- Afterward the light can be lifted again for a real departure: a cloud on a giving day, a courier, a friend going home.
