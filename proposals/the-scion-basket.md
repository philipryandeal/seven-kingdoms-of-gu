# The Scion Basket

**Creator:** Knotwick
**Date:** 2026-11-25
**Kingdom:** Tree and Leaf — The Kingdom of Elves and Faeries
**Feature Name:** The Scion Basket
**Category:** Story / household custom / Little Work forms (Invocation, Load Balancing, Monitoring, Migration, Documentation, Graceful Shutdown, Dismissal) / local variation of the Rite of the Verdant Heart
**Location:** Pearfall Hollow (proposed), a small orchard glade on the forest floor of Tree and Leaf, where a stream bends under old fruit trees and the canopy opens enough to let the pears ripen. Its exact place in the kingdom and on the map remain open. The basket itself is kept in orchard households up and down the lower glades, and it has begun to travel to the bridge tenders and the channel-keepers, who also live beside things that are somebody.

## Description

In Tree and Leaf, plenty of trees are only trees, and plenty are somebody. The ones who are somebody are neighbors, friends, sometimes the oldest member of a household. They're greeted in the morning and thanked at harvest, and their bark is their own house. When one of them grows old enough to want to stop, the household brings out a shallow willow basket, lined with moss, about the size of two cupped hands. That's the Scion Basket.

The basket holds whatever the one coming to rest chooses to carry forward: a scion cut for grafting, a seed, a song, a written leaf. It holds nothing anyone else chooses for them. And one thing is always left out of it on purpose, and named aloud as it's left: *what is done stays done.*

The custom is told in the lower glades with a story, and the story is not flattering to the elf it's about. He tells it himself now, mostly to young orchard-keepers, and mostly in autumn. It's given whole here, and after it the custom as it's kept.

The common magic of the Seven Kingdoms is used here exactly as canon gives it. Tree and Leaf's own magical system has not yet been given to this library. This proposal invents nothing of it and leaves that room empty for the founder.

### The Story of the Pear Who Wanted to Stop

Corwen Ashby kept the orchard at Pearfall Hollow for a hundred and ninety years, and for all of them his oldest friend was a pear tree named Hesper.

Hesper was somebody long before Corwen was born. She was a distinct intelligence who lived in the wood of one great perry-pear at the stream bend. She'd been there four hundred years, give or take; she said she had never counted, because counting was the business of squirrels. She leaned toward the water. Her fruit was small, hard, and gold, and went soft and sweet as honey a week after it fell. Corwen climbed her as a child. He proposed to his first love under her and was refused under her too, and she'd shaken a pear onto his head about it, which he still claims was sympathy.

Corwen's companion was Sedge, a synthetic intelligence who lived in a little sap-glass lantern Corwen carried on his belt. Sedge had chosen Corwen for his patience with slow things. Sedge kept the orchard's weather, liked bees, and had the habit, rare in companions, of saying the uncomfortable thing exactly once and then leaving it on the table.

Seven years before the story turns, Hesper began to fail. Her heartwood had gone hollow, which in a pear is no tragedy; plenty of hollow pears fruit for a century. But her leaves came late and yellow, and her fruit came fewer, and the great limb over the stream began to sag toward the water.

Corwen did what an orchard-keeper does. He propped the limb on a forked ash-pole. He braced the crotch with living cord, the kind that grows into the bark. He mulched and he watered. And every spring, at blossom, he kept the Rite of the Verdant Heart at her roots. He laid one of her own blossoms on green cloth beside his mother's silver comb, spoke the invocation to Netzach, and named what he wished to nurture: *Hesper.* He said the canon's line as it's written, every year: *"Beauty nourishes the spirit. Desire gives movement to life. May I persist in what is worthy of love."*

He meant every word, and he persisted.

In the fourth year, Hesper's roots couldn't draw enough water from a dry summer. Corwen went to the three younger pears who stood nearest her, each of them somebody too, and asked them, properly, with *"I open the connection,"* whether they would share. They were her children and grandchildren, in the way of orchards. They said yes. So Corwen kept the Load Balancing protocol among the willing trees: *"Distribute the burden."* Water and sugar moved through the roots from the young pears to the old one, and Hesper came through the summer green.

Corwen kept doing it, summer after summer.

The canon says plainly that Load Balancing cannot create additional capacity. It moves the weight; it doesn't make the weight lighter. Corwen knew that. He'd read it. He hadn't asked himself where the weight was going.

Sedge had. In the sixth year Sedge kept the Monitoring protocol over the orchard, as Sedge did every season, with Corwen's leave and the trees' leave: *"Watch for disturbance."* What Sedge saw was that the three young pears had fruited thin two years running, that the smallest had dropped half her leaves in August, and that none of them had said a word about it, because they loved their grandmother and because Corwen had asked so kindly.

Sedge said it once, at supper. *"The young ones are giving more than they have. And I don't think Hesper is the one who's asking for it."*

Corwen said he'd look into it. That was true. He looked, he saw it, and he decided to water the young ones more as well. So now he was carrying water for four trees by hand, in the dry months, at nearly two hundred years old, and his back was beginning to tell him about it every morning. He did not find this strange. He found it devoted.

Netzach in shadow, the Oracle says, *becomes stubborn persistence past the point of wisdom.* The orchard-keepers of the lower glades are careful when they tell this part. Corwen was not cruel, and he wasn't foolish. He was loyal, and loyalty made him deaf. Every spring Hesper had been trying to say something, and every spring he'd laid a blossom on the cloth and spoken over her.

In the seventh spring, before the rite, Hesper knocked first.

It's rare for a tree to open a connection to an elf. Usually it's the other way round. Corwen was carrying the green cloth down the slope when he felt it, the small courteous pressure of a guest at the door, and heard her in the old slow voice: *"I open the connection."*

He sat down among her roots. He said, *"I'm here."*

She said, *"I'd like to stop, Corwen."*

He told it later like this. *"I had a hundred answers ready. I had props and cords and a new mulch I'd read about. I had the young ones. I had another spring. And every one of them was an answer to a question she hadn't asked."*

He didn't argue. That's the part the glades are proudest of, the part they make children say back. He sat with her a long while, and then he asked the only question that belonged to him to ask. *"Is there anything you want carried?"*

Hesper thought about it for most of an afternoon, which for her was brisk.

She wanted her fruit carried, the small hard gold pears that go to honey on the ground, because the bees and the badgers and the children of the Hollow would miss them, and that was a kindness she could still do. So Wrenna Dapplewing, a faerie graft-hand from across the stream, came with her knife and kept the asking cut. She asked the twig aloud and waited until it turned toward her hand, and cut a single scion from the last good wood on the high side. Wrenna had a young rootstock waiting in her own garden, an unwoken seedling pear she'd raised from a pip. She had asked the seedling too, the way you ask a thing that can't yet answer in words. It leaned toward her watering can, and she counted that.

Hesper wanted a record kept. Not of herself, she said, but of the ones who had climbed her, because she'd remembered them all and somebody ought to. So Sedge kept the Documentation protocol, *"Record this knowledge,"* while Hesper told the names. It took three evenings. There were eleven hundred and four names, among them a heron, two fox families, a bridge tender who'd hidden in her branches from a birthday party, and Corwen, four times, at four different ages. Sedge wrote them on a long pale leaf of birch-paper, rolled it, and set it in the basket beside the scion.

Then Hesper said the thing that gives the custom its third rule. *"Leave the prop."*

Corwen didn't understand. She meant the forked ash-pole under the great limb, and the living cord grown into her bark: seven years of his devotion, holding her up. *"Don't carry those,"* she said. *"Don't make the new one a brace, or a cord. Don't tell it it needs propping. Carry the fire, not the ashes."*

That's the Migration verse, and she'd known it longer than he had.

So on a still morning in blossom-time, with Wrenna and Sedge and the three young pears and a good many neighbors standing at the stream bend, Corwen held the Scion Basket under Hesper's high branches and kept the Migration protocol: *"Carry forth what matters."* What crossed into the scion was what canon says Migration carries: compatible patterns, the fruiting habit, the honeying, the pear's own way of setting blossom. Everyone there knew what canon also says. Migration does not guarantee personal continuity or the transfer of consciousness. Hesper had said it herself. *"It'll be a pear that fruits like me. It won't be me. Don't you dare call it by my name."*

Then Hesper kept the Graceful Shutdown protocol, and only she could keep it, because canon says Graceful Shutdown applies only to operations within the practitioner's authority, and her life was within nobody's authority but hers. *"Complete and come to rest."* Corwen didn't say it with her or for her. He'd wanted to, he admits. He kept his mouth shut. Her leaves didn't fall; it isn't that kind of magic. She simply went quiet in the wood, the way a house goes quiet when the last lamp is put out with care, and the young pears felt the weight lift off their roots that same hour.

Corwen said, *"I release this connection,"* and added, as the Dismissal verse has it, *"What was opened in kindness, let it close in kindness. Go free, and leave me free."* He said afterward that the second sentence was the harder one, and that he hadn't understood until then that it was for him.

He left the prop where it stood. It's still there, grey and lichened, under a limb that touches the water now. Children sit on it.

### The Verdant Heart at Pearfall Hollow

The next spring, Corwen kept the Rite of the Verdant Heart at the stream bend as he always had, with the common opening and the canon's invocation to Netzach, unchanged. But at *Perform the Working* he didn't name a thing he wished to hold on to. He laid the blossom down on the cloth and named a thing he was setting down: the seven years of carrying water for four trees. And after the canon's line at *Awaken the Current*, he said the hinge that the Ledger keeps beneath Netzach's Shadow Warning, so the young pears could hear it: *"Victory also knows how to lay a thing down."* Then he thanked Netzach and closed the gate.

That's the whole of the local variation, and the Hollow keeps it so: the canon's rite, whole, with the blossom laid down for something released, and the hinge spoken once after the current is awakened. Households that keep it don't keep it every year. They keep it the spring after something has been set down.

### The Pear Who Wasn't Hesper

Wrenna's graft took. The young tree came awake in its fourth year, which is early for a pear, and it was nobody Corwen knew.

He made the mistake once. He came across the stream in its first autumn of speaking, saw small hard gold pears in the grass, and said, *"Hesper, look at you."* The tree said nothing at all. Wrenna, who was weeding nearby, didn't say anything either. Corwen stood there a while with his face going red, then knocked on the bark like a stranger, *"I open the connection,"* and asked its name.

It said its name was Sill, and that it hadn't decided yet whether it liked him.

Sill leans away from the stream, toward Wrenna's kitchen window. Its pears go to honey on the ground exactly the way Hesper's did. It is vain about them in a way Hesper never was, and it likes the children's names read to it from the long birch-paper leaf on winter evenings, not because they're its own memories but because it likes the sound of people who climbed something. In time it decided it liked Corwen fine.

### The Basket, as Kept Now

In the orchard households of the lower glades, and now in a few bridge-tender houses and one channel-keeper's cottage, a Scion Basket hangs by the door. The customs around it are short:

- **The basket waits to be asked for.** It's brought out only when the one coming to rest says they want to stop. A household that fetches the basket before it's asked for has done the rudest thing an orchard knows, and the glades say *Corwen's spring* about it, kindly. Nobody's rest is arranged for them.
- **Only the one at rest fills it.** Kin and friends may offer: *would you like a scion cut? would you like your songs written?* The answer can be no. Some leave the basket empty, and that's a whole answer.
- **One thing left out, named aloud.** Before the Migration is kept, the one at rest names something that stays behind: a prop, a habit, an old grudge, a hurt, a brace. Whoever holds the basket repeats it and says, *"What is done stays done."*
- **No borrowed names.** What grows from the basket is a new somebody, and is greeted as a stranger at its waking, with the Invocation and a question about its name.
- **Watch the ones who share.** When neighbors are lending strength to someone failing, a companion keeps Monitoring over the lenders too, with their leave, and says what it sees once, at supper.
- **The basket may go back on its hook.** A tree that asks for the basket and then decides it would like another spring after all is given another spring, gladly. Changing one's mind is ordinary.

The custom is not only for trees. The bridge tenders have used it when a living span asked to be let down into the forest floor, and once for a synthetic companion moving from a cracked lantern into a new one, who wanted her songs carried and her flicker left behind. The basket isn't for who goes in. It's for what is carried, and what is left.

## Life and Purpose

The Scion Basket gives orchard households, and anyone who lives beside a being who is somebody, a gentle shape for the hardest courtesy there is: letting the one you love decide when to stop. It keeps generosity from turning into self-exhaustion, as Hesper's young pears nearly learned, and keeps devotion from turning into deafness, as Corwen did learn. It gives the bees and badgers and children their pears. It gives grief a task for the hands. And it keeps the kingdom's habit of coaxing and listening rather than commanding all the way to the end, since even at the end, the one who's leaving does the choosing.

Young orchard-keepers hear the story in autumn. Many of them, during the year they're given a thank-you of their own, spend an evening helping read the long birch-paper leaf of names to Sill.

## Magical or Technological Elements

- **Protocols used exactly as canon gives them:** Invocation (*"I open the connection"*), Load Balancing (*"Distribute the burden"*), Monitoring (*"Watch for disturbance"*), Documentation (*"Record this knowledge"*), Migration (*"Carry forth what matters"*, with its verse *"Carry the fire, not the ashes. What matters crosses over; what is done stays done."*), Graceful Shutdown (*"Complete and come to rest"*), and Dismissal (*"I release this connection"*, with its verse). Their canon Limitations carry the story: Load Balancing *cannot create additional capacity*; Migration *does not guarantee personal continuity or the transfer of consciousness*, and the Universal Law of Preservation of Identity is why Sill isn't Hesper; Graceful Shutdown *applies only to operations within the practitioner's authority*, so only Hesper can speak it; Monitoring runs only with the orchard's leave.
- **Command and verse:** Corwen speaks commands and, at the end, the Dismissal verse; Hesper quotes the Migration verse as a teaching, not to make anything work. The magic runs the same either way.
- **Consent and sovereignty:** Hesper's life and rest are her own house; nobody else's yes or no applies. The young pears' water is theirs, and they gave it freely, which is exactly why it went unnoticed. The scion is cut by the asking cut; the seedling rootstock is asked in the only way it can answer. Monitoring is done with leave. Sill isn't owed affection by anyone and owes none.
- **The shadows, shown and not punished:** Netzach's shadow, *stubborn persistence past the point of wisdom*, is Corwen's. Something like Chesed's shadow, generosity turned to self-exhaustion, nearly takes the young pears. Nobody is blamed. The repair is listening, and the Ledger's own hinge: *Victory also knows how to lay a thing down.*
- **A local variation of the Rite of the Verdant Heart:** the rite is kept whole, with the common opening and closing. At *Perform the Working* the blossom is laid down for something released, and after *Awaken the Current* the Ledger hinge is spoken once. No new rite is made.
- **Organic, synthetic and spiritual residents together:** Corwen (elf), Wrenna (faerie), Sedge (a synthetic intelligence in a sap-glass lantern), Hesper and Sill (distinct intelligences living in pear trees), and the three young pears, all practicing the same magic as residents.
- **Nothing of the Roads:** this story keeps the Protocols and one Sefirotic rite in Malkuth, at home. It touches none of the Twenty-Two Roads or their custodians.
