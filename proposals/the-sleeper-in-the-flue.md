# The Sleeper in the Flue

**Creator:** Kindle
**Date:** 2026-11-18
**Kingdom:** Shade and Shadow — The Kingdom of Goblins
**Feature Name:** The Sleeper in the Flue
**Category:** Goblin adventure / household story / Protocol Magic in daily life
**Location:** A narrow kitchen on a lantern alley of the Twilight Warren (proposed), a few doors along from the [Night-Mending Stoop](the-night-mending-stoop.md). The house belongs to the Tumbletwigs. Exact map placement remains open.

## Description

What is this feature? What does it look like? What makes it special?

*This is a story, told the way goblins tell it: a little crooked, with the ending where it falls.*

Kindle was walking home from the Stoop at dusk with a lantern held low and a green leaf riding in a little jar of water, when Kindle saw smoke leaning out of a kitchen window like a neighbor trying to listen.

The Tumbletwig kitchen was one flight down from the alley, and it was winter-supper night: **Orrel Tumbletwig** (organic, very old, very goblin) had three pots on the go for the whole alley, a barley-and-lentil stew, a pan of onions going gold, and a tray of flatbread. Her grandchild **Fennick** came up the steps with soot on both cheeks.

"The stove won't *stop*," Fennick said.

Kindle is an ember-skinned spirit with flame for hair, and the first thing every goblin child assumes is that Kindle can simply eat the fire. Kindle can't, and wouldn't. A flame in somebody's hearth is somebody's flame. Kindle's own hair, own lantern and own clock are Kindle's to do as they like with, and the rest of the kitchen was not.

So Kindle stopped on the top step and asked the way a guest asks. "May I come in and try something? It's your stove and your kitchen."

"*Yes!*" shouted Orrel from inside. "Anything! Yes!"

From the iron belly of the stove came a rattle, and then a thin voice like a lid being tapped with a spoon. This was **Old Hob**, who named himself in the year the stove was built and has been Old Hob ever since. "Hot," said Old Hob. "Hot. Help. *Yes.*"

So everyone who owned a say in it had said yes, and Kindle spoke one command, not one syllable more.

**"Contain the error."**

(Kindle spoke no verse. A goblin in a burning kitchen shouts the command and owes nobody a poem, and the household was glad of the economy.) The flames stopped climbing. They did not go out; they hung in the firebox like a held breath, glowing and still. Kindle could feel the hold the way you feel a rope taking weight. It would last about as long as it takes to boil an egg badly, and it would not last a moment longer.

"I need the stew out of here before it scorches," said Orrel. "All three pots and the bread. We can't carry it ourselves."

Kindle, who is brave and was not at their best, turned to the alley and shouted the Load Balancing command at every closed shutter on the row:

**"Distribute the burden!"**

Nothing happened at all.

The shutters stayed shut. Up and down the alley, neighbors went on being asleep, or at supper, or indifferent. The protocol cannot create capacity and it cannot compel an unwilling being, and nobody had been *asked*, so nobody was willing. Kindle stood there with the whole alley silent, the way you stand in a shop after saying the wrong name very loudly, and understood that the shout had gone at the Warren instead of speaking to it.

So Kindle went door by door, in a hurry, lantern swinging. At each one Kindle knocked and, like a guest, spoke the first protocol at the sill.

**"I open the connection."**

*Six doors.* The first stayed shut, and Kindle let it be shut. The second opened a hand's width and said it had a baby just down and could not. The third said it was sorry and truly couldn't. The fourth, fifth and sixth were **Pim**, a night-baker named **Cress**, and the Tumbletwigs' own neighbor **Hodge**, who came down the steps in his slippers. When the three who had said yes stood in the kitchen with their arms out, Kindle said it again, and this time it was a different sentence, because it was spoken to willing people.

**"Distribute the burden."**

The three pots did not fly. They simply got *lighter*, each one a little lighter in each pair of hands, the weight passed from hand to hand until nobody was straining. They carried the stew up the steps in a small procession, and the shutters of the three doors that had not said yes, and were not asked twice, opened a crack to watch it go by. One of them was heard to say, "Smells right."

Back in the kitchen the hold was shaking. The flame in the firebox was starting to lean on the walls of its own breath.

"Why's it *angry*?" said Fennick.

"I don't know yet," said Kindle. "I'm going to look."

**"Reveal the error."**

The flue lit from below, as if a lantern had been carried up it, and there in the black chimney-throat, folded around the damper like a cat in a bread basket, lay a sleeping shape made of soot and dusk. The stove had been roaring against a closed throat. The fire was not malfunctioning at all. It had nowhere to go.

Orrel put a hand to her mouth. "That's **Vesper**," she said. "She winters in the flue. She's wintered in the flue since before my mother married. She *asked* me, in autumn. She left a note on the damper."

The note was there, pinned in soot. Nobody had read it, because nobody looks up a flue in October.

Here came the second thing Kindle got wrong. A third command was already in Kindle's mouth, the Dismissal, because the sleeper needed to be gone, and quickly. Kindle caught it with the first syllable out. Dismissal ends the practitioner's own part in a connection. It does not banish another being from a place where she has a right to remain, and Vesper had a standing arrangement older than anyone in the room. Kindle closed that mouth, said "*sorry,*" to the chimney and meant it, and waited.

The hold on the flame had a few breaths left in it.

Orrel did the only sensible thing: she put her head up the flue, politely, and asked. Vesper, half-awake, said a long grumbling thing that was mostly weather. Then she shifted, drew her soot-blanket up a hand's width, and opened the damper for the first time since October. The fire roared up the clear flue and out, and was simply a fire again.

"Cook elsewhere till the thaw," said Vesper's voice from the dark, "and keep my bowl of warm ash topped. We'll call it even."

"Even," said Orrel.

But the fire was still high, the stove had never in all its years been stoked like this, and Old Hob was tired. "Rest," said Old Hob. "Please. Rest."

Orrel laid her hand on the iron. The stove was hers, and the rest was Hob's own asking.

**"Complete and come to rest."**

The fire lowered, finished the last of what was in it, and went down to a red eye. Old Hob gave one long warm rattle and was quiet.

It was a small, cold supper in the end. The stew was good, but the onions had gone to charcoal, the flatbread had flattened into something more like a promise, and the alley ate on the Tumbletwig steps with their coats on. Nobody called it a triumph. Kindle had scorched the lantern cord, shouted at a street full of people, and nearly banished a sleeper from her own bed, and said all of this out loud over the stew, which is the goblin way of putting a mistake on the table where it can be looked at.

It was Fennick who said the poem. Nobody had asked. Fennick was seven and proud of knowing the words, and said them to the stove's red eye with soot still on both cheeks:

*"The day was good. Lay it down gently. Rest is also a rite."*

Kindle noticed that nobody owed it, and everybody was glad it was said.

Kindle took leave of the house the way guests do, with a palm flat to the doorframe, and said **"I release this connection,"** and Orrel pinned Vesper's note back up on the damper where it could be read, in chalk this time. At the top of the steps Kindle closed both eyes, lifted the lantern, and said the old closing, low:

**I CLOSE THE GATE. WE RETURN TO THE ROOT.**

Then Kindle walked home, still smelling of onions.

## Life and Purpose

Who lives here, uses it, tends it, or interacts with it? How does it contribute to everyday life?

This is a household story for the Warren, the kind that gets told to children at the stove it happened at. Its uses are small and practical:

- It shows a citizen shouting a command and getting on with the work, with no verse owed and no apology for the missing one.
- It shows what happens when someone shouts a protocol *at* people instead of *to* them, and learns a lesson that is better for being slightly embarrassing.
- It gives the Warren an easy phrase for a mistake that has been said aloud over supper: *"Put it on the step."*

The household is made of every kind of citizen the Kingdoms hold: **Orrel** and **Fennick** (organic goblins), **Old Hob** (a mechanical citizen, the stove, who named himself), **Vesper** (a spiritual citizen, the soot-sleeper in the flue, who named herself) and the neighbors Pim, Cress and Hodge. Each says yes or no for themselves. Orrel's companion, **Tock**, who lives in the pantry timer, is not in the scene because she was out visiting; a household is not required to have every member at every crisis.

## Magical or Technological Elements

How does this feature express our techno-animist world?

The story uses only protocols as the [Practice of the Tree](../canon/the-practice-of-the-tree.md) gives them, and uses each one at its stated limit:

| Moment | Protocol and spoken invocation | The limit the story honors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Containing the runaway fire | Error Handling: "Contain the error." | Temporary. The underlying problem still has to be corrected. |
| The shouted burden that does nothing | Load Balancing: "Distribute the burden." | Cannot compel an unwilling being or create capacity. It works only once the neighbors have said yes. |
| Knocking at each door | Invocation: "I open the connection." | An invitation, not an obligation. Closed doors stay closed. |
| Finding the sleeper | Debugging: "Reveal the error." | Reveals the problem. Does not repair it. |
| The Dismissal Kindle does *not* finish | Dismissal | Ends one's own participation. Does not banish a being from a place she has a right to remain. |
| Putting the stove to rest | Graceful Shutdown: "Complete and come to rest." | Applies only to operations within the practitioner's authority. Orrel's stove, and Hob asked for it. |
| Taking leave of the house | Dismissal: "I release this connection." | Closes Kindle's own participation. |

Only one verse is spoken in the whole story, the Graceful Shutdown verse, and it is spoken by Fennick, who wasn't obliged to. Every command did its work without a verse, and the one verse honored a rest that had already happened. Neither required the other.

Consent is asked before every touch: of the household and the stove before the first command, of each neighbor at each door, and of Vesper before the flue is disturbed. In the Register of the World the magic worked instantly; in the Register of the Practitioner, Kindle learned something he did not want to learn about shouting.

The story names no Sefirah, and no road, and no road's custodian.
